Power, Story, and Love

The Prince

Machiavelli recommends breaking promises when keeping them threatens a ruler's position. He also asks why some cruelties secure a reign while others bring it down.

7 stops · 14 min read

scroll

Cesare Borgia gave a governor full powers to restore order in the Romagna, then had him killed and displayed in a town square. The execution shifted blame for the cruelties onto the man who had carried them out. Machiavelli recommends imitating Borgia's methods; he says the people were satisfied and stunned.

The Prince asks how someone acquires a state and keeps it. A prince here is a sole ruler, whether he inherited his position or acquired it. New rulers owe rewards to people who helped them, while those they displaced want revenge. Even their troops may belong to someone else. A reputation for goodness won't settle those conflicts.

The early chapters examine those foundations of rule, including the danger of relying on hired soldiers. The later chapters ask how a prince should treat his subjects and allies. These seven stops follow that argument, with a return to Borgia. The passages use W. K. Marriott's 1908 translation; the panels compare some of his choices with the Italian.

Stop 1 / Conduct and survival

Learning to do wrong

Marriott / Chapter 15 / excerpt

Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity.

A ruler who keeps his word can be defeated by someone who breaks theirs. Machiavelli calls his approach the verità effettuale, the truth of what happens in practice. He asks what a choice does to a ruler's position, including when a praised virtue helps bring him down.

Earlier advice to rulers, often called mirrors for princes, commonly linked good character with successful government, though the books varied widely. Machiavelli objects to treating praised conduct as a sufficient guide to staying in power. His prince must be able to act against it when survival requires it.

He still calls the familiar good qualities praiseworthy and tells a ruler to avoid vices where he can. But retaining the state can override those restraints. The prince decides what necessity demands, including when to harm his subjects.

imparare a potere essere non buonoItalian / Chapter 15 / excerpt

  • to know how to do wrong,Marriott / excerpt
  • to learn to be able not to be good,Mansfield / p. 61 / excerpt

Mansfield preserves the emphasis on learning an ability; Marriott makes the wrongdoing explicit. Both instruct the prince to be capable of acting against goodness.

Stop 2 / Paying for generosity

Generosity and its bill

Marriott / Chapter 16 / excerpt

Therefore it is wiser to have a reputation for meanness which brings reproach without hatred, than to be compelled through seeking a reputation for liberality to incur a name for rapacity which begets reproach with hatred.

A prince who wants to be known as generous spends his reserves on displays and favors, then raises taxes to keep paying for them. A few people receive the gifts; many more pay the bill. In Machiavelli's account, the effort leaves the ruler poor and his subjects angry.

Thrift has the opposite effect. Keeping expenses within ordinary revenues lets a ruler defend himself without extraordinary taxes. He disappoints the people waiting for gifts, but leaves the much larger number of taxpayers less burdened. Marriott's “meanness” means stinginess here, and “rapacity” means taking greedily from others.

Generosity can still help someone gain power. A commander can also distribute plunder taken from outsiders to keep soldiers following him. Machiavelli's concern for the people paying for largesse has a boundary: the protection he recommends for a prince's subjects doesn't extend to those his army loots.

Stop 3 / Punishment and loyalty

Fear without hatred

Marriott / Chapter 17 / excerpts

It may be answered that one should wish to be both, but, because it is difficult to unite them in one person, it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with.

… men have less scruple in offending one who is beloved than one who is feared, for love is preserved by the link of obligation which, owing to the baseness of men, is broken at every opportunity for their advantage; but fear preserves you by a dread of punishment which never fails.

For Machiavelli, love depends on other people's willingness to remain loyal. Punishment is something the prince can administer, so he prefers fear when he can't have both.

The prince must also avoid being hated. Machiavelli tells him to leave his subjects' property and “their women” alone, treating sexual violation partly as an injury to men's households and honor. An execution needs a visible reason and justification. He warns that confiscation produces enemies, then makes the much broader claim that people forget a father's death sooner than the loss of their inheritance.

In chapter 9, the people's basic wish is to avoid oppression, while the powerful want to dominate them. A prince needs the people on his side when trouble comes. In chapter 19, their goodwill makes conspiracies harder: an assassin who expects public hostility has less reason to try. Keeping that support limits how the prince can use fear.

The condition in “safer to be feared”

Both texts retain the condition: fear is safer when the prince can't have both. The next paragraph requires him to avoid hatred.

Italian / Chapter 17 / excerpt
è molto più sicuro essere temuto che amato, quando si abbia a mancare dell’uno de’ dua.
W. K. Marriott / excerpt1908
it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with.

Stop 4 / Borgia and Agathocles

Cruelty, used well

Marriott / Chapter 7 / excerpt

Under this pretence he took Ramiro, and one morning caused him to be executed and left on the piazza at Cesena with the block and a bloody knife at his side. The barbarity of this spectacle caused the people to be at once satisfied and dismayed.

Borgia's governor, Remirro de Orco, is called Ramiro in Marriott's version. Machiavelli says he restored order through severity. Borgia replaced his sweeping authority with a civil court, then arranged his death to blame the cruelty on him. The Italian adds that his body lay in two pieces. Marriott omits that detail, but preserves the killing and its purpose.

Machiavelli admires Borgia's attempt to establish authority that could survive the loss of his powerful father, Pope Alexander VI. But Borgia loses his state after his father's death. The chapter first emphasizes bad fortune, then faults Borgia for helping Julius II, an enemy, become pope. The ruler whose measures Machiavelli recommends ultimately fails.

Chapter 8 asks why some rulers retain power after terrible crimes. Its phrase is “cruelties well used,” qualified by “if it is permissible to speak well of evil” (my translation). Cruelty should be concentrated at the start, necessary to secure the ruler, then stopped and followed by benefits to the subjects. Continuing violence renews their injuries and prevents trust.

Marriott / Chapter 8 / excerpt

Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory.

Agathocles, in Machiavelli's account, seized Syracuse by having senators and wealthy citizens killed. Machiavelli recognizes his military ability while denying him the honor due to great rulers. Marriott's “talent” translates virtù, also used for Agathocles' courage nearby. Here, gaining power and deserving glory remain different achievements.

What “well used” requires
Italian / Chapter 8 / excerpt
e di poi non vi si insiste drento ma si convertiscono in più utilità de’ sudditi che si può.
W. K. Marriott / excerpt1908
and that are not persisted in afterwards unless they can be turned to the advantage of the subjects.

Marriott's “unless” can sound like permission to continue cruelty if it benefits the subjects. The Italian says it is no longer persisted in, but turned toward as much benefit for them as possible. The next paragraph describes gaining their loyalty after the injuries stop.

Stop 5 / Breaking a promise

The lion and the fox

Marriott / Chapter 18 / excerpt

A prince, therefore, being compelled knowingly to adopt the beast, ought to choose the fox and the lion; because the lion cannot defend himself against snares and the fox cannot defend himself against wolves. Therefore, it is necessary to be a fox to discover the snares and a lion to terrify the wolves.

A prince can use law or force. Because law is sometimes insufficient, Machiavelli says he also needs the capacities of animals. He reads Achilles' education by Chiron, a centaur with a human upper body and a horse's body, as teaching this combination.

The fox supplies the cunning that strength alone lacks. A prudent ruler should break faith when keeping it harms him and the reasons for his promise have gone. Machiavelli argues that others won't keep their word to the prince, so he needn't keep his to them. Plausible excuses, he adds, will always be available.

Deception requires a reputation for honesty. Most people see the prince from a distance; those who know his conduct may fear contradicting the public view. He should appear faithful and humane, and act that way where possible, while staying ready to do the opposite. The appearance helps him break a promise without immediately losing people's trust.

The animals in the Italian
Italian / Chapter 18 / sentence
Bisogna, adunque, essere golpe a conoscere e’ lacci, e lione a sbigottire e’ lupi.
W. K. Marriott / excerpt1908
it is necessary to be a fox to discover the snares and a lion to terrify the wolves.

Golpe means fox, and lione means lion. Other editions use volpe. Marriott preserves the division of work: the fox spots traps, the lion frightens wolves.

Stop 6 / Judging the result

What success excuses

Marriott / Chapter 18 / excerpt

For that reason, let a prince have the credit of conquering and holding his state, the means will always be considered honest, and he will be praised by everybody; because the vulgar are always taken by what a thing seems to be and by what comes of it;

“The ends justify the means” isn't a sentence in The Prince. This passage predicts that people will praise a successful ruler's methods. The chapter uses that prediction to support its advice: a ruler who deceives can protect himself through success and a convincing appearance. Machiavelli is recommending the conduct whose reception he describes.

The Italian also mentions the absence of a court to which one can appeal against a prince's actions. Marriott's “which it is not prudent to challenge” shifts the emphasis toward the danger of protesting. In both versions, the ruler's power makes opposition difficult.

In the Discourses, I.9, Machiavelli excuses Romulus' killing of Remus because he founded institutions for the common good. He distinguishes this from violence for personal ambition and warns against passing unchecked power to an heir. The outcome excuses an act he recognizes as wrong; the goal is a political community with good institutions.

The words behind the slogan
Italian / Chapter 18 / excerpt
Facci dunque uno principe di vincere e mantenere lo stato: e’ mezzi saranno sempre iudicati onorevoli, e da ciascuno laudati;
W. K. Marriott / excerpt1908
let a prince have the credit of conquering and holding his state, the means will always be considered honest, and he will be praised by everybody;

The prince is told to win and retain the state; his means will then be judged honorable. The slogan blurs the difference between getting praise and deserving it, which chapter 8 preserves for Agathocles.

Stop 7 / Preparing for change

Fortune and fixed habits

Marriott / Chapter 25 / excerpt

I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less.

Fortuna is the chance and changing circumstances a ruler doesn't control. Machiavelli compares it to a flooding river: embankments built in calm weather limit later damage. Earlier chapters recommend similar preparation, such as establishing forces of one's own.

Machiavelli argues that a cautious ruler can prosper when caution suits the times, then fail when circumstances require speed. People rarely change their habits, he says, especially after success. His example is Pope Julius II, whose bold action suited his circumstances. Had the times required caution, he says, Julius would have failed.

The chapter nevertheless ends by preferring impetuous action. Fortune is a woman, he says, whom a man must beat and force into submission; she favors the aggressive young. Domination of a woman becomes the model for overcoming events. That general preference for aggression sits awkwardly with Julius' example, where its usefulness depends on circumstances. The chapter leaves the tension unresolved.

The violence in the fortune image
Italian / Chapter 25 / excerpt
la fortuna è donna, et è necessario, volendola tenere sotto, batterla et urtarla.
W. K. Marriott / excerpt1908
fortune is a woman, and if you wish to keep her under it is necessary to beat and ill-use her;
Harvey C. Mansfield / p. 101 / excerpt1998
to beat her and strike her down.

Marriott's “beat and ill-use” and Mansfield's “beat her and strike her down” both describe an assault. The older wording already preserves the violence of the image.

A republican advising the Medici

Machiavelli served Florence's republic from 1498 until the Medici returned to power in 1512. He lost his office and was imprisoned and tortured in 1513 under suspicion of conspiracy. Later that year, writing from his country property, he told Francesco Vettori he had composed a short work on principalities and wanted the Medici to employ him. Calling the book a job application captures one plausible purpose; it doesn't establish everything he meant to do with it. Biography; letter of 10 December 1513.

His republican commitments are evident in the Discourses on Livy. In I.58, he argues that a people governed by laws can be wiser and more constant than a prince. Advising a new ruler and defending a republic can coexist in a writer who thinks founding a political order may require one person's exceptional authority.

Rousseau later read the book as teaching the people what princes do. The stronger proposal that the whole work is satire, put forward by Garrett Mattingly, remains contested. Machiavelli recommends Borgia's measures and ends by urging the Medici to free Italy from foreign domination. His letter seeking employment supports taking the practical advice seriously. A republican reader can learn from it without assuming that each instruction means its opposite.

Machiavelli later received work from the Medici, including a commission to write the Florentine Histories in 1520. He died in 1527; The Prince was first printed in 1532. Later life and publication.

The instructions leave a problem for the people being ruled. Machiavelli makes a prince's survival grounds for harming them, but gives them no way to test whether the harm was necessary. A ruler who can deceive them about his conduct can also deceive them about that necessity.
