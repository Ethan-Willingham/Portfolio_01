Marcus Aurelius's Meditations

Marcus Aurelius warns himself against becoming a Caesar. His reminders connect the use of power to justice, and acceptance of death to his belief in a providential world.

28 selected passages / 5 translators, 1634 to 2002 / 3 min introduction

Scroll

An emperor warns himself not to become a Caesar. In Meditations 6.30, Marcus Aurelius says imperial power can change a person, then tells himself to remain just and willing to help. He remembers his adoptive father Antoninus Pius listening patiently to criticism.

The book keeps returning to a demanding claim: only goodness makes a life good. Wealth, health, and reputation are worth caring for, but possessing them does not make someone just; losing them does not make someone wicked. In 2.11, Marcus puts death and pain in the same category. He calls these things neither good nor evil because he reserves those words for moral character.

When he says a judgment causes distress, he means that an event arrives with an assessment: this is unbearable, this disgraces me. He asks whether the assessment is true and whether it interferes with acting well. The force of 4.7, where removing a judgment removes the harm, depends on his definition of harm. A person can injure his body or block his plans without forcing him to become unjust. Treating every injury as imaginary would lose that distinction.

Marcus also thinks human beings are made for one another. The morning exercise in 2.1 lists people who will annoy him, then compares them to hands or teeth that must cooperate. Getting out of bed is justified by work for the community. 5.20, on adapting when someone obstructs him, begins with the obligation to help and bear with others.

The reason for accepting events is partly religious. In 7.9, one divine reason pervades the universe and is shared by rational beings. Providence means that the whole has an order worth trusting, even when an individual suffers. Marcus also considers a world made by atoms, as in 4.3. Scholars disagree about what those alternatives reveal about his convictions. His repeated appeals to the gods make it difficult to read the book as a wholly secular account of emotional control.

His fellowship with humanity does not amount to a modern account of equality. In 1.17, he thanks the gods for an obedient wife and good household servants. Book 1 also credits the private education his family could afford. These are a ruler's reflections. They do not tell us how his subjects experienced his rule, and his warnings against misusing power cannot establish how consistently he followed them.

Marcus ruled from 161 to 180 CE and wrote this work in Greek. Its direct addresses to himself and repeated exercises support the usual scholarly view that he was writing for his own moral improvement. We cannot establish every detail of his intentions or the order in which he composed it. Two place headings name the country of the Quadi and Carnuntum, linking some of the writing to his northern campaigns. They do not locate every entry in an army camp. The surviving text and its form leave those limits in place.
