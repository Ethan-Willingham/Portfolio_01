The Kama Sutra, in plain English

The Kama Sutra gives instructions for household budgets, adultery, and earning a living as a courtesan. Sexual positions occupy one chapter. Its wider subject is how people pursue erotic pleasure, including the money, status, and obligations that shape their choices. The book's own contents, 1.1.16-23

Its opening places kama, desire or pleasure, alongside dharma, religious and moral duties, and artha, material success. Pleasure generally comes after duty and livelihood when they conflict, but it still deserves study. The book briefly mentions moksha, release from rebirth, in old age; its working framework is the three worldly aims. 1.2.1-15

The main setting is a household with money and leisure. The cultivated man learns music and painting, entertains friends, and employs messengers to arrange relationships. Wives manage household affairs; courtesans assess lovers, earnings, and risks. Women sometimes speak and act for themselves, but their choices sit inside hierarchies of gender, class, and marriage. Book 5 advises men on pursuing other men's wives. Book 7 offers erotic remedies and spells.

Vatsyayana argues for a woman's pleasure and warns against forcing a bride before she trusts her husband. The same courtship chapter permits threats and unwanted touching. His chapter on striking condemns dangerous blows while describing other forms in detail.

Vatsyayana draws on earlier teachers. Doniger and Kakar place its composition probably in northern India in the third century AD; the date and the author's life remain uncertain. Sources and limits

The explanations are paraphrases; the English blocks are excerpts, with omissions marked [...]. References give book, chapter, and sutra. For the unnumbered 1883 English, these identify corresponding passages. Sanskrit excerpts may be shorter than the English selections.

  • Sanskrit. Jean Fezas's GRETIL e-text, in IAST, the standard Roman-letter transliteration. Hyphens, apostrophes, and the file's colons marking negative prefixes are normalized; list numbering is omitted and punctuation regularized. References follow the Oxford edition. GRETIL labels the final verses 7.1.52-59 within its second chapter; Oxford numbers them 7.2.52-59. These are excerpts from an e-text, not a manuscript study.
  • The 1883 edition. Project Gutenberg's transcription. It is conventionally called Burton's translation, but Bhagavanlal Indrajit and Shivaram Parashuram Bhide prepared the translation, Forster Fitzgerald Arbuthnot helped prepare and finance it, and Richard Burton edited and helped publish it (Oxford introduction, pp. liv-lv). Its title page names the Kama Shastra Society and specifies private circulation.
  • Doniger and Kakar. Oxford World's Classics paperback, 2003, copyright 2002. Excerpts were checked against this copy; its introduction, pp. l-lviii, informs the translation discussion. Unlike the 1883 edition, it distinguishes the root text from Yashodhara's later commentary. It remains an interpretation of a difficult text.
