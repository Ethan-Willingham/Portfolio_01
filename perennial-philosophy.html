What Huxley thinks the mystics know

Aldous Huxley's The Perennial Philosophy treats the Hindu Self and Buddhist awakening as knowledge of the same divine reality. His first chapter puts them beside Christian and Sufi accounts of union with God. Discovering an eternal Self, ceasing to identify anything as self, and loving God don't obviously describe the same achievement.

Published in 1945, the book is an anthology with Huxley's commentary. He arranges religious writings by topic, from the nature of reality to charity and action. Their common source, he argues, is the divine Ground: the reality on which everything depends.

Agostino Steuco published De perenni philosophia in 1540. Huxley's opening credits Leibniz with the phrase. Steuco's title predates him.

The claim

Huxley's four claims

Huxley gave this argument a four-part form in his 1944 introduction to the Bhagavad Gita. These are paraphrases of his claims:

  1. Everything depends on a divine Ground. The physical world and individual minds are its manifestations. The Ground is present in them and exceeds them.
  2. Direct knowledge of it is possible. A person can come to know this reality through an intuition that unites the knower with what is known.
  3. The everyday ego doesn't exhaust who you are. Huxley posits an eternal spiritual Self of the same or similar nature as the Ground. A person can learn to identify with it.
  4. That knowledge is the purpose of life. Detachment and love prepare a person for the union he considers our final end.

Accepting the value of compassion doesn't commit you to an eternal Self. Unusual states of consciousness don't establish that they disclose the source of the universe.

In his opening chapter, Huxley favors Advaita Vedanta, the nondual reading in which Atman, the Self, is one with Brahman, ultimate reality. He reads other traditions through that possibility, though his definition allows likeness to the Ground as well as identity with it.

How to know

Practice changes the observer

Knowledge is a function of being.The Perennial Philosophy, Introduction

Huxley's example is an astronomer whose instruments reveal things unavailable to the naked eye. He proposes a comparable change through moral and contemplative practice: becoming less possessive and more loving would make knowledge of the Ground possible. Reading the doctrine wouldn't perform this work for you.

A musician hears distinctions a beginner misses. An experienced contemplative might also notice things the rest of us overlook. Lack of practice is a poor reason to dismiss their report.

The analogy leaves a verification problem. Astronomers can compare instruments, examine the same object, and check a prediction. Divine union supplies no equally straightforward way to separate accurate perception from a compelling experience that has been misunderstood. Training can improve attention while teaching someone what to expect.

Huxley selects writers he regards as qualified by their holiness. Their conduct can support their credibility. It becomes circular if agreement with him helps decide who qualifies. A comparison would need to include accomplished practitioners whose reports resist his reading.

The practice

Detachment has an ethical test

In chapter 6, Huxley warns that self-denial can leave a person proud, angry, and cruel. Giving up pleasures can become another way to feel superior. Extreme bodily austerities can damage health and encourage competition.

His positive standard, charity, means love that doesn't depend on getting something back. A demanding routine or an unusual experience tells us less than whether the person becomes more considerate when there is nothing to gain. The book links contemplation to conduct, including the work by which someone earns a living.

Someone who listens to criticism without immediately preparing a defense might notice a mistake and repair it. Reduced self-concern can help them, whether or not their deepest nature is divine. That benefit doesn't require treating ordinary identity or self-protection as a moral failure.

Refusing a demand or defending yourself can be warranted. Greed and pride explain some harm. Coercion, ignorance, and conflicting obligations can't all be reduced to one oversized ego.

The comparison

Five passages in context

Hindu text

What the salt is meant to show

“It is the Self, and thou, O Svetaketu, art it.”

In the Chandogya Upanishad, Uddalaka teaches his son Svetaketu about the underlying reality of living things. In 6.13, the boy dissolves salt in water. He can no longer retrieve it, but water tasted at the surface, middle, and bottom is salty. Something can be present throughout without being visible.

The repeated teaching is tat tvam asi, often rendered “that art thou.” Huxley takes it as the identity of Self and ultimate reality. The earlier discussion of sleep and the origin of things makes the scope clear: the father is teaching what a person is and what sustains the world.

Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7 and 6.13.1 to 3. F. Max Müller, The Upanishads, Part I (1879), pp. 101 and 104 to 105. Quotation: 6.13.3.

Early Buddhist discourse

Consciousness is included in what to release

“This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.”

The Characteristic of Not-Self examines five groups of experience: bodily form, feeling, perception, choices, and consciousness. Each changes, can bring distress, and cannot simply be ordered to behave as we wish. The Buddha asks whether it makes sense to identify these things as a lasting self.

The instruction above applies to consciousness as well as the body. Understanding this leads to disillusionment, the fading of desire, and liberation. The discourse doesn't replace the ordinary ego with a divine Self behind it.

Releasing attachment to what you call yours resembles Huxley's practical advice. Discovering a divine Self would be an additional claim. The discourse also doesn't say that people cease to exist in ordinary life.

Samyutta Nikaya 22.59, Bhikkhu Sujato. Quotation: paragraphs 7 and 10; argument: paragraphs 2 to 6; liberation: paragraphs 11 to 12.

Mahayana Buddhist text

The text anticipates Huxley's comparison

“my Tathāgata-garbha is not the same as the ego taught by the philosophers”

Huxley quotes the Lankavatara's language of a pure inner nature. Tathāgata-garbha, often translated as Buddha-nature or Buddha-womb, can sound like a concealed eternal Self. The sutra itself raises that problem.

Mahamati asks whether an enduring, pure nature within beings is the same as the philosophers' eternal self. The Buddha answers no. The teaching is explained through emptiness and the absence of a separate self. Its reassuring language helps people who fear the teaching of egolessness; it should not lead them to cling to a soul.

The sutra explicitly rejects the identification its language might suggest.

Lankavatara Sutra, chapter 2, section XXVIII, D. T. Suzuki (1932), pp. 78 to 79. The quotation is a fragment of the Buddha's first reply.

Christian mystic

Union changes the person's love and will

“it is transformed in God through love.”

In The Ascent of Mount Carmel, John distinguishes two kinds of union. God continually sustains every creature's existence; without that dependence, it would cease to be. The union a practitioner seeks is a further transformation through love and grace.

He describes the person's will coming into conformity with God's will. His image is a window filled with sunlight. The window appears to be light, yet its nature remains distinct from the light that illuminates it.

Huxley acknowledges this distinction in his Gita introduction: Christian union through grace needn't mean identity of substance. He still proposes one knowledge across both accounts. The comparison must explain why identity with ultimate reality and participation in God's life count as the same knowledge.

The Ascent of Mount Carmel, book 2, chapter 5, E. Allison Peers translation, linked 1962 edition. Quotation: paragraph 3. Window comparison: paragraphs 6 to 7.

Sufi poem

The lover's dependence on the Beloved

“The Beloved is All, and the lover (is merely) a veil; the Beloved is Living, and the lover (is merely) a corpse.”

Huxley's excerpt can sound like a claim that God is the only reality. In the opening of the Masnavi, the line also belongs to a poem about longing, greed, and the transformation love brings. The following couplet imagines a lover deprived of Love's care as a bird without wings.

The surrounding passage invokes Moses at Sinai. Rumi expresses this relationship through Islamic scripture, with the lover dependent on the source of life.

The language allows a reading of self-effacement in God. This couplet alone can't establish the same experience or account of reality as the Upanishad.

Masnavi, book 1, lines 19 to 34, Ibrahim Gamard (2000). Quotation: line 30. Moses: line 26; wingless bird: line 31.

What would count

Agreement has more than one explanation

The strongest case for Huxley is cumulative. One practitioner's report might be idiosyncratic. If different traditions repeatedly produced closely matching experiences and changes in conduct, the agreement would need explaining. Huxley takes it as evidence that the practitioners know one reality. A critic could accept the agreement and attribute it to capacities of the human mind.

Do they describe the same felt change? Give it the same meaning? Encounter the same reality? These are separate questions. A recurring state of consciousness would be interesting even if its theological interpretations were incompatible.

Shared ancestry and borrowing also need checking before the reports count as independent. Huxley's own first chapter acknowledges contact between Daoism and Mahayana Buddhism. Translators may choose the same English word for terms with different uses. Collections may preserve the passages later readers find compelling. Agreement produced by any of these routes would be weaker evidence for independent discovery.

Selection is necessary in an anthology. It becomes misleading when the selected material is made to stand for every religion, or when disagreement is assigned to less advanced people by definition. What would count as a qualified contrary report, and make Huxley revise the theory?

The dispute

Does belief shape the experience itself?

Steven Katz's 1978 essay challenges the distinction between a universal experience and the local interpretation added afterward. His proposal, often called contextualism or constructivism, is that a person's concepts, training, and expectations help shape the experience while it happens. A Christian's encounter with God and a Buddhist's awakening would not simply be different names for an identical prior event.

Calling both experiences ineffable, impossible to express adequately, doesn't identify their contents. Katz asks why only one kind of experience should be beyond language.

But the universal claim that every experience must be shaped by concepts also needs an argument. The question is whether practice can sometimes suspend the activity that ordinarily does this shaping.

W. T. Stace's Mysticism and Philosophy (1960) distinguishes an outward experience of unity amid things from an inward experience in which sensations and thoughts disappear. He argues for common features across reports while separating experience from interpretation. Those two categories matter: perceiving a landscape as one and being aware without distinct objects are not automatically the same event.

Robert Forman develops the narrower challenge through what he calls a pure consciousness event: being awake without thoughts, images, or sensory contents. In his account, reports of such states suggest that consciousness can persist when its ordinary contents fall away. If that is possible, it challenges a theory that requires every conscious event to be organized by concepts. It doesn't demonstrate Huxley's divine Ground.

Frithjof Schuon offers a different defense. In an interview explaining his position, he places religious unity in metaphysical truth beyond the mutually exclusive forms of doctrine. He treats intellectual intuition as capable of certain knowledge of that truth. Religious forms can remain different in this account. The dispute then concerns the certainty of that intuition: why should someone who doubts it accept the unity?

A shared state would still leave open whether it reveals the source of reality. Even Forman's narrower claim about consciousness needs a way to distinguish that state from alternatives.

What makes a remembered interval evidence of wakeful awareness without contents? How much was present but later forgotten? A sincere report can leave those questions unresolved. The anthology supplies no test that separates these possibilities.
