The Tao Te Ching

The best ruler leaves people thinking they did the work themselves. The Tao Te Ching keeps returning to the same difficulty: knowing when your effort has become interference.

01 Summary

The Tao Te Ching, in plain English

A ruler should receive a military victory with funeral rites. Chapter 31 of the Tao Te Ching says that celebrating victory means taking pleasure in killing. Elsewhere, the best ruler finishes the work so quietly that the people think they did it themselves. Much of this book about water and valleys is advice to someone with power.

Its recurring problem is wanting too much and trying to control too much. A ruler chasing conquest harms the country; a person chasing wealth or approval becomes dependent on keeping it. The proposed answer is wu wei, usually translated as nonaction. Sometimes that means leaving things alone. Sometimes it means acting early, doing what is needed, and stopping before help becomes interference. It asks us to give up ambition as well as unnecessary effort.

Tao, also spelled Dao, means the Way: the source of things and the course through which they develop. The other central word is de, the te in the title, translated as virtue, power, or potency. It is how the Way works in a particular being. In chapter 51, the Way gives things life and de nourishes them. The ideal is ziran, literally “self-so,” things developing of themselves. A person or ruler can support that development without owning or directing everything.

The examples are ordinary things. A pot needs its empty interior. Water benefits things and occupies low places. Living bodies are supple; dead bodies are stiff. These comparisons challenge the assumption that more force, more possessions, or a higher position must be better. The sage, the book’s model of a wise person, knows when to yield and when there is enough.

Its clearest list of what to preserve is the three treasures in chapter 67: compassion, frugality, and reluctance to put yourself ahead of the world. Compassion makes courage possible; frugality leaves something to give. Elsewhere the sage extends goodness to people who are not good and trust to people who are not trustworthy. Restraint is tied to how you treat other people.

The political criticism can be sharp. Chapter 53 describes a well-kept court beside neglected fields and empty granaries, then calls the rulers’ luxury robbery. Chapter 75 blames hunger on excessive taxes. Chapter 77 says human society takes from those with too little to serve those with too much. The book’s preference for governing less includes a demand that rulers stop enriching themselves at everyone else’s expense.

Some of its remedies are harder to accept. Chapters 3 and 65 recommend keeping people without knowledge or desire, or making them simple. You can read this as opposition to scheming, but the ruler still decides what the population should know. Chapter 80 imagines a small community whose people have transport and tools but leave them unused, growing old without visiting their neighbors. This ideal sacrifices freedom to move and explore along with the desire to conquer.

The text also speaks of spirits, ancestral sacrifice, and preserving life. It became a foundational scripture of religious Daoism. A secular reading is possible, but the neat separation between philosophy and religion leaves out some of what is actually on the page.

We know less about its author than the familiar story suggests. It is traditionally credited to Lao Tzu, or Laozi, usually understood as “Old Master.” Its authorship and date of composition remain disputed. Laozi passages survive on bamboo slips from a tomb dated around 300 BC; silk copies came from a tomb sealed in 168 BC. Those copies differ in wording and order. The 81 chapters used here follow the received arrangement, meaning the text handed down through later editions. The manuscript history gives us evidence of the book’s development, not a secure date for one author writing it all.

The versions below disagree partly because the Chinese is compressed, partly because they use different editions, and partly because some writers adapt it freely. In chapter 13, for example, some versions recommend caring for the world as for your own body; others recommend a ruler who values personal life more than dominion. Those are different instructions. My explanations choose readings where necessary and name the disputes that change the meaning. Comparing versions helps expose those choices; it cannot make them all equally faithful.
