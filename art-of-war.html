The Art of War

A commander can win a battle and leave the state too exhausted to survive the next one. Sun Tzu asks how to make an enemy submit at a cost the victor can afford.

15 passages · 5 min guide

What victory costs

Sun Tzu ranks an enemy army's submission without battle above winning a hundred battles. Chapter three then lists the targets in order of preference: the enemy's plans, its alliances, its army, and finally its walled cities. A siege takes months to prepare and can spend soldiers without taking the city.

Keeping an army in the field is costly. In chapter two, troops tire, weapons wear out, and reserves run short. Transporting provisions impoverishes the people at home; an army nearby drives prices up. Other rulers can attack the exhausted state. Victory must leave the state able to survive.

Assess both sides before committing troops. Are they trained? Will they obey? Does the ground permit the attack? Get intelligence from people who know the enemy's situation, then mislead the enemy about your own. In chapter six, an enemy unsure where the attack will come has to defend several places. Its men are divided while the attacker concentrates at one.

Adapt as the enemy changes. Like water flowing according to the ground, an army should strike exposed weakness and avoid defended strength. Some sentences promise victory with striking confidence. Others admit a limit: in chapter four, a commander can protect the army but cannot guarantee that the enemy will offer an opening.

The book accepts conquest and harsh methods of command. Chapter eleven advises putting soldiers where they must fight because they cannot escape. Chapter thirteen includes expendable spies carrying false intelligence and orders execution for a spy who reveals a secret too soon, along with its recipient. Avoiding battle can reduce destruction while still imposing one ruler's will on another.

The Chinese leaves some subjects and connections unstated. Words such as shi, the force or advantage arising from a situation, resist a single English equivalent. Even the brief statement about deception sounds different in translation:

兵者，詭道也Chapter 1 · Giles §18

  • All warfare is based on deception.Giles, 1910
  • A military operation involves deception.Cleary, 1988
  • Warfare is the art (tao) of deceit.Ames, 1993
  • The Way of War is a Way of Deception.Minford, 2002
  • Warfare is a way of deception.Mair, 2007

Giles states a basis for warfare; Ames names an art; Cleary describes an operation. The examples that follow explain the false signals. Dao means way or method here; this word alone doesn't settle the book's relation to Daoism. A larger disagreement appears below: does knowing both armies promise safety or victory?

A military book, with a disputed author

Sun Tzu, or Sunzi, means Master Sun. Tradition identifies him as Sun Wu, a general serving the ruler of Wu around 500 BCE. Whether he existed, and when the book was written, remain disputed. Columbia's source guide places compilation in the later fourth century BCE; Victor Mair argues for development from the middle fourth to early third century.

Bamboo manuscripts excavated at Yinqueshan in 1972 preserve parts of the book in a Han tomb library from the second century BCE, alongside material associated with Sun Bin. Distinct Sunzi textual traditions existed by then. They leave Sun Wu's authorship unproved, along with the tradition that Sun Bin was his descendant. Mair discusses the manuscripts and that distinction in Soldierly Methods, pages 5 and 49 to 50.

The book's advice on marching, camp sites, siege, fire, and spies is only partly represented here.

Text and translations

The Chinese selections follow the received text at the Chinese Text Project, meaning the text transmitted through later editions. Each selection has a chapter and Giles paragraph reference; other translations use different numbering. The formlessness selection joins two separated parts of chapter six.

The English entries quote portions of these editions. Bracketed ellipses mark editorial omissions, including punctuation. Captions show where an entry covers less Chinese or adds a nearby sentence. A missing version means the page has no excerpt for it.

  • Lionel Giles, 1910. Sun Tzŭ on the Art of War, Luzac. The complete translation and commentary are available at Project Gutenberg.
  • Samuel B. Griffith, 1963. The Art of War, Oxford / Clarendon Press, with an introduction and a foreword by B. H. Liddell Hart. Edition.
  • Thomas Cleary, 1988. The Art of War, Shambhala. The edition places earlier Chinese commentaries beside the translation. Edition.
  • Roger T. Ames, 1993. Sun-Tzu: The Art of Warfare, Ballantine. It incorporates the fragmentary Yinqueshan finds, including supplementary material. Edition.
  • Denma Translation Group, 2001. The Art of War, Shambhala. Kidder Smith's companion at Bowdoin explains the textual choices behind the translation.
  • John Minford, 2002. The Art of War, Viking / Penguin. It presents the translation first, then repeats it with commentary. Selections appeared in the New England Review; captions identify those excerpts by journal page.
  • Victor H. Mair, 2007. The Art of War: Sun Zi's Military Methods, Columbia. His 2008 companion study explains terms and disputed historical claims.
