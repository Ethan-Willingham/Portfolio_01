# The Number editorial review

Reviewed October 3, 2026. Edited only `archive/the-number/the-number.html` and this review. No shared files or git state changed.

## Reader payoff and reading path

The reader can turn annual retirement withdrawals into a portfolio target, then compare how income, living costs, existing investments, debt and assumed returns change the accumulation schedule. The article explains the historical withdrawal research and the account, tax, insurance and household questions that the accumulation calculator cannot answer.

The twelve existing section anchors remain. The main path now moves from the withdrawal rule and target table to the calculator, tax examples, debt, earning capacity, sources of retirement spending and omitted risks. Occupation details, four wealth stories and the Dominguez reference are optional disclosures. Both static charts and all five original images remain. The closing gives four concrete checks and ends on the tool's limit.

## Specific original problems

- The description promised that savings would out-earn spending forever. The calculator announced that crossing its line established perpetual income. The paper behind the 4% rule tested historical withdrawals over finite periods; the calculator did not model withdrawals at all.
- The prose prescribed a simple stock fund while borrowing 4.7% from research using a different allocation. It also treated a real return assumption as dependable growth and suggested that a lower rate established safety for a fifty-year retirement.
- The reference curve combined post-tax household income, an aggregate saving rate, and net worth including housing, then called the combination a median household. Its $192,700 net-worth figure also differed from the original Fed table.
- Cheap debt never received payments in the model. It could compound indefinitely even as the tool reported surplus available for investing. Nominal debt APR was compared with a real investment return. The withdrawal buttons labeled approximate multiples 29 and 31 as exact rates of 3.5% and 3.25%.
- The tax examples omitted material filing and income assumptions. Roth conversions were described as automatically tax-free deduction-sized transactions, and Marketplace help was assigned a universal dollar subsidy without a local premium or complete eligibility calculation.
- Savings were treated as a voluntary dial, with repeated claims that saving rate was the entire problem. Wages, housing, care, disability and debt constrain that choice.
- Wealth-at-death stories were made to support early-retirement probability. Home-inclusive net-worth percentiles were compared with investable portfolios. The long list of unrelated averages and success statistics added confidence without improving the reader's calculation.
- The post repeated its ending in captions, section summaries, calculator verdicts and the last section. The promise of freedom repeatedly replaced a specific mechanism.
- The phone layout overflowed its viewport. The Vega image credited Alfred Palmer and described a red headscarf and plaid shirt that are absent from the actual image.

## Calculator changes and retained interactions

Preserved all eight slider/typed input sets and their IDs, the annual/monthly spending pair, linked withdrawal target and deadline behavior, three withdrawal buttons, preset action, goal-seek suggestions, inflation comparison, dynamic SVG chart and section reveals. The reference preset remains an example; it resets spending/withdrawal and time links while preserving the user's chosen returns and withdrawal rate, matching its previous interaction behavior.

Necessary corrections:

- All surplus now repays modeled debt before new investment contributions, including low-rate debt. Existing investments remain invested. Nominal monthly debt interest is converted to real dollars using the disclosed 3% inflation assumption. Spending excludes payments for debt entered in the model to avoid counting them twice.
- Exact reciprocal multiples produce 3.5% and 3.25% targets. The displayed multiple is rounded, while the calculation is not.
- Assets exclude housing, vehicles and emergency reserves. The reference uses financial assets and explicitly identifies the mismatched source years and synthetic combination.
- A crossing reports a constant-return accumulation scenario. A target met today no longer declares retirement safe. Failure to cross within 100 years is a model limit, not "never."
- The deadline contribution search's $80,000/month bound is identified as a search limit. Inflation text identifies its assumed rate and the need to index nominal contributions.
- Linked withdrawal and deadline fields retain their computed display when blurred without an edit, rather than rendering internal null values as 0 or "null." A linked deadline with no positive crossing displays "n/a."
- Zero real returns can be selected; negative real returns can be typed. Nonfinite entries are rejected, and typed money values have finite caps. Very large projections no longer create an excessive chart-grid loop.
- Debt payoff is marked at the actual value on the net-assets curve rather than at zero. Both static chart values and captions now state their contribution and return convention.
- The page's local flex/grid wrapping and table containers prevent horizontal page overflow while keeping internal scrolling for wide tables.

## Residual limits

This is intentionally an accumulation calculator, not a historical withdrawal backtest, Monte Carlo forecast, tax optimizer or individual retirement recommendation. It assumes constant real income, spending and returns; one blended debt APR; simplified monthly repayment; and no contractual minimum-payment schedule. Its stock-and-bond allocation, retirement horizon, Social Security, pensions, account-access restrictions and withdrawal taxes must be evaluated outside the tool. Those limits now appear beside the controls and consequential outputs.

Official annual tables and dated averages can change. The current-rule and reference-data check date is visible in the hero, calculator, career note and footer. IRS Roth/HSA publications are the latest published editions available during review; special cases are summarized rather than exhaustively reproduced. Root still owns the combined editorial/browser review and integration. There is no known blocking issue in these assigned files.
