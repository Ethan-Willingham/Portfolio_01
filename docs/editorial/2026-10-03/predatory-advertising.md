## Specific problems in the original

The opening asserted industry coinage of the disparaging term and treated a journalist's fish-bait analogy as an account of neural control. The essay repeatedly assigned an involuntary reaction to every reader: disgust seized the eyes, every click disappointed, celebrity recognition meant attachment, a censor bar doubled deprivation, and knowing the technique could not interrupt it. The ten useful designs were obscured by these claims and by repeated tour narration.

The psychology cards crossed the boundary between a measured task and a commercial outcome. The 3.7-second result was described as a raw curiosity-rating notch. fMRI anticipation findings became a dopamine explanation for a particular photograph. Direct-gaze viewing time became faster initial capture and emotional contagion. The money category conflated loss aversion, fear of missing out, and a universal two-to-one effect. The AI category claimed comparative growth without comparison data.

Corporate totals mixed advertiser spending, publisher payouts, and the whole native-ad market without adequate dates or denominators. The law paragraphs turned contextual disclosure guidance into a universal label requirement and a rule against false testimonials into a blanket ban on celebrity or AI testimonials. The caption and alt text for the Korean asset described shocked faces that are not in the image. The credits classified every example as fictional, although that is only established for the first three illustrations.
