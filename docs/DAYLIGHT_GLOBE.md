# Daylight Globe

The globe combines dated NASA and EUMETSAT satellite imagery, calculated sunlight, historical city lights and NOAA's short-term aurora forecasts. Each source keeps its own clock. The time controls select actual dated images and saved forecast grids where available. The site remains static, with locally vendored Three.js r128 and no new runtime library. The opening view centres on the device location with browser permission; its controls remain visible. The rolling time slider is the only time selection control.

## Satellite clouds and their history

`js/globe-clouds.js` combines GOES East/West GeoColor, Himawari red-visible and clean-infrared maps from NASA GIBS with Meteosat IODC natural colour/infrared and MTG true colour/infrared from EUMETView. These regional feeds publish at ten- or fifteen-minute intervals. The recorder prepares a snapshot every fifteen minutes, using each product's latest advertised observation at or before that snapshot. It never selects a later observation or carries an observation beyond its native cadence. With the current aligned feeds, ten-minute products use observations zero or five minutes before a quarter-hour; fifteen-minute products match the snapshot time. Missing infrared intervals stay gaps. Visible channels may be absent when delayed or dark; the same dated infrared supplies cloud structure over the reference terrain. Catalog bounds follow all five infrared products, so delayed daylight colour cannot freeze every continent. The default slider covers the last 12 elapsed hours, including across local midnight, with up to 49 snapshot positions. The newest snapshot trails the clock while providers process their observations; the replay hint reports the last available cloud time. Live shows the cloud age in minutes. Each fresh visit checks both providers and the separately captured cloud index, even when a valid shared archive is present. Online checks repeat every five minutes; returning online triggers a fresh check. The archive cannot cap newer provider observations: direct requests fill its unpublished tail, and those positions join the compact replay. Canonical and uncached metadata paths are compared for both providers; one failed replica does not invalidate the other. During the October 6 UTC verification, NASA infrared clocks lagged the current time by about 70 minutes, while EUMETSAT was closer to 30 minutes. The complete global snapshot follows the slowest infrared feed. Selecting fifteen minutes or more beyond the displayed snapshot says "Clouds through" with that time, while calculated sunlight advances. The accessible summary retains the individual observation range. The interface only exposes the rolling 12-hour window; Live restores its current endpoint. The snapshot clock does not claim every pixel was acquired simultaneously. Live rejects a source more than five hours old. Three-hour EUMETSAT multimission maps remain available for older dates, back to their common June 2021 archive boundary.

Phone and desktop replay use the same 2048 by 1024 maps. WMS 1.3.0 requests use latitude-first EPSG:4326 bounds and an explicit advertised observation time. NASA requires whole-second timestamps without a fractional suffix. The recorder fetches ten independent maps: lossless PNG for the three NASA temperature-palette infrared channels, and JPEG for the remaining channels. JPEG compression changes palette RGB codes and can turn warm pixels into isolated bright clouds. Temperature codes are normalized at the original resolution before any display reduction. The recorder then blends visible and infrared RGBA images and encodes two WebP files at quality 90 with alpha quality 100. Lossy compression is safe after palette inversion because neutral brightness varies continuously. PNG retains its native missing-data alpha; the near-black fill mask applies only to JPEG. Sampled pairs total about 1.0 to 1.4 MB. Both have the same dimensions and compression settings. Recent visitors receive these prepared pairs; direct WMS remains a fallback. Every infrared feed is required. A delayed or missing visible channel leaves its pixels absent, and the current infrared layer supplies its cloud structure during both day and night. Each captured frame records source availability and all ten observation clocks. Responses require a valid image signature and matching dimensions; compressed pixels are decoded before installation. Since JPEG has no alpha, near-black fill (all channels below eight) is conservatively masked. This is a display heuristic, not an instrument quality flag; very dark valid pixels and compression artifacts can affect coverage. A three-degree fade stays inside that mask and wraps continuously at the longitude seam. Missing polar data exposes the reference terrain.

Meteosat natural colour includes cyan ice; a selective correction turns those bright features white. GOES GeoColor supplies real daytime colours and infrared-derived nighttime colours. All visible products show their bright cloud structure in white over the same static reference terrain, retaining consistent coloured oceans and land. Snow and bright ground can also appear as cloud. Older three-hour natural-colour maps retain their observed land colours. NASA clean-infrared images use a temperature colour palette. The display normalizes that published palette to neutral brightness; repeated grey palette values use the warmer interpretation by default. Bounded connected grey regions enclosed by unambiguous cold colour codes take the cold interpretation, preventing black punctures inside very cold storm cores. The decoder evaluates each complete region once, retains its original grey-code variation, requires cold support on every side and at least 60 percent of its observed boundary, and limits the region to three degrees in either direction (with an eight-pixel minimum at tiny fixture resolutions). Long grey bands, open boundaries, ordinary warm terrain and missing alpha retain their original interpretation. Processing revision 6 rebuilds prepared history, so cached revision 5 maps cannot keep the old dark cores. This conservative local decoding uses the same dated source pixels in the recorder, the stopped view and the replay worker. It does not recover a unique physical temperature from an ambiguous palette. It then applies the same illustrative thermal brightness curve as the EUMETSAT infrared overlay. Cold ground can appear bright, and some low warm clouds are indistinct. These layers do not measure a precise cloud fraction. Quarter-hour maps use one infrared brightness field on both hemispheres, so regional visible-image coverage cannot introduce another brightness edge. Its display curve uses a smooth entrance from 0.28 through 1.0 normalized infrared brightness. The upper range remains graded, preserving observed differences inside cold storm cores that the previous 0.75 ceiling clipped to solid white. Visible structure contributes only where infrared coverage is absent. The same field supplies day clouds and night-light attenuation. The selected instant supplies the only lighting transition; clouds are no longer cut off by a separate source-time Sun mask. Visible channels without usable nighttime data retain their gaps, which infrared fills. Source clocks and observed pixels stay unchanged. Older three-hour colour composites retain their source-time lighting blend. Short display crossfades connect quarter-hour observations; they do not estimate cloud motion between scans. The same observed visible and infrared cloud structure covers the night hemisphere. A dim blue-grey cloud layer softens the historical city lights, retaining 35 percent of their brightness under the brightest cloud pixels. The transition follows the selected sunlight continuously. Missing coverage leaves the reference surface and lights visible. Cloud brightness is an illustrative opacity cue, not a measured optical depth.

All five satellites are requested separately for visible and infrared channels, avoiding hard borders inside the NASA and Meteosat server mosaics. A smooth blend weights observed pixels toward the satellite with a more direct view, using approximate equatorial orbital longitudes, and fades at the geostationary horizon. It never adds cloud data outside the observed masks. Some GOES scans encode a bad horizontal band as opaque, saturated white infrared and cyan GeoColor, even in the original PNG. Before thermal normalization, a conservative display check masks rows below 60 degrees latitude only when near-white saturation covers at least 18 percent of the whole map width and 85 percent of its observed pixels. It masks the affected scan rows in both channels of that satellite, with four rows of padding at 2048 pixels to remove resampled borders, then blends remaining observations or exposes the reference map. Small bright cloud cores and polar snow stay intact. This heuristic is not an instrument quality flag. Archive processing revisions rebuild older prepared frames when this changes. This smooths display joins; differences in sensor detail, calibration and scan time can remain.

Six requests prepare the replay nearest the selected time first. The shared 2048-pixel observation pairs remain unchanged. A dedicated worker decodes and reduces them to one compact display map containing visible brightness, visible coverage, thermal brightness and thermal coverage. Dragging uses a 1536 by 768 map on desktop and a 1024 by 512 map on phones with WebGL2. WebGL1 retains 1024 by 512 on desktop and 512 by 256 on phones. All tiers respect the GPU texture limit. These maps stay at a consistent resolution throughout a drag. The worker runs one preparation at a time and transfers its pixel buffer. If workers or OffscreenCanvas are unavailable, a bounded fallback yields between small row batches. For direct WMS fallback, the same worker reads the ten original maps at their source resolution, masks scan artifacts and normalizes temperature codes before reducing the neutral pixels, feathering coverage and blending satellites. Reducing palette RGB first would mix unrelated temperature codes and reintroduce speckles. The stopped view still processes the original full-resolution sources.

The separate full-resolution canvas cache is 32 / 80 MiB, and compressed responses remain bounded at 48 / 96 MiB. A full civil day can exceed the compact cache; evicted frames prepare again on demand. Six compact GPU textures are retained for nearby and revisited frames. Two small render targets preserve the currently displayed cloud coverage when a new observation interrupts a 120-millisecond crossfade. This prevents rapid reversals from resetting the blend to an older frame. Slider events coalesce into one selection per animation frame. Warm dragging performs no full-size cloud decodes or uploads. After 350 milliseconds without input, the selected original observation pair loads and fades in over 220 milliseconds. The compact map remains visible if that detail request fails. These are display crossfades between real observations, not a meteorological interpolation or an invented motion field. The accessible source clock belongs to the incoming observation.

A separate 128-response cache holds shared WebP assets, and direct WMS uses a 640-response cache. Missing, future and unsupported selections clear the cloud layer on the next animation frame. A cold selection keeps the last installed clouds and their actual clock until its replacement is ready, labeled Loading clouds. Late requests cannot replace the newest selection.

The opening view waits for its decoded cloud pair, local astronomy assets, saved aurora and all available compact frames in the rolling 12-hour window. The replay portion of its loading bar counts prepared cloud frames. Controls can select any published snapshot during preparation; the previous clouds remain visible until its pair is ready. Preparation stops at 26 seconds from renderer startup if sources stall and continues afterward. Live imagery and every new replay frame share the same base resolution; desktop zoom requests 4096 pixels once the camera is closer than 3.5 Earth radii, or when the canvas height exceeds 900 physical pixels, including fullscreen. The complete 2048-pixel pair stays visible during the upgrade. Phones retain the 2048 cap. Dragging uses the consistent compact tier described above; the stopped view retains the original resolution. Source resolution and viewing angle can still differ across satellites. Provider errors settle into a dated three-hour or daily fallback, or the reference map, with their source clocks disclosed.

`tools/archive-globe-clouds.cjs` runs independently of visitors in the weather workflow. It retains 26 hours of published quarter-hour snapshots, up to 105 frames, providing a buffer beyond the default 12-hour window. Version 2 manifests record per-product observation clocks; existing version 1 hourly snapshots remain readable during migration. It verifies timestamps, coverage, dimensions and SHA-256 hashes before publishing a complete snapshot to the generated `globe-clouds` branch. The snapshot has no parent commit, and an exact branch SHA lease protects concurrent publication. Publication retries transient server errors after five and fifteen seconds, checks whether an ambiguous error already accepted the exact commit, and stops if another writer changed the branch. An older recorder refuses to replace a snapshot with a newer processing revision. The main branch and its site files are untouched by pixel publication. Only current snapshot files are retained on that branch; GitHub may retain older unreachable Git objects. The manifest and immutable, hash-named images are served through raw GitHub with CORS. Each browser validates the image size, dimensions and checksum, then prepares the compact replay and decodes the active full-resolution pair. Cache Storage improves later visits but does not supply the shared history. Source or scheduler outages can leave gaps or stale history; direct dated sources remain available as fallback. The recorder also retains the separately validated cloud index as a metadata fallback, so a temporary catalog timeout cannot block already advertised newer scans. Its metadata deadline is 30 seconds, independent of the browser loading budget. New frames publish before slower repairs to older colour companions. This cannot promise uninterrupted observation during a provider outage.

NASA NOAA-21 and NOAA-20 VIIRS corrected-reflectance composites remain the final daily fallback. They use a separate 16-response cache and a conservative black-fill mask. A daily image is explicitly labeled, rather than presented as hourly weather.

Individual satellite downloads retry once in the browser and twice in the recorder, preserving the exact published time and bypassing cached error responses. The recorder repairs up to six retained frames with missing, now-published visible channels per capture, after publishing new weather. Attempts rotate by their previous retry clock and wait at least 15 minutes between repairs. A replacement must recover a channel without losing any existing observation. Failed or poorer repairs preserve the valid old pair; fully unavailable colour feeds still use infrared. Repair clocks are validated and superseded asset files are removed from the bounded snapshot. This recovers transient gaps that would otherwise remain frozen into the shared history and cause neighbouring snapshots to switch between visible and thermal display curves.

## Aurora, replay and visibility

OVATION provides 65,160 geographic values: a one-degree grid with 360 longitudes and 181 latitudes, plus Observation Time and Forecast Time. The parser validates every cell, uniqueness, finite coordinates, probability range and valid UTC times. Pin sampling is bilinear with longitude wrapping. The GPU texture has two padded seam columns and ClampToEdge, so its 362 by 181 non-power-of-two dimensions work in WebGL1 as well as WebGL2. Geographic node centres, both polar rows and the duplicate seam are independently tested against raw source coordinates.

The glow illustrates the stronger forecast above a 10 percent display threshold, with a soft entrance to 22 percent. Raw values below that threshold remain available in the pin. No geographic latitude cutoff is imposed. `js/globe-aurora.js` creates thin, folded ribbons with genuine vertical geometry. Their rays follow an approximate centered dipole, while each geographic footpoint samples the untouched NOAA probability texture. Both hemispheres remain supported, including unusual low-latitude forecasts. Earth hides curtains behind it; their upper rays can rise beyond the limb, and orbiting changes their apparent depth. Green emission sits lower, with restrained red higher up and a faint violet lower edge. Fine rays use screen derivatives to avoid flickering at a distance. Slow changes in height and brightness suggest motion without moving the forecast oval.

The displayed base is about 100 km above Earth. The upper extent is deliberately stretched to roughly 900 km so the curtains read at globe scale. NOAA does not supply these individual heights, folds, rays, colours or calibrated radiance. Strength 1.5 sets illustrative screen exposure; daylight suppression, forecast timestamps, probabilities and locations retain their previous meaning. The fixed geometry uses eight WebGL1-compatible parts and about 13.2 MiB of typed buffers. Replay reuses those buffers and selects only forecast-supported segments, rather than rebuilding the ribbons on each time input. A real 28 percent saved forecast and the authentic 41 percent storm fixture both produce raised green pixels beyond the globe silhouette in Chrome and mobile WebKit. The old surface-shell alternatives have been superseded by these curtains.

Physical references: [NASA's description of curtains and field-aligned rays](https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/Education/waurora1.html), [aurora photographed from the ISS](https://science.nasa.gov/earth/earth-observatory/aurora-australis-observed-from-the-international-space-station-44348/).


Live fetches NOAA every minute. It accepts observations at most three hours old or five minutes ahead, and forecasts at most 90 minutes old or two hours ahead. These are application freshness limits. Stale glow disappears and is labeled unavailable. Kp retains its own three-hour interval and is not used to manufacture the oval. Earth keeps its actual date-specific tilt.

NOAA's public geographic JSON provides the latest forecast only. Its rolling image animation is not a historic numeric grid API. `tools/archive-globe-aurora.cjs` therefore preserves genuine fetched grids as compact, lossless, gzip JSON, retaining 30 days. The manifest records both source timestamps and SHA-256 of each saved file. Initial history contains authentic snapshots collected during development, beginning with observations on October 4, 2026 at 23:38 UTC; coverage initially has gaps.

`tools/archive-globe-weather.cjs` coordinates independent cloud-index and aurora captures. A narrowly scoped GitHub Actions workflow starts at minutes 7, 22, 37 and 52, then takes twelve fresh samples at five-minute intervals during each run. Keeping the runner active fills more history than depending on a new scheduled job for each sample. It retains up to 10,000 manifest entries, enough for 30 days of five-minute sampling. It validates freshness, avoids duplicate writes and commits only the aurora subtree and two cloud time indices, with bounded rebase retries. Actual schedule delivery can be delayed. Browser history reads raw files on the repository's main branch, independent of Pages rebuilds; GitHub's five-minute cache can add latency. The static deployed files provide a fallback copy. Each saved frame is about 4 to 6 KB compressed in sampled conditions. Retention deletes current files, while old Git objects remain in repository history.

The decoded aurora cache holds up to 400 grids, enough for a five-minute day. A verified latest NOAA grid is also retained during the browser session with its actual observation and forecast timestamps. Explore chooses the saved forecast target closest to the selected instant, within 90 minutes, excluding measurements later than that instant. It labels both the actual forecast target and its input observation time. It does not interpolate forecasts, reuse the current grid for arbitrary dates, or invent history before recording began. Missing history stays unavailable. Native gzip decompression is required for replay; if it is unavailable the rest of the globe continues.

The percentage is NOAA's modeled probability overhead, not a personal chance of seeing it through clouds or city lights. A forecast is also different from a confirmed sighting. NOAA says bright aurora can sometimes be visible roughly 600 miles from its overhead location. Pins separately report distance to nearby cells above 10 percent, with daylight/twilight qualifiers. Clear dark skies, an unobstructed horizon and low light pollution matter; Moonlight can reduce apparent brightness. There is no local cloud fraction or guaranteed naked-eye visibility calculation. The authentic storm fixture has Kp 5.67; no storm grade is inferred by rounding it.

## Astronomy and clocks

[Math notes](DAYLIGHT_GLOBE_MATH.md) document the NOAA Meeus Sun calculation, minus 0.833 degree sunrise horizon, polar transitions, Schlyter lunar orbit with perturbations and independent reference fixtures. The Sun disk follows the same calculated direction that lights Earth, with a fixed approximate 0.535-degree angular diameter. It is placed 75 display units from the current camera along that direction, avoiding the large false parallax of a compressed Earth-centered distance. True observer parallax, annual solar disk variation and relativistic corrections are omitted. The internal solar-framing test helper sits 145 degrees from the Sun at radius 4; an approximately 10.395-degree aim shift and at least a 58-degree horizontal field fit Earth and Sun together. A 42-degree vertical floor preserves Earth's limbs in ultrawide and short landscape views. Orbiting uses Earth-centered framing; the actual Sun is visible when its bearing enters the camera view.

The photosphere uses a simple linear limb-darkening law with coefficient 0.6. The halo and restrained rays illustrate camera bloom, not measured solar corona or a calibrated exposure. `js/globe-optics.js` computes camera-relative spherical disks for Earth and the displayed Moon. Single-body visible area uses analytic spherical-cap overlap. Visible photospheric flux integrates continuous angular arc unions, weighted by the same limb-darkening law. Overlapping Earth/Moon shadows cannot remove the same light twice. Core pixels use actual GPU depth testing. The displayed Moon is opaque from either side. Zero visible solar area also hides the core explicitly, so a near-plane slice through the compressed Moon cannot reveal a contradictory Sun. Observer bloom may spread a small glint over a foreground limb, but its strength follows remaining flux and reaches exactly zero at full occultation. Compression makes these visual crossings unsuitable for predicting terrestrial eclipse locations or dates.

The sky is centered on the camera at radius 120 to represent infinity, while Earth-oriented directions follow the selected instant. Measured magnitude controls size and brightness with a quarter-power display compression; it does not represent a single photographic exposure. B-V gives approximate stellar colour. The 23 missing indices use a neutral colour. Local solar glare dims nearby stars. Failed catalog loading leaves no invented fallback stars and is disclosed in the accessible summary. Keeping stars visible beside bright Earth and Sun expands the display dynamic range, as explained by [NASA's exposure discussion](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/earth-matters/2011/09/28/where-are-the-stars/).

The reader's clock and slider use the browser's civil zone and Intl, including daylight saving. Both cloud structure and partially attenuated historical lights remain visible across the night hemisphere.

The top-left version label is set by the loaded renderer script, so it identifies the executing build even if the HTML is cached. Live is the default and keeps advancing. The slider and Live button stay visible, beside the scene on desktop and below it on phones. The slider covers the last 12 elapsed hours, including across local midnight and daylight-saving changes. Detailed source clocks and missing-data explanations remain in the accessible summary. The visible time output includes its civil-zone abbreviation. Dragging, zooming, pinning and fullscreen preserve the astronomical state. The pin has four semantic facts: solar time, daylight, sunrise/sunset and aurora. Its text summary updates after mode, source or pin changes rather than every animation frame.

Dragging and arrows can orbit continuously through either pole. The camera's tangent up vector follows the orbit without a pole flip; horizontal input follows the visible orientation when inverted.
