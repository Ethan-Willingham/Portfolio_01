# Daylight Globe

The globe combines dated NASA and EUMETSAT satellite imagery, calculated sunlight, historical city lights and NOAA's short-term aurora forecasts. Each source keeps its own clock. The time controls select actual dated images and saved forecast grids where available. The site remains static, with locally vendored Three.js r128 and no new runtime library. The opening view centres on the device location with browser permission; its controls remain visible. The Sun button frames a dusk Earth and the real solar bearing.

## Satellite clouds and their history

`js/globe-clouds.js` combines GOES East/West GeoColor, Himawari red-visible and clean-infrared maps from NASA GIBS with Meteosat IODC natural colour/infrared and MTG true colour/infrared from EUMETView. These regional feeds publish at ten- or fifteen-minute intervals. The recorder prepares a snapshot every fifteen minutes, using each product's latest advertised observation at or before that snapshot. It never selects a later observation or carries an observation beyond its native cadence. With the current aligned feeds, ten-minute products use observations zero or five minutes before a quarter-hour; fifteen-minute products match the snapshot time. Missing infrared intervals stay gaps. Visible channels may be absent when delayed or dark; the same dated infrared supplies cloud structure over the reference terrain. Catalog bounds follow all five infrared products, so delayed daylight colour cannot freeze every continent. The default slider covers the last 12 elapsed hours, including across local midnight, with up to 49 snapshot positions. The newest snapshot trails the clock while providers process their observations; its time remains visible. Selecting fifteen minutes or more beyond the displayed snapshot says "Clouds through" with that time, while calculated sunlight advances. The Sources section discloses the individual observation range. Choosing a date switches to a civil-day slider; Live restores the rolling window. A complete civil day has 96 snapshots, with 92 or 100 on daylight-saving transition days. The snapshot clock does not claim every pixel was acquired simultaneously. Live rejects a source more than five hours old. Three-hour EUMETSAT multimission maps remain available for older dates, back to their common June 2021 archive boundary.

Phone and desktop replay use the same 2048 by 1024 maps. WMS 1.3.0 requests use latitude-first EPSG:4326 bounds and an explicit advertised observation time. NASA requires whole-second timestamps without a fractional suffix. The recorder fetches ten independent JPEG maps, blends them into visible and infrared RGBA images, then encodes two WebP files at quality 90 with alpha quality 100. Sampled pairs total about 1.0 to 1.4 MB. Both have the same dimensions and compression settings. Recent visitors receive these prepared pairs; direct WMS remains a fallback. Every infrared feed is required. A delayed or missing visible channel leaves its pixels absent, and the current infrared layer supplies its cloud structure during both day and night. Each captured frame records source availability and all ten observation clocks. Responses require a valid image signature and matching dimensions; compressed pixels are decoded before installation. Since JPEG has no alpha, near-black fill (all channels below eight) is conservatively masked. This is a display heuristic, not an instrument quality flag; very dark valid pixels and compression artifacts can affect coverage. A three-degree fade stays inside that mask and wraps continuously at the longitude seam. Missing polar data exposes the reference terrain.

Meteosat natural colour includes cyan ice; a selective correction turns those bright features white. GOES GeoColor supplies real daytime colours and infrared-derived nighttime colours. All visible products show their bright cloud structure in white over the same static reference terrain, retaining consistent coloured oceans and land. Snow and bright ground can also appear as cloud. Older three-hour natural-colour maps retain their observed land colours. NASA clean-infrared images use a temperature colour palette. The display normalizes that published palette to neutral brightness; repeated grey palette values use the warmer interpretation to avoid painting warm ground as cloud. It then applies the same illustrative thermal brightness curve as the EUMETSAT infrared overlay. Cold ground can appear bright, and some low warm clouds are indistinct. These layers do not measure a precise cloud fraction. The selected instant changes illumination; the source-time Sun determines where visible imagery can be used. No invented cloud motion fills the interval between quarter-hour snapshots. The same source-time visible or infrared cloud structure covers the night hemisphere. A dim blue-grey cloud layer softens the historical city lights, retaining 35 percent of their brightness under the brightest cloud pixels. The transition follows the selected sunlight continuously. Missing coverage leaves the reference surface and lights visible. Cloud brightness is an illustrative opacity cue, not a measured optical depth.

All five satellites are requested separately for visible and infrared channels, avoiding hard borders inside the NASA and Meteosat server mosaics. A smooth blend weights observed pixels toward the satellite with a more direct view, using approximate equatorial orbital longitudes, and fades at the geostationary horizon. It never adds cloud data outside the observed masks. Some GOES scans encode a bad horizontal band as opaque, saturated white infrared and cyan GeoColor, even in the original PNG. Before thermal normalization, a conservative display check masks rows below 60 degrees latitude only when near-white saturation covers at least 18 percent of the whole map width and 85 percent of its observed pixels. It masks the affected scan rows in both channels of that satellite, with four rows of padding at 2048 pixels to remove resampled borders, then blends remaining observations or exposes the reference map. Small bright cloud cores and polar snow stay intact. This heuristic is not an instrument quality flag. Archive processing revisions rebuild older prepared frames when this changes. This smooths display joins; differences in sensor detail, calibration and scan time can remain.

Six workers preload compressed prepared pairs. A separate 128-response cache holds WebP assets, and the direct WMS fallback uses a 2128-response cache. A bounded 48 MiB phone / 96 MiB desktop memory cache keeps recent compressed frames, while the existing 64 / 96 MiB decoded LRU keeps a few sharp canvas pairs. Only the active pair has GPU textures. Cached compressed selections decode without downloading another copy; rapid slider input coalesces pending decoding to the newest selection. Frames can take a short moment to decode when scrubbing beyond the decoded cache. The last installed pair stays visible and pinned until both replacement maps are decoded. Its source label remains unchanged, and the replay line says Loading clouds during the wait. Source and generation guards prevent a late result from showing at the wrong time. A source error remains a labeled gap once the request settles; future times, unavailable dates and hypothetical tilts hide weather immediately. The shared manifest has a 3.5-second deadline and includes validated publication bounds, avoiding provider metadata calls in the recent path. Direct metadata has a six-second deadline; validated quarter-hour and three-hour indices saved by the weather recorder provide fallback publication bounds. NASA metadata is checked through its usual URL and a minute-keyed URL, using the newer validated infrared bound. Saved catalog and pixel publication cannot regress behind already verified observations when a metadata replica lags. Neither path extrapolates beyond an advertised end.

The opening view waits for its decoded cloud pair, local astronomy assets, saved aurora and an hourly overview of the rolling window plus the two nearest quarter-hour maps. The remaining quarter-hour snapshots then preload in the background with visible progress. Controls can select any published snapshot during preparation; the previous clouds remain visible until its pair is ready. Preparation stops at 26 seconds from renderer startup if sources stall and continues afterward. Live imagery and every new replay frame share the same base resolution; desktop zoom can still request 4096 pixels, while phones retain the 2048 cap. This removes the former 1024-pixel replay previews and their frame-by-frame sharpening jumps. Source resolution and viewing angle can still differ across satellites. Provider errors settle into a dated three-hour or daily fallback, or the reference map, with their source clocks disclosed.

`tools/archive-globe-clouds.cjs` runs independently of visitors in the weather workflow. It retains 26 hours of published quarter-hour snapshots, up to 105 frames, providing a buffer beyond the default 12-hour window. Version 2 manifests record per-product observation clocks; existing version 1 hourly snapshots remain readable during migration. It verifies timestamps, coverage, dimensions and SHA-256 hashes before publishing a complete snapshot to the generated `globe-clouds` branch. The snapshot has no parent commit, and an exact branch SHA lease protects concurrent publication. Publication retries transient server errors after five and fifteen seconds, checks whether an ambiguous error already accepted the exact commit, and stops if another writer changed the branch. The main branch and its site files are untouched by pixel publication. Only current snapshot files are retained on that branch; GitHub may retain older unreachable Git objects. The manifest and immutable, hash-named images are served through raw GitHub with CORS. Each browser validates the image size, dimensions and checksum, then decodes only the active pair. Cache Storage improves later visits but does not supply the shared history. Source or scheduler outages can leave gaps or stale history; direct dated sources remain available as fallback. This cannot promise uninterrupted observation during a provider outage.

NASA NOAA-21 and NOAA-20 VIIRS corrected-reflectance composites remain the final daily fallback. They use a separate 16-response cache and a conservative black-fill mask. A daily image is explicitly labeled, rather than presented as hourly weather.

## Aurora, replay and visibility

OVATION provides 65,160 geographic values: a one-degree grid with 360 longitudes and 181 latitudes, plus Observation Time and Forecast Time. The parser validates every cell, uniqueness, finite coordinates, probability range and valid UTC times. Pin sampling is bilinear with longitude wrapping. The GPU texture has two padded seam columns and ClampToEdge, so its 362 by 181 non-power-of-two dimensions work in WebGL1 as well as WebGL2. Geographic node centres, both polar rows and the duplicate seam are independently tested against raw source coordinates.

The glow illustrates the stronger forecast above a 10 percent display threshold, with a soft entrance to 22 percent. Raw values below that threshold remain available in the pin. This global threshold suppresses weak equatorial and singular-pole traces present in actual NOAA responses; no geographic latitude cutoff is imposed. The default uses one thin elevated shell, restrained curtain modulation and a weak face-on contribution that grows toward the limb. This keeps the forecast visible without painting the cloud surface green. Curtain detail and motion are illustrative, not a high-resolution observation of individual auroral rays. An unlisted noindex lab retains the four visual alternatives, with narrow curtains selected provisionally.

Live fetches NOAA every minute. It accepts observations at most three hours old or five minutes ahead, and forecasts at most 90 minutes old or two hours ahead. These are application freshness limits. Stale glow disappears and is labeled unavailable. Kp retains its own three-hour interval and is not used to manufacture the oval. Hypothetical tilts hide weather and use the reference map.

NOAA's public geographic JSON provides the latest forecast only. Its rolling image animation is not a historic numeric grid API. `tools/archive-globe-aurora.cjs` therefore preserves genuine fetched grids as compact, lossless, gzip JSON, retaining 30 days. The manifest records both source timestamps and SHA-256 of each saved file. Initial history contains authentic snapshots collected during development, beginning with observations on October 4, 2026 at 23:38 UTC; coverage initially has gaps.

`tools/archive-globe-weather.cjs` coordinates independent cloud-index and aurora captures. A narrowly scoped GitHub Actions workflow starts at minutes 7, 22, 37 and 52, then takes twelve fresh samples at five-minute intervals during each run. Keeping the runner active fills more history than depending on a new scheduled job for each sample. It retains up to 10,000 manifest entries, enough for 30 days of five-minute sampling. It validates freshness, avoids duplicate writes and commits only the aurora subtree and two cloud time indices, with bounded rebase retries. Actual schedule delivery can be delayed. Browser history reads raw files on the repository's main branch, independent of Pages rebuilds; GitHub's five-minute cache can add latency. The static deployed files provide a fallback copy. Each saved frame is about 4 to 6 KB compressed in sampled conditions. Retention deletes current files, while old Git objects remain in repository history.

The decoded aurora cache holds up to 400 grids, enough for a five-minute day. A verified latest NOAA grid is also retained during the browser session with its actual observation and forecast timestamps. Explore chooses the saved forecast target closest to the selected instant, within 90 minutes, excluding measurements later than that instant. It labels both the actual forecast target and its input observation time. It does not interpolate forecasts, reuse the current grid for arbitrary dates, or invent history before recording began. Missing history stays unavailable. Native gzip decompression is required for replay; if it is unavailable the rest of the globe continues.

The percentage is NOAA's modeled probability overhead, not a personal chance of seeing it through clouds or city lights. A forecast is also different from a confirmed sighting. NOAA says bright aurora can sometimes be visible roughly 600 miles from its overhead location. Pins separately report distance to nearby cells above 10 percent, with daylight/twilight qualifiers. Clear dark skies, an unobstructed horizon and low light pollution matter; Moonlight can reduce apparent brightness. There is no local cloud fraction or guaranteed naked-eye visibility calculation. The authentic storm fixture has Kp 5.67; no storm grade is inferred by rounding it.

Sources: [NOAA aurora product and model](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/products/aurora-30-minute-forecast), [ground viewing guidance](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/content/tips-viewing-aurora), [planetary Kp](https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/products/planetary-k-index), [NOAA's plotting implementation](https://github.com/NOAA-SWPC/OVATION-legacy).

## Astronomy and clocks

[Math notes](DAYLIGHT_GLOBE_MATH.md) document the NOAA Meeus Sun calculation, minus 0.833 degree sunrise horizon, polar transitions, Schlyter lunar orbit with perturbations and independent reference fixtures. The Sun disk follows the same calculated direction that lights Earth, with a fixed approximate 0.535-degree angular diameter. It is placed 75 display units from the current camera along that direction, avoiding the large false parallax of a compressed Earth-centered distance. True observer parallax, annual solar disk variation and relativistic corrections are omitted. The opening observer sits 145 degrees from the Sun at radius 4; an approximately 10.395-degree aim shift and at least a 58-degree horizontal field fit Earth and Sun together. A 42-degree vertical floor preserves Earth's limbs in ultrawide and short landscape views. The Sun button restores this view; orbiting restores Earth-centered framing smoothly.

The photosphere uses a simple linear limb-darkening law with coefficient 0.6. The halo and restrained rays illustrate camera bloom, not measured solar corona or a calibrated exposure. `js/globe-optics.js` computes camera-relative spherical disks for Earth and the displayed Moon. Single-body visible area uses analytic spherical-cap overlap. Visible photospheric flux integrates continuous angular arc unions, weighted by the same limb-darkening law. Overlapping Earth/Moon shadows cannot remove the same light twice. Core pixels use actual GPU depth testing. The displayed Moon is opaque from either side. Zero visible solar area also hides the core explicitly, so a near-plane slice through the compressed Moon cannot reveal a contradictory Sun. Observer bloom may spread a small glint over a foreground limb, but its strength follows remaining flux and reaches exactly zero at full occultation. Compression makes these visual crossings unsuitable for predicting terrestrial eclipse locations or dates.

Sources: [NASA photosphere](https://solarscience.msfc.nasa.gov/surface.shtml), [Neckel and Labs limb profiles](https://articles.adsabs.harvard.edu/pdf/1994SoPh..153...91N), [Spencer et al., glare rendering](https://www.graphics.cornell.edu/pubs/1995/SSZG95.pdf), [NASA eclipse photography](https://eclipse.gsfc.nasa.gov/SEpubs/19980226/text/photography.html), [Perlman and Wellner spherical-cap geometry](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-8994/3/3/574).

The background now uses 5,070 actual HYG 4.1 stars, apparent visual magnitude 6 and brighter, with measured coordinates, proper motions, visual magnitudes and B-V indices. [Star notes](DAYLIGHT_GLOBE_STARS.md) document the pinned source, compact binary, licensing, precession and sidereal transform. The sky is centered on the camera at radius 120 to represent infinity, while Earth-oriented directions follow the selected instant. Measured magnitude controls size and brightness with a quarter-power display compression; it does not represent a single photographic exposure. B-V gives approximate stellar colour. The 23 missing indices use a neutral colour. Local solar glare dims nearby stars. Failed catalog loading leaves no invented fallback stars and is disclosed in Sources. Keeping stars visible beside bright Earth and Sun expands the display dynamic range, as explained by [NASA's exposure discussion](https://science.nasa.gov/blogs/earth-matters/2011/09/28/where-are-the-stars/).

Current Earth uses date-specific obliquity. Alternate tilts are hypothetical; the Moon is hidden at 45 degrees. Lunar direction and illumination follow the selected instant. Orbital distance is compressed, with actual solar light transported into the display frame to retain the physical phase from the camera position. Surface libration is omitted.

The reader's clock and Explore use the browser's civil zone and Intl, including daylight saving. Geographic pins use explicitly labeled local apparent solar time, incorporating longitude and the equation of time. Exact civil boundary data evaluated during research was about 12 MB compressed; a compact approximate lookup admitted wrong offsets. Sunrise and sunset use the same stated clock basis as the pin.

## Controls and accessibility

Every visit requests the device location through the browser's Geolocation API, with an eight-second acquisition timeout, low-power accuracy and at most five minutes of browser-cached position age. Location permission does not hold the loading screen open. A latitude and longitude rounded to one decimal place are remembered in local storage for at most 30 days, allowing an immediate initial view while the fresh fix arrives. A new fix replaces that remembered view, including after travel. The site sends no location coordinates to the global imagery, weather or astronomy services. Denied permission clears the remembered position and gives a short explanation; My location retries. Dragging, zooming, keyboard exploration or selecting Sun prevents a late position from moving the camera. My location explicitly returns to the current device position. Without a valid location, the initial view faces Africa rather than claiming a guessed home location.

Opening the page directly as a local file uses the public raw-GitHub copies of the Earth, historical lights, Moon and star catalog. Chrome blocks fetches to neighbouring file URLs; the published copies allow cross-origin requests and still decode into the same bounded canvas textures. HTTP and HTTPS pages retain local same-origin asset requests. Each asset has a 12-second deadline; a failed city-light map is now disclosed along with unavailable terrain and stars. The reference terrain stays below the selected visible or infrared clouds. Night opacity uses observed brightness before any reference-land colour is mixed in, avoiding false cloud masking over bright terrain. Both cloud structure and partially attenuated historical lights remain visible across the night hemisphere.

Live is the default and keeps advancing. Date, time and tilt controls are always visible, beside the scene on desktop and below it on phones. Date, time or tilt changes leave Live; Live restores the current instant, rolling 12-hour window and actual tilt. Rolling hours use elapsed time through daylight-saving changes; historical dates keep their civil-day controls. Short cloud and aurora labels retain their actual source times. Full timestamps, missing-data explanations, Kp and credits live in the optional Sources section, which stays reachable in fullscreen. The visible time output includes its civil-zone abbreviation. Dragging, zooming, pinning and fullscreen preserve the astronomical state. The pin has four semantic facts: solar time, daylight, sunrise/sunset and aurora. Its text summary updates after mode, source or pin changes rather than every animation frame.

With the globe focused, arrows rotate, plus/minus zoom, Enter pins the centre and Escape leaves fullscreen before clearing a pin. Controls have explicit labels and 44-pixel targets. Native fullscreen has a CSS fallback. Its focus cycle includes source summaries and links. Enlarged text can scroll the rail/dock to reveal each full control without horizontal clipping. Rendering stops in hidden or offscreen tabs and resumes on return. Animation remains enabled for every reader by owner instruction.

The loading screen covers the scene until local Earth, city lights, Moon and star assets are decoded, cloud imagery has settled into a source or labeled fallback, NOAA and archive checks have settled, the opening replay overview has finished or reached its startup deadline, and the installed cloud texture has fully blended into a rendered frame. Its bar counts weighted completed work (clouds carry five of fourteen units, replay three); it is not a download-byte percentage. Initial time controls are visible and become active when loading finishes. Fullscreen remains usable during loading. Request failures settle instead of leaving the reader waiting for a source that is unavailable.
