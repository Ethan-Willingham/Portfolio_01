# Fire simulation research for Sluice

Researched September 20, 2026. This is a greenfield design study for the next fire system. The polygon coal and staged material burn model shipped separately in v28.61. The initial implementation now ships in v28.63; see [FIRE_SIMULATION.md](FIRE_SIMULATION.md) for its measured behavior and remaining scope. This document records the original survey and proposed roadmap.

The recommendation is a bounded WebGPU reacting-flow solver, with reusable fluid algorithms from NVIDIA Flow, combustion modeling informed by Fire-X and the Bifrost research, and a separate material model informed by FlameForge. No inspected browser package supplies all of the requested material, oxygen, heat, geometry, and water interactions. The implementation choices below are our engineering assessment, not performance claims made by those projects.

The survey covered browser demos, open GPU solvers, scientific CFD, material-combustion research, and commercial effects tools. The strongest candidates received source and license checks. This is a practical survey, not an exhaustive inventory of every fire project. External solvers were not built or benchmarked locally.

Retain required copyright and license notices for any source actually adapted, record its revision, and describe modifications. No external source code was incorporated during this research. A paper's Creative Commons license or a project website's template license is not evidence that its simulator source has been released.

Fire-X sets a useful ambition, with an important cost limit. Its [paper, section 5 and appendix A.2](https://helgewrede.github.io/firex/data/FireXExtinguishingFireWithStoichiometricHeatRelease.pdf) reports an RTX 4090 workstation. Example grid steps range from 7 to 65 ms; rendering ranges from 10 to 960 ms depending on the scene. These are component timings, not a promise of full-game frame rate. Section 6.2 also acknowledges diffuse small flames where an explicit flame-front method does better. Use its coupled thermodynamics as a reference, then measure our own domain and renderer.

For Sluice, the proposed division of work is:

1. **Moving air and reacting gases.** A staggered grid stores velocity, density/enthalpy, oxygen, selected fuel species, combustion products, water vapor, and soot. Hot gases move because of buoyancy and expansion. Fuel burns only where fuel, oxygen, temperature, and the reaction model allow it. The pressure solve must retain combustion expansion instead of forcing every heated cell to preserve volume. Bifrost's published low-speed treatment is a useful reference for that distinction.
2. **Burning materials.** Each body has dry mass, moisture, char/ash reservoirs, heat capacity, conductivity, reaction parameters, and a surface/interior temperature representation. Heating can dry and decompose a solid before released gases ignite. Char oxidation is a surface reaction with its own oxygen supply. Wood needs an insulating char layer; paper needs thin geometry; liquid fuel needs evaporation. These are material definitions feeding the same gas system.
3. **Geometry and heat exchange.** Rasterize the real moving polygons into gas-cell occupancy and face openings. The grate, pile, walls, and flue determine air supply. Return gas-to-solid heat and surface oxygen flux. Let heat loss and fuel consumption affect shrinking bodies and their mechanical state. Air passages narrower than the grid require refinement or a measured porous-flow approximation; increasing solver iterations cannot recover missing geometry.
4. **Water and steam.** Transfer energy into actual water mass, budget evaporation against available energy, and add that lost liquid mass to vapor. Wet fuel stores moisture and must spend energy drying. The existing liquid solver can keep owning liquid motion while a thermal buffer and explicit exchange pass add the missing thermodynamics. The coupling needs accounting for both phases and device-loss recovery.
5. **Visible fire.** Derive luminous soot from temperature and soot concentration; derive the blue reaction contribution from combustion activity and fuel properties. Smoke persists after visible flame ceases. Hot solids can glow after gas burning stops. Display exposure and color mapping remain artistic choices, but they must not alter reaction rates or heat delivered to the boiler. The Bifrost paper specifically separates soot glow from reaction emission.

The existing WebGPU infrastructure can host this work. `js/smoke-wgpu.js` borrows `liquid.device` after `liquid.readyPromise`, shares its queue, and handles device loss. `SMOKE_WGPU_GOLIVE` is true in `js/sluice/190-smoke-webgl.js`, but the master `USE_WEBGPU_SMOKE` switch in `020-state.js` is false. The shipped smoke backend is WebGL; the shared-device WebGPU port is available infrastructure, not the default backend. `js/liquid-wgpu.js` already has GPU-resident particle buffers, asynchronous mirrors, and mutation sequencing. Reuse those lifecycle patterns and coordinate conventions. The new fire kernels should be designed independently of the existing flame renderer.

The critical integration change is ownership. The current coal model must become a material adapter to the new gas simulation. It cannot keep independently burning the same fuel and adding heat while the gas solver also consumes it. Give thermal reservoirs and gas fields one authoritative update path, with geometry commands entering and acknowledged reductions leaving. If thermal state lives on the GPU, CPU body sizing, UI, and saving read versioned summaries. Asynchronous readback may delay display, but it must never spend fuel twice, restore older reservoirs, or duplicate evaporated water. Boiler heat is measured transfer into the vessel, not flame brightness.

Define the unit mapping before tuning. Game coordinates need a chosen physical length and depth; each material needs a mass scale. Heat capacity, latent heat, temperatures, gas injection, and the boiler's water heating must use consistent units. Legacy normalized coal temperatures require an explicit save migration. A large parameter table does not make the model physically coherent if its mass and energy scales disagree.

The first domain should cover the boiler, air inlet, and enough flue to establish draft. A 256 by 192 two-dimensional grid has 49,152 cells and is a reasonable initial experiment, not an established optimum. A later 192 by 128 by 32 slab has 786,432 cells and can resolve motion through depth while keeping a fixed side view. Full three-dimensional turbulence costs substantially more and should follow measured evidence that it improves the visible result. Two-dimensional flow has different vortex behavior; it must be described honestly as an approximation.

Use a compact dense grid first. Sparse allocation adds complexity that is valuable for world-scale spreading fire, but a small occupied firebox may not benefit. Benchmark multigrid against simpler pressure iteration at the actual grid size. Flow's pressure infrastructure is a reference, not a reason to transplant its entire allocator or editor.

For transport, explicitly evaluate conservation. Semi-Lagrangian and limited MacCormack advection are useful fluid tools, but limiter bounds alone do not establish conservation of fuel or oxygen. Start the chemical fields with conservative fluxes and positivity constraints, or demonstrate and bound the error of another scheme. Reaction integration must limit consumption to available species, balance product mass, account for released energy, and handle stiff rates with substeps or an appropriate implicit update. Avoid fixing unstable temperature by silently discarding energy.

Implementation should advance through observable gates:

| Gate | Concrete result | Evidence required |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Numerical core | A standalone WebGPU burner with debug views for velocity, oxygen, fuel, heat, and soot. | Closed-cell reaction mass/energy balance; no reaction without oxidizer; inert transport conservation; pressure residual; fixed-step consistency. |
| 2. Flow around fuel | Real boiler geometry, moving coal polygons, grate inlet, and flue outlet. | No transport through sealed walls; measured inlet/outlet balance; blocked draft reduces burning; bellows changes air velocity and oxygen supply. |
| 3. Material feedback | Drying, gas release, char oxidation, interior heating, and heat delivered to the vessel. | A wet piece lights later than an otherwise identical dry piece; disconnected cold fuel stays cold without heat transfer; total heat respects consumed fuel; save/reload preserves reservoirs. |
| 4. Material variety and water | At least coal and wood as distinct materials, plus cooling and evaporation of actual water. | Different burn curves arise from geometry/properties; water mass lost equals vapor added; heat removed matches warming and phase change; remaining hot fuel can reignite when conditions permit. |
| 5. Presentation and performance | Reaction-driven flames and smoke with measured quality tiers. | Browser shader/runtime checks; GPU timestamps where supported; whole-game frame-time and memory measurements with water, smoke, and slimes active; compact-screen readability; device-loss and fallback checks. |

Use a reference CPU implementation for small conservation cases and compare the GPU result within explicit tolerances. Test grid refinement and several render frame rates without changing simulation time. A visual comparison alone cannot establish correct chemistry or coupling. FDS reference cases can later assess plume and thermal behavior, but a two-dimensional game model should not be presented as a validated three-dimensional engineering simulator.

The first playable milestone is an oxygen-limited coal fire with a real draft and conserved heat feeding the boiler. The same interface then admits wood, paper, and liquid fuels without replacing the gas solver. Thin flame-front tracking, richer radiation, structural breakage, and spreading world fires are later extensions with separate costs and validation needs.
