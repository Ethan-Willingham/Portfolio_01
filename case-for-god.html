Can You Argue Your Way to God?

Pascal offers a wager on salvation. C.S. Lewis offers arguments from moral obligation and the claims of Jesus. The difficult part is deciding which premises to accept.

Pascal and C.S. Lewis / 9 min read

Scroll

The question

What counts as a reason to believe?

Pascal's wager ends with advice to take holy water and have masses said. His skeptical reader accepts the calculation but still cannot make himself believe. Pascal recommends the practices of believers, hoping that habit will change what argument has failed to change.

Pascal asks whether seeking faith can be sensible before you know whether Christianity is true. C.S. Lewis gives reasons for thinking it is true, first through our sense of right and wrong, then through claims the Gospels attribute to Jesus. Each argument needs assumptions that the reader may have reason to question.

Pascal

A finite life against an infinite gain

The wager appears in the Pensées, notes for a defense of Christianity left unfinished at Pascal's death in 1662 and published in 1670. The fragment's date of composition is uncertain. Editors arrange the papers differently: it is Brunschvicg 233, Lafuma 418, or Sellier 680. Its opening, Infini rien, means infinity, nothing. Publication and manuscript history

The wager starts from the assumption that reason cannot settle whether God exists. Pascal turns to the consequences of a choice: if Christian faith can bring eternal happiness, how should an uncertain person live?

Yes; but you must wager. It is not optional. You are embarked.Pensées, Brunschvicg 233, translated by W.F. Trotter

You can suspend judgment about God's existence. You still have to decide how to spend your life. Pascal treats failing to seek faith as a choice with consequences too. His argument recommends action under uncertainty; it supplies no evidence that God exists.

The calculation uses expected utility: the value of each outcome weighed by its probability. Any positive chance of infinite happiness seems to outweigh a finite cost. Pascal begins with equal chances, then extends the reasoning to much worse odds. His final argument needs a positive probability, not 50 percent. Two possible answers needn't be equally likely.

A finite cost can still consume a life. Religious commitments affect money and relationships as well as time. Pascal first describes the loss as nothing, then lets his reader object that the stake may be too large. He answers with the infinite reward and the virtues he expects Christian practice to produce.

Can practice produce belief?

Accepting the wager has not made Pascal's reader a believer. Pascal recommends following people once in the same position, participating in worship and reducing the passions he thinks obstruct faith. He expects this to produce sincere belief.

Trotter renders the French vous abêtira as deadening one's acuteness. Pascal believes the skeptical reader needs to change, and his reader objects to this part of the remedy. If the doubt is warranted, training oneself out of it would be another cost. French manuscript and revisions

The assumptions

Which promise should the wager use?

The table contains only Pascal's God and no God. Different religions make different demands and promises. A possibility of eternal happiness cannot select Christianity if a rival path offers it too. A hypothetical deity could reward honest doubt. The wager needs an independent reason to favor one account of salvation. Pascal's wider defense of Christianity matters here.

Every imagined deity needn't receive equal probability. A defender can use evidence or religious experience to distinguish the candidates. Those judgments would then help decide the choice; the payoff table alone would not do it. The many-gods objection and replies

Infinity makes choices harder to compare

Suppose you flip a coin and seek faith only if it lands heads. That strategy still has a positive chance of infinite gain and the same infinite expected utility as seeking faith directly. The basic rule cannot distinguish them. Adding infinite rewards and infinite punishments can produce an undefined calculation instead.

Philosophers have proposed other ways of comparing infinite prospects. Those defenses require further rules. Repeating that infinity is larger than any finite number does not resolve the comparison. Infinite payoffs and alternative decision rules

What would count as winning?

The wager also needs a connection between its recommended action and salvation. Can an initially self-interested search become sincere? Would God accept it? Pascal proposes a route through habit and humility; he has not established a reward for calculated assent.

Lewis

Why does an obligation feel binding?

Lewis begins with a broken promise. Someone says, Come on, you promised. The reply usually offers an excuse. Both people seem to recognize a standard that the promise-breaker has failed to meet. Lewis calls this the moral law: a standard for how we ought to behave, including when we would rather do something else.

This is the opening of Mere Christianity, published in 1952 after four series of BBC talks broadcast from 1941 to 1944. Lewis was then a fellow and tutor in English at Magdalen College, Oxford. He became a professor at Cambridge in 1954. His first broadcast was on August 6, 1941, after the main Blitz. The broadcast and college records

The title names his attempt to explain central Christian beliefs shared across denominations. His preface compares that common ground to a hall, with the churches as rooms opening from it. He expects a believer eventually to enter a room. The proposed common ground is still Lewis's selection.

Lewis distinguishes wanting to help from thinking you ought to help. Hearing a cry for help, you might want both to rescue someone and to stay out of danger. The moral judgment can tell you to act on the weaker desire. He also argues that calling one society morally better than another requires a standard beyond whatever either society happens to approve. Learning a standard from other people, he adds, does not by itself make it an invention: we learn arithmetic that way too. Book I, chapter 2

His further inference is that this authority points toward a power behind the universe, something more like a mind than matter. Conscience gives him access to what that power asks of us. Lewis limits the conclusion in Book I, chapters 4 and 5: he has not yet established the Christian God, or even a personal God capable of forgiving us.

Origin and authority are different questions

Naturalistic accounts explain moral judgment through evolved capacities, learning, and reflection. A system that evaluates competing desires can be part of a mind that developed naturally; it need not be a supernatural judge. Shared human needs can also help explain why some moral expectations recur. Explaining conscience's development leaves open whether its judgments are right. Evolutionary explanations and their limits

Dismissing a judgment just because it has an evolutionary history confuses its origin with its justification. Our capacity for reasoning has a history too. Conversely, feeling an obligation intensely does not establish that its source is divine. People can feel bound by incompatible duties, including duties other people reasonably reject.

Even an objective moral truth, one that holds regardless of approval, need not be a command. Secular moral realists accept such truths while disputing the need for a divine lawgiver. Lewis's argument must defend that further step. His observation about obligation is worth examining, but the existence of an ought does not settle what makes it binding. Theistic and secular accounts of moral authority

The trilemma

What did Jesus claim about himself?

Lewis next challenges anyone who accepts that Jesus claimed to be divine while judging him merely a great moral teacher. In Book II, chapter 3, he points to Jesus forgiving sins, claiming to have always existed, and claiming authority to judge the world. Lewis reads these as claims belonging to God, not ordinary human wisdom.

Either this man was, and is, the Son of God: or else a madman or something worse.Mere Christianity, Book II, chapter 3, The Shocking Alternative

The familiar summary is liar, lunatic, or lord: a trilemma, a choice among three alternatives. Lewis thinks the character of Jesus in the Gospels rules out deception and delusion. That assessment needs checking, along with the claim that Jesus said and meant what Lewis attributes to him.

01

Could the claim have been dishonest?

Lewis regards calculated deception as inconsistent with Jesus's character. That is an assessment of the Gospel portrait. Good moral teaching does not logically rule out a teacher's dishonesty, and suffering does not prove every claim a person makes. This explanation would need historical evidence, just as the alternatives do.

Lewis's conclusion continues in Book II, chapter 4, The Perfect Penitent.

02

Could he have been sincerely mistaken?

A sincere false belief about one's religious role does not by itself establish severe mental illness or invalidate everything one teaches. Lewis needs to show why this particular error would have those consequences. Conversely, observing intelligence or composure cannot rule out every delusion. Neither conclusion is a diagnosis we can responsibly make from a few Gospel scenes.

03

What would establish the conclusion?

If Jesus made an unambiguous claim to be God, and it is true, then Lewis's conclusion follows. Eliminating other explanations could support that conclusion too, but the alternatives must be adequately described and actually ruled out. Finding two labels unappealing does not finish the argument.

Stephen T. Davis's defense of the argument

04

Do the texts report the historical claim?

The Gospels could preserve, interpret, or develop a claim in different ways. Their authors' understanding of Jesus is not automatically a transcript of his words. The familiar legend objection asks whether later belief shaped the account; it need not deny that Jesus existed. A divine portrait in a text and a historical speaker's intention are separate questions.

Dale Martin on reconstructing the historical Jesus

The issue is not confined to John's explicit language about Jesus and the Father. Mark also presents him forgiving sins, in Mark 2:1 to 12. The scene raises a question about divine authority, but interpreting that authority as identity with God is a further step. John 10:30 to 38 raises the relationship differently. Choosing and interpreting these passages is part of the case for divinity.

The argument challenges a reader who accepts Lewis's interpretation and the accuracy of the records, then sets Jesus's claims aside as unimportant to his teaching. A reader who disputes either premise has raised a question the three labels cannot answer.

Texts, historical records, and objections
  1. Pascal's text. Pensées, fragment 233, W.F. Trotter. Read the whole fragment for its shifts from no loss to a finite stake, from equal chances to worse odds, and from argument to religious practice.
  2. Pascal's papers and publication. Manuscript reconstruction, Lafuma 418 to 426 / Sellier 680; the Port-Royal edition of 1670. The editors distinguish the wager from adjoining notes, including one on habit and fear of hell. Treating all those materials as one fixed modern argument obscures the manuscript.
  3. The wager's disputed assumptions. Alan Hájek, Pascal's Wager, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, especially sections 4, 5, and 7. Covers finite costs, probability, rival religions, infinite utilities, and voluntary belief. The matrix above is a simplified model, not Pascal's own diagram.
  4. Lewis's text. Mere Christianity, preface; Book I, chapters 1 to 5; Book II, chapters 3 and 4. PBS reproduces Book I, chapter 2. These are parts of a larger defense of Christian doctrine, not the entire book's case.
  5. Lewis's work and broadcasts. Magdalen College, Oxford; Magdalene College, Cambridge archives; the official Lewis site's records of the first broadcast, the 1944 talks, and their wartime context.
  6. Morality and naturalistic explanation. Morality and Evolutionary Biology and Moral Arguments for the Existence of God, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. Distinguish how moral judgment develops from whether moral truths exist and what could ground obligations.
  7. The trilemma debate. Daniel Howard-Snyder, Was Jesus Mad, Bad, or God?... or Merely Mistaken?, and Stephen T. Davis's reply, Faith and Philosophy 21(4), 2004. A published disagreement, rather than a consensus that believers or skeptics can simply claim.
  8. Gospels and history. Dale Martin, Yale, The Gospel of Mark, Johannine Christianity, and The Historical Jesus. Historical-critical readings distinguish the authors' theological portraits from independently established history.
