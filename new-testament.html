01 The whole collection

The New Testament, in plain English

The oldest books in the New Testament are letters. By the time the Gospels were written, churches were already reading Paul.

The New Testament contains twenty-seven books. Four Gospels tell Jesus’s life, death, and resurrection. Acts follows his followers from Jerusalem toward Rome. Twenty-one letters address churches and individuals. Revelation ends with judgment and a world in which death is gone. The order helps you follow the story, but Paul wrote his earliest letters before any of these Gospels.

Jesus belongs to the Jewish world of Roman-ruled Galilee and Judea. He and his first followers draw on Israel’s scriptures, worship Israel’s God, and argue about Torah, Jewish law. The Gospels introduce him alongside John the Baptist, a Jewish preacher calling people to repentance. Their language of Messiah, covenant, sacrifice, Passover, and resurrection comes from this setting. Jews were already disagreeing about how to live faithfully under Roman rule; the Gospels’ disputes are part of that world.

All four accounts lead to Jesus’s crucifixion under Pontius Pilate and the announcement that he has risen. They tell much of the rest differently. Matthew, Mark, and Luke share enough wording and sequence to be called the Synoptic Gospels, meaning they can be viewed together. Most scholars think Matthew and Luke used Mark, alongside other material. Their agreement therefore isn’t three independent confirmations of every event. John has a different arrangement and gives Jesus longer speeches. The audiences behind each book have to be reconstructed.

The shortest and probably earliest

Mark

Jesus arrives as an adult, announces God’s kingdom, heals and casts out demons, and repeatedly predicts suffering that his disciples cannot accept. The ending used in modern critical editions stops at 16:8: the tomb is empty, a messenger says Jesus has risen, and the women flee in fear before any risen appearance is narrated.

The teacher and Jewish Messiah

Matthew

Matthew gives Jesus a genealogy through David and Abraham, a virgin birth under Herod, and five large blocks of teaching. Jesus fulfills Israel’s scriptures, says he fulfills the law, forms a church, and ends by sending disciples to every nation.

The first of two volumes

Luke

Luke includes Mary’s story, Jesus’s birth in Bethlehem, and sustained attention to poverty, prayer, and the Spirit. Its Jesus begins with good news for the poor and ends by opening the scriptures to his followers. Acts is volume two, carrying the same story through Peter, Paul, and the gentile mission.

The Word made flesh

John

John begins before creation, calls Jesus the divine Word, and emphasizes signs and long conversations. Its chronology and voice differ sharply from the other three. Jesus speaks openly about who he is, washes his disciples’ feet, and makes self-giving love the mark of belonging to him.

Acts begins with Jesus’s ascension and the arrival of the Holy Spirit at Pentecost. His followers share resources, preach, meet resistance, and admit gentiles, people who aren’t Jews. Must these converts be circumcised and keep Jewish law? Acts 15 describes a meeting in Jerusalem that decides against requiring circumcision. Paul’s own account in Galatians 2 shows a sharper quarrel over whom Jewish believers would eat with. The expansion was also an argument about belonging. Acts ends with Paul under guard in Rome, still preaching; it doesn’t tell his death.

Paul’s letters show churches disagreeing about meals, sex, money, leadership, spiritual gifts, and Jesus’s return. At Corinth, members sue one another and leave poorer people hungry at the communal supper. In Galatia, circumcision threatens to divide the movement. At Thessalonica, believers worry that their dead will miss Jesus’s return. Paul answers these questions through Christ’s death and resurrection.

The other letters widen the picture. Hebrews explains Jesus through priesthood, sacrifice, and covenant. James asks what faith is worth if a hungry person stays hungry. First Peter addresses believers facing hostility. First John links knowing God with loving others. The Pastoral Letters, First and Second Timothy and Titus, set rules for church offices and households; many scholars think later followers wrote them in Paul’s name. Second Peter answers people asking why Jesus still hasn’t returned.

Revelation addresses seven churches in Roman Asia, in what is now western Turkey. It is an apocalypse, a revelation delivered through visions. Its beasts, Babylon, and slaughtered Lamb draw on Jewish prophetic imagery to portray empire, judgment, and Jesus’s victory. The first readers lived under Roman power, though scholars debate how much organized persecution they faced. Christians have since read the visions as past events, recurring patterns, or future events. The book ends with a city descending from heaven and God dwelling with humanity on a renewed earth.

For a first pass, read one Gospel from beginning to end before combining it with the others. Mark is the shortest. Luke followed by Acts gives a continuous account of Jesus and the movement after him. First Corinthians then shows what an early church was actually arguing about. Revelation will make more sense with Daniel and the prophets nearby.

02 History and transmission

What can actually be known

What the evidence can establish

Historians broadly agree that Jesus existed, taught within first-century Judaism, and was crucified under Pontius Pilate. Paul’s undisputed letters, mostly dated to the 50s CE, are our earliest surviving Christian writings. The books in this collection were written in Greek. First Corinthians 15 preserves a confession about Jesus’s death and resurrection that Paul says he received from others.

The Gospel narratives don’t name their authors. The familiar names come from early church attribution, whose reliability scholars debate; John appeals to the testimony of an unnamed beloved disciple. A common dating puts Mark around 70 CE, Matthew and Luke around 80 to 90, and John around 90 to 100. Earlier and later dates are argued, especially for Luke and Acts. Seven letters are widely accepted as Paul’s: Romans, First and Second Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, First Thessalonians, and Philemon. The six others bearing his name have different degrees of disputed authorship. Hebrews names no author.

No original manuscript survives. Thousands of Greek copies have been catalogued. Most are medieval and contain only part of the collection. Papyrus 52 preserves a few lines of John. It is often dated to the second century from its handwriting, but that method cannot fix a narrow date; published arguments allow a date into the third century. Papyrus 46, usually dated around 200, preserves much of an early collection of Paul’s letters and Hebrews.

What remains a reconstruction

Paul names people he says saw the risen Jesus, including Peter and James. This is evidence that resurrection testimony circulated early. The accounts differ over what happened, and historians disagree about what can be inferred from them. A manuscript date can establish when someone copied the claim. It cannot establish that God raised Jesus.

The canon, the list of books treated as scripture, took centuries to settle. Four Gospels and collections of Paul’s letters circulated by the second century, alongside other Christian writings. Churches disagreed over Hebrews, James, Revelation, and works such as the Shepherd of Hermas. Athanasius’s Easter letter of 367 gives the first surviving list matching the familiar twenty-seven exactly. Other churches continued to disagree.

Copies differ in spelling, word order, and sometimes whole passages. Most differences have little effect on translation; some matter. The two earliest surviving nearly complete Greek Bibles end Mark at 16:8, and early witnesses to John lack the woman caught in adultery (7:53 to 8:11). The familiar closing praise in Matthew’s Lord’s Prayer and the explicit Trinitarian phrase in First John 5:7 also have uneven or late manuscript support. Modern translations usually mark these differences in notes or brackets.

How safely did this reach us?

Thousands of copies let editors compare the Greek wording. The oldest copies are incomplete, and some readings remain uncertain.

Date, wording, and interpretation are separate questions.

Early witnesses Early, incomplete

The earliest fragments are generally assigned to the second or third century from handwriting. Substantial papyri survive from around 200, and much fuller Bibles from the fourth century. No surviving copy is an original, and dates for the earliest fragments remain approximate.

Greek wording Strong, qualified

Greek manuscripts, early translations, and ancient quotations let editors compare readings across centuries. Most wording is well supported. For some passages, the earliest recoverable reading remains disputed; manuscript abundance does not guarantee certainty at every verse.

English meaning Contested

Many passages translate straightforwardly. Others depend on what words such as faith, flesh, righteousness, and justification mean in context. Clear Greek wording can still support competing theological readings.

Based on the Center for the Study of New Testament Manuscripts, the Institute for New Testament Textual Research, and the composition and canon sources below. It measures access to the text, not whether its theological claims are true.

03 Expand the ideas

Fifteen central ideas

Each entry connects a teaching with Christian practice, then gives passages from the American Standard Version of 1901.

01Jesus announces God’s kingdom and calls for repentance.The opening message

In Mark, Jesus begins by announcing that God’s kingdom is near. Kingdom here means God’s rule, present in healings and forgiveness but also awaited in the future. He calls people to repent, to turn from wrongdoing and change how they live. The prayer “thy kingdom come” keeps that future hope in ordinary worship.

In practice Confession, penance, conversion preaching, and daily self-examination all return to repentance. Churches differ over the forms, but changing one’s conduct remains part of the call.

PassagesASV, 1901

The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe in the gospel.Mark 1:15

Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done, as in heaven, so on earth.Matthew 6:10

02Jesus is Messiah, Son of God, Lord, and Word made flesh.The identity at the center

Messiah, or Christ, means anointed one and carries Jewish expectations of a deliverer or king. Son of God can be a royal title as well as a claim about Jesus’s relationship to God. The books use these titles differently. John calls Jesus the eternal Word made flesh; Philippians pictures every knee bowing to him. Later creeds draw on these passages, but their technical definitions took centuries of argument.

In practice Catholic, Orthodox, and most Protestant churches confess Jesus as divine and human. The Nicene Creed affirms his divinity and incarnation; Chalcedon later defines his divine and human natures more precisely. These traditions understand Father, Son, and Spirit as the one God, the Trinity. That doctrine draws on the New Testament without appearing there as a finished creed.

PassagesASV, 1901

In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. … And the Word became flesh, and dwelt among us …John 1:1, 14

that in the name of Jesus every knee should bow … and that every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.Philippians 2:10-11

03The cross and resurrection are the center of the good news.Death, forgiveness, and new life

First Corinthians 15 says Christ died for sins, was buried, and was raised. Paul presents this as teaching he received. The books explain the cross through several images, including sacrifice, ransom, reconciliation, and victory over hostile powers. Paul’s resurrection claim concerns a transformed body and the future raising of the dead. It isn’t just a claim that Jesus’s influence continued.

In practice Easter, Holy Week, Sunday worship, baptism, and funerals return to death and resurrection. Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant accounts of how the cross saves differ, even where they share these celebrations.

PassagesASV, 1901

Christ died for our sins according to the scriptures; and that he was buried; and that he hath been raised on the third day according to the scriptures;1 Corinthians 15:3-4

that like as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life.Romans 6:4

04Salvation is grace received by faith, and living faith produces works.Gift and response

Paul says a person is justified, put right with God, by faith apart from works of the law (Romans 3:28). Circumcision and gentile inclusion are central to his argument; scholars disagree about how broadly “works of the law” reaches. James uses Abraham to argue that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone (2:24). Christians have long argued over whether the two writers use these terms differently or disagree. Ephesians brings gift and conduct together: salvation is by grace, and the saved are created for good works.

In practice Protestant traditions stress justification by faith; Catholic and Orthodox traditions also speak of grace enabling a person’s cooperation and transformation. The Catholic-Lutheran Joint Declaration records substantial agreement on grace and good works. It does not settle every Protestant dispute or speak for Orthodox churches.

PassagesASV, 1901

for by grace have ye been saved through faith; and that not of yourselves, [it is] the gift of God; not of works, that no man should glory. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works, …Ephesians 2:8-10

Even so faith, if it have not works, is dead in itself.James 2:17

05Love of God and neighbor sums up the law.The great commandments

Jesus joins commands from Deuteronomy and Leviticus: love God and love your neighbor as yourself. These are Jewish teachings before they are Christian ones. In Luke’s Good Samaritan story, the person who helps the injured traveler is a Samaritan, a member of a community with which Jews had religious and social tensions. John gives disciples another measure: love one another as Jesus has loved them.

In practice These commands shape charitable giving, pastoral care, and Christian arguments about justice. Churches regularly appeal to the same command while disagreeing about what it requires in a particular case.

PassagesASV, 1901

Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. … Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself.Matthew 22:37, 39

A new commandment I give unto you, that ye love one another; even as I have loved you, … By this shall all men know that ye are my disciples, …John 13:34-35

06Jesus commands love of enemies and forgiveness.The costly extension of love

Jesus commands his followers to love enemies and pray for persecutors. Matthew connects this with God giving sun and rain to good and bad people alike. The same Gospel makes forgiveness of others a condition of receiving forgiveness. The command is severe, and Christian traditions differ over how it applies to self-defense, punishment, and war.

In practice Peace churches, including Quakers and Mennonites, reject participation in war. Catholic and many Protestant traditions permit force under just-war conditions. Forgiveness and reconciliation also shape confession and worship; their use to pressure abuse victims is a failure that a description of the ideal should not hide.

PassagesASV, 1901

but I say unto you, love your enemies, and pray for them that persecute you; that ye may be sons of your Father who is in heaven: …Matthew 5:44-45

For if ye forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But if ye forgive not men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses.Matthew 6:14-15

07The poor and excluded receive special attention.Poverty and wealth

Luke gives poverty unusual prominence: Mary sings of the hungry being fed and the rich sent away empty, and Jesus announces good news to the poor. The demands reach beyond sympathy. He tells followers to sell possessions and give alms (Luke 12:33), and tells a rich ruler to give his wealth to the poor (18:22). James condemns favoring a rich visitor over a poor one. Christians disagree about how far these commands require voluntary poverty or redistribution.

In practice Almsgiving, food banks, shelters, and mutual aid put these teachings into action. Monastic poverty and liberation theology, which reads Christian faith through the lives of the oppressed, make stronger demands on property and social arrangements.

PassagesASV, 1901

The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, Because he anointed me to preach good tidings to the poor: He hath sent me to proclaim release to the captives, …Luke 4:18

Inasmuch as ye did it unto one of these my brethren, [even] these least, ye did it unto me.Matthew 25:40

08Prayer begins with “Our Father” and seeks God’s will.The Lord’s Prayer

The Lord’s Prayer asks for God’s kingdom, daily bread, forgiveness, and deliverance from evil. Matthew says “our Father”: even an individual praying it speaks as part of a group. Jesus also prays in distress at Gethsemane, asking to escape suffering while submitting to God’s will. The letters add thanksgiving, prayer for others, and requests for help.

In practice Christians say the Lord’s Prayer in worship and at home. Prayer ropes, rosaries, daily prayer services, and spontaneous prayer give different forms to the habit. Many of these forms developed long after the New Testament.

PassagesASV, 1901

Our Father who art in heaven, Hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come. Thy will be done, as in heaven, so on earth. Give us this day our daily bread.Matthew 6:9-11

In nothing be anxious; but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known unto God.Philippians 4:6

09Baptism joins a person to Christ’s death, life, and people.Water and belonging

Baptism marks entry into the movement. Matthew commands baptism in the name of Father, Son, and Holy Spirit; Acts often describes baptism in Jesus’s name. Paul explains it as sharing Christ’s death and resurrection. The books connect baptism with forgiveness, the Spirit, and membership in a community, without answering every later question about how the ritual works.

In practice Catholic, Orthodox, and most Protestant churches use water and the Trinitarian formula. They disagree about infant baptism, immersion, and whether baptism itself brings new spiritual life. Oneness Pentecostals use Jesus’s name; Quakers and the Salvation Army generally do not practice water baptism.

PassagesASV, 1901

Go ye therefore, and make disciples of all the nations, baptizing them into the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit:Matthew 28:19

Or are ye ignorant that all we who were baptized into Christ Jesus were baptized into his death? We were buried therefore with him through baptism unto death: …Romans 6:3-4

10The Lord’s Supper joins bread and cup with Christ’s death.Bread, cup, and communion

Paul records the bread-and-cup tradition before the Gospels were written. Jesus calls the bread his body and the cup the new covenant in his blood, and tells his followers to repeat the meal. Paul rebukes Corinthians whose communal supper leaves some hungry while others get drunk. His account connects remembering Christ with how the participants treat one another.

In practice Catholics celebrate the Eucharist at Mass, Orthodox Christians at the Divine Liturgy, and Protestants use names such as Communion and the Lord’s Supper. They disagree over Christ’s presence, sacrifice, and who may receive. Frequency ranges from daily or weekly celebration to occasional services; some traditions omit the rite.

PassagesASV, 1901

This is my body, which is for you: this do in remembrance of me. … For as often as ye eat this bread, and drink the cup, ye proclaim the Lord’s death till he come.1 Corinthians 11:24-26

The cup of blessing which we bless, is it not a communion of the blood of Christ? The bread which we break, is it not a communion of the body of Christ?1 Corinthians 10:16

11The Holy Spirit gives life, power, gifts, and character.God present within the community

In Acts, the Holy Spirit empowers preaching and comes to gentiles as well as Jews. Paul describes gifts such as teaching, healing, and tongues, given for the community’s benefit. He also names the Spirit’s fruit: love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control. Extraordinary experiences and everyday conduct both belong to the account.

In practice Churches invoke the Spirit in prayer, baptism, and worship. Pentecostal and charismatic communities give tongues, prophecy, and healing a more prominent place. Other Protestants argue that some gifts were limited to the apostolic period.

PassagesASV, 1901

But ye shall receive power, when the Holy Spirit is come upon you: and ye shall be my witnesses …Acts 1:8

But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, meekness, self-control; …Galatians 5:22-23

12The church is one body with many necessary members.The church as a body

Paul compares the church to a body whose parts need one another. People treated as weaker deserve special honor, and a gift matters because it helps the others. The Greek ekklesia, usually translated church, means an assembly. In these books it can refer to a household gathering, believers in a city, or Christ’s people together.

In practice Congregations gather for worship, teaching, meals, and mutual care. Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant traditions disagree about bishops, papal authority, and congregational government. Those differences affect who can teach, lead worship, and make binding decisions.

PassagesASV, 1901

For as the body is one, and hath many members … so also is Christ. For in one Spirit were we all baptized into one body, …1 Corinthians 12:12-13

Now ye are the body of Christ, and severally members thereof.1 Corinthians 12:27

13Holiness reaches the body, money, sex, speech, power, and work.Everyday conduct

Paul calls bodies living sacrifices and asks believers to be changed by the renewing of their minds. The letters apply faith to sexual conduct, speech, work, money, and meals. Jesus warns that wealth can compete with loyalty to God. These demands also reach family life: his restrictions on divorce in Mark 10 and Matthew 19 remain a major source of disagreement among churches.

In practice Fasting, confession, sexual ethics, honest work, and generosity give these teachings daily consequences. Churches differ over divorce and remarriage, celibacy, property, and which disciplines bind all believers.

PassagesASV, 1901

I beseech you therefore, brethren, by the mercies of God, to present your bodies a living sacrifice, holy, acceptable to God, [which is] your spiritual service. And be not fashioned according to this world: but be ye transformed by the renewing of your mind, …Romans 12:1-2

But be ye doers of the word, and not hearers only, deluding your own selves.James 1:22

14Christians are sent to witness among every people.Good news moving outward

Matthew ends by sending disciples to all nations. Acts follows preaching, baptism, and new congregations across the eastern Mediterranean. Paul describes his work as an appeal for reconciliation with God. The message is meant to cross boundaries, though the books don’t supply a single later model for organizing missions.

In practice Preaching, Bible translation, and founding congregations continue this work. Missions have also operated alongside colonial rule and coercion. Churches now commonly insist on voluntary conversion.

PassagesASV, 1901

Go ye therefore, and make disciples of all the nations … teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I commanded you: and lo, I am with you always, …Matthew 28:19-20

We are ambassadors therefore on behalf of Christ, as though God were entreating by us: …2 Corinthians 5:20

15Christian hope is resurrection, judgment, and a remade creation.The future of bodies and the world

Paul expects the dead to be raised and mortal bodies changed. Revelation ends with a new heaven and earth and a city descending from heaven, where God dwells with humanity. These passages are the basis for the Christian hope of bodily resurrection and a renewed creation. Other passages speak of being with Christ after death; how these fit together is disputed.

In practice The Nicene Creed ends with resurrection of the dead and the life of the world to come. Christian funerals use that hope while mourning the person who died. Churches disagree over the rapture, the millennium, the state between death and resurrection, and the sequence of final events.

in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trump: for the trumpet shall sound, and the dead shall be raised incorruptible, and we shall be changed. For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality.1 Corinthians 15:52-53

And I saw a new heaven and a new earth: … and death shall be no more; neither shall there be mourning, nor crying, nor pain, any more: … Behold, I make all things new.Revelation 21:1, 4-5

Where Christians converge

This selection reflects Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and many Protestant teachings. It does not cover all Christian traditions. Non-Trinitarian churches disagree about Jesus’s identity, some traditions omit baptism or Communion, and even churches sharing a creed disagree about how salvation works. Fifteen entries are a selection, not an official count of the book’s ideas.

04 Do not skip

Hell, slavery, women, sexuality, anti-Judaism, delay, contradiction, salvation

These passages have shaped church rules and harmed people. Their wording and the arguments over it belong beside the more familiar teachings.

Hell has several names and images

English “hell” can translate Gehenna, Hades, or Tartarus; Revelation adds a lake of fire. Matthew 25:46 places eternal punishment beside eternal life. The dominant traditional reading is conscious punishment without end. Other Christians read the destruction passages as final extinction, or passages about reconciliation as grounds for eventual restoration. Each reading faces textual difficulties. The books do not give one consistent map of the afterlife.

The letters regulate slavery

Ephesians 6:5 tells enslaved people to obey masters. First Peter 2:18 includes harsh masters. Paul sends Onesimus back to Philemon and asks that he be received as a beloved brother (Philemon 12, 16). Whether Paul expects his release is debated; he does not explicitly demand it, much less abolish slavery as an institution. Abolitionists appealed to equality in Christ, especially Galatians 3:28. Defenders of slavery appealed to the commands to obey. Both arguments drew on passages actually present in the collection.

Women teach and are told not to teach

Women support Jesus’s work, host churches, and prophesy. Priscilla helps teach Apollos (Acts 18:26); Paul commends Phoebe and names Junia, whom many scholars understand as a woman counted among the apostles (Romans 16). First Timothy 2:12 forbids a woman to teach or exercise authority over a man. First Corinthians 14:34-35 tells women to be silent, though chapter 11 assumes women pray and prophesy. Some scholars argue that the silence verses were added later; others explain the tension through context. Churches disagree over whether the restrictions are local or permanent.

Same-sex acts and ancient categories

Romans 1:26-27 condemns same-sex acts in an argument about idolatry and desire. First Corinthians 6:9 uses malakoi and arsenokoitai, words whose precise scope is disputed. The traditional reading prohibits same-sex sexual relations generally. Affirming readings argue that exploitation, status, and excess matter to the ancient setting, and that the texts do not address modern marriages between equal partners. Romans is harder to limit to exploitation alone than the disputed vocabulary in Corinthians. Applying either passage to modern relationships requires an argument about which features of the ancient acts carry over.

Anti-Jewish passages and their reception

Jesus, Paul, and the first disciples were Jews. That does not remove the problem of John’s hostile uses of “the Jews,” Matthew 27:25’s crowd accepting bloodguilt, or First Thessalonians 2:14-16. The settings and targets of these passages are debated; some reflect conflicts among Jews and others a movement increasingly separating from Jewish communities. Later Christians used them against Jews collectively, helping justify exclusion and violence. Romans 11 rejects the claim that God has rejected Israel and warns gentile believers against boasting. Those warnings did not prevent the later uses of the other passages.

The expected return was delayed

Mark 13:30 says “this generation” will not pass before the events described. First Thessalonians 4:15 speaks of “we that are alive” at the Lord’s coming. Many scholars read these and Revelation’s “the time is at hand” as evidence of an expectation of an imminent end. Other readings connect parts of the sayings with Jerusalem’s destruction or distinguish different events. Second Peter 3 explicitly answers people asking what happened to the promised coming. The delay was already a question within the collection.

The accounts disagree about events

Matthew and Luke give different genealogies and birth narratives. Judas hangs himself in Matthew 27:5; Acts 1:18 describes a fatal fall in which his body bursts open. The resurrection accounts differ over people, places, and sequences. On the usual reading, John puts Jesus’s crucifixion before the Passover meal, while Matthew, Mark, and Luke place it after a Passover supper. Harmonizations have been proposed, including alternative calendar readings. Some are possible, but they add explanations that the accounts themselves do not give.

Can people outside Christianity be saved?

John 14:6 says no one comes to the Father except through Jesus; Acts 4:12 says salvation is found in no other name. First Timothy 2:4 says God desires everyone to be saved. Many Christians hold that explicit faith in Jesus is necessary. Others distinguish salvation through Christ from consciously knowing him. Catholic teaching, for example, allows salvation for people who do not know the Gospel through no fault of their own and respond to grace through conscience. Universalists hope or argue that everyone will eventually be reconciled. The texts do not simply teach that all religions are equivalent, and Christians disagree over how far their promises extend.

  19. The delayed return. The Cambridge study of the delay of the parousia in Luke and Acts documents the question as an internal problem early Christian authors interpreted rather than a modern objection invented from outside.
  20. Resurrection and historical method. Oxford’s The Historicity of Jesus’ Resurrection argues for the resurrection, offering a different assessment of what the historical evidence can establish from the Yale historical-Jesus lecture linked above.
  21. Exceptions to sacramental practice. The United Pentecostal Church International explains baptism in Jesus’s name. Quaker teaching and the Salvation Army’s statement on baptism and Communion describe inward participation without requiring the outward rites.
  22. Difficult passages. The references in the last section can be checked in the ASV texts of Matthew, Acts, Romans, Philemon, First Peter, First Timothy, First Thessalonians, Second Peter.
  23. Jewish context. Dale Martin’s Yale lecture on first-century Judaism explains the Roman and Jewish setting. The Gospel portraits and the discussion of gentile inclusion above should be read within that setting.
  24. Salvation outside the church. Catholic Catechism paragraphs 846 to 848 state the qualified allowance described here. The Lausanne Covenant, linked above, represents a more restrictive evangelical account.
  25. Image. The site’s New Testament image is Rembrandt’s Return of the Prodigal Son, about 1668, held by the State Hermitage Museum and marked public domain.
