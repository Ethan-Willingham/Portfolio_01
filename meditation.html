An eight-week course, tested against medication

An eight-week mindfulness course performed comparably to escitalopram, an anxiety medication, in a randomized trial of 276 adults with anxiety disorders. The course included weekly classes, a day-long retreat, and 45 minutes of daily practice. Hoge 2023

MBSR, mindfulness-based stress reduction, combines meditation with instruction, movement, group discussion, and ways to handle stress outside the session. Its results belong to that package; they don't tell us what a few minutes on a phone will do.

Notice the breath at your nose or belly. Your attention moves to an unfinished email; you notice and return. You're practicing recognizing distraction and choosing where to attend. In a wider awareness practice, you might observe the thought itself: its words, the tension it brings, and how it changes.

Loving-kindness rehearses goodwill toward people. Tonglen uses images of suffering and relief. Prayer addresses God. Slow breathing changes the breath. The explorer keeps these different instructions visible.

The evidence

Which benefit, compared with what?

Madhav Goyal's review of 47 trials and 3,515 participants found moderate-quality evidence for small improvements in anxiety, depression, and pain from mindfulness programs. Comparison groups received activities such as health education or attention from an instructor. Goyal 2014 good

The review found no evidence that meditation outperformed treatments such as exercise or therapy. That doesn't establish equivalence: the direct comparisons were fewer, and some were too small to settle the question.

The anxiety trial met its prespecified threshold for MBSR to be acceptably close to medication. Participants knew their treatment; clinicians rating their symptoms did not. It supports MBSR as an option for those patients, with replication needed.

Brief exercises have evidence too. In a 2024 trial, a 15-minute body scan reduced immediate self-rated stress compared with an audiobook. Participants were mostly students without mental illness; the trial didn't test treatment of an anxiety disorder or lasting change. Sparacio 2024 good

Mindfulness-based cognitive therapy, or MBCT, combines meditation with cognitive therapy to help prevent another depressive episode. A meta-analysis of nine trials found a lower hazard of relapse over 60 weeks than comparison care, with a smaller advantage over active treatments. Participants' depression was in full or partial remission. Kuyken 2016 good Discuss whether the course fits your treatment plan with a clinician.

Unpleasant experiences need context

Researchers interviewed 96 people after mindfulness courses; 58% reported an unpleasant experience they attributed to meditation. Among 78 with relevant duration data, five reported a negative effect on functioning lasting more than a month. A brief disturbing thought and a lasting impairment are different outcomes. Without a nonmeditating comparison group, this study couldn't establish the excess risk. Britton 2021

In a 2026 analysis of app trials, distressing experiences occurred among people who had not completed guided meditations too. Those exposure groups weren't randomly assigned. Neither comparison can tell us how much distress meditation caused. Beloborodova 2026 limited

Anxiety, traumatic memories, and detachment have been documented in meditation research. Stop if a practice leaves you distressed or disconnected, and seek qualified help if that continues, even when the cause is uncertain. Lindahl 2017

What a paid method establishes

A Transcendental Meditation review found modest blood-pressure reductions, without establishing that a teacher-assigned mantra outperforms other words. Schneider 2022 The 2025 AHA/ACC guideline says TM may be a reasonable addition to lifestyle changes or medication, while noting the small samples and short duration of trials. AHA/ACC guideline, section 5.1 limited

The TM entry distinguishes its paid course from a free mantra exercise. Their similarity isn't evidence that they produce identical results.

Try it

A short attention exercise

  1. Find a supported position.

    Sit comfortably, eyes open if you prefer. Set a short timer, perhaps five minutes.

  2. Choose a sensation.

    Notice your natural breath at the nose, chest, or belly. If that's uncomfortable, try your feet touching the floor or sounds in the room.

  3. Notice when attention leaves.

    You may start planning dinner. Once you realize it, return to the sensation. You don't need to finish the thought or criticize yourself.

  4. Finish and check how you feel.

    Look around before getting up. Distraction lets you practice returning; distress is a reason to pause or change the exercise.

This is a sample of focused attention. It doesn't reproduce MBSR or establish a five-minute dose for a clinical benefit.

Changing the breath

A small randomized study tested five minutes daily for a month. Cyclic sighing, a double inhale followed by a long exhale, improved reported positive mood more than mindfulness. It didn't establish an instant anxiety treatment or lasting change in resting heart rate or heart-rate variability. Balban 2023

In a separate trial of 400 people, slow breathing provided no extra mental-health benefit over equally guided breathing at a normal pace. Fincham 2023 limited The best pattern remains unsettled.

Choosing a course

Match the teaching to the aim

Your aimWhat to look for
Try meditation at homeA short breath, mantra, or body-scan exercise you can comfortably repeat
Help with ongoing anxiety or painA qualified clinical mindfulness instructor, alongside a discussion of other treatment options
Prevent another depressive episodeA clinician-led discussion of MBCT and your existing relapse-prevention plan
Practice within a faithA teacher in that tradition who explains its purpose as well as its method
Attend a retreatThe actual schedule, screening process, teacher access, and arrangements for leaving or getting help

For a home trial, notice whether you can return to a task, how you respond to irritation, and whether the exercise leaves you feeling worse. A calm sitting doesn't establish a change outside the session.

Clinical courses have evidence for particular outcomes in particular patients. How far that evidence carries into a different practice, or a different dose, is still a question. The studies don't test spiritual progress.
