Star Signs, X-Rayed

A birth chart can locate Jupiter without telling you whether you're lucky. Try the calculations, compare their conventions, and see what happens when a reading ignores your birthday.

How accurate is that about you?

This custom paragraph illustrates the Forer, or Barnum, effect. In Forer's 1949 paper, 39 students completed a test and then received identical, individually named sketches containing 13 statements, drawn largely from an astrology book. Their sketch ratings averaged 4.26 on a scale from 0 to 5. That demonstrates the weakness of personal recognition as a test of accuracy; it doesn't by itself test natal astrology.

House comparison

Houses divide the chart relative to the horizon. Whole sign uses the entire rising sign as house one; Equal starts at the rising degree. Both have ancient precedents. Placidus divides daily motion by time and was popularized in the seventeenth century. Highlighted entries change house when the rule changes.

What this calculator assumes

An ephemeris gives celestial positions over time. This one uses Paul Schlyter's simplified formulae. They omit corrections such as the travel time of light and small changes in Earth's orientation. Whole-degree readouts suit a demonstration; they don't certify which side of a boundary a planet occupies.

All body positions here are geocentric, viewed from Earth's center. In particular, the Moon's position from a specific place on Earth's surface can differ. The rising sign, or Ascendant, is the point of the ecliptic on the eastern horizon. An incorrect birth time or UTC offset can substantially change it.

City presets supply rounded coordinates and modern standard-time offsets. They don't look up daylight saving or historical time zones. Check the offset in force at birth. Unknown-time mode uses local noon, samples positions across that calendar date, and hides the Ascendant and houses. Sampling can flag changing signs; it cannot recover the birth instant.

Placidus becomes problematic near the polar circles. This implementation uses Equal above 66 degrees north or south, or if its iterative calculation fails to converge, and labels that substitution. Its house assignments use ecliptic longitude alone. They are an illustration of the conventions, not a full astrological house-position calculation. The Swiss Ephemeris manual describes the more complete calculations.

Signs and the sky

Three labels for the Sun

In the tropical zodiac, Aries begins at the March equinox and each sign spans 30 degrees of the ecliptic, the Sun's apparent annual path. Sidereal zodiacs also use equal signs, with a starting point tied to the stars. Their chosen offsets differ. The panel uses an approximate Fagan-Bradley offset, about 25 degrees today, rather than treating one sidereal convention as the uniquely correct sky.

Astronomical constellations are unequal regions. Under the IAU boundaries, the Sun's path crosses thirteen, including Ophiuchus. The panel's constellation label is only a fixed modern month-and-day guide. It ignores the entered year and time, so it cannot establish your birth constellation near an entry date.

Precession, the slow change in Earth's axis direction, shifts the equinox against the stars. That doesn't make a tropical astrologer's chart an accidentally outdated star map: the seasonal starting point is deliberate, and Ptolemy explicitly favored it. The fair question is whether the chosen coordinates predict something about a person.

The interpretations changed too. Ptolemy's Tetrabiblos, written in the second century CE, records planetary rulerships and qualities such as heat and moisture. It isn't the source of every modern description of an Aries or a Gemini. The panel labels its personality words as sample modern shorthand and keeps the historical records separate.

How the chart acquired its layers
  1. Late fifth century BCE
    Equal signs in Babylonia
    The uniform zodiac made calculation easier by dividing the celestial band into twelve equal parts. Its structure parallels a schematic year of twelve 30-day months. Historian John Steele traces it through astronomical and astrological tablets. Earlier constellation lists were a different system; the equal zodiac didn't simply shrink them to twelve.
  2. Second century CE
    An influential Greek synthesis
    Ptolemy, Book I, presents the Sun as heating and drying, Mars as excessively drying, and Jupiter as temperate. His explanations belong to an ancient physical theory. He also records inherited rulerships, rather than supplying a date when each present-day personality trait was invented.
  3. 1781 / 1846 / 1930
    New bodies enter the chart
    The discoveries of Uranus, Neptune, and Pluto gave later astrologers new objects to interpret. Their assigned meanings developed after discovery. Those dates are astronomical milestones, not moments when astrologers unanimously settled a meaning.
  4. 1930s
    Horoscopes become a mass-market column
    R. H. Naylor's feature on Princess Margaret appeared in the Sunday Express on August 24, 1930. Historian Nicholas Campion describes the move from birth-date forecasts toward familiar Sun-sign profiles. Astrology and published forecasts already existed; this was a change in audience and format.
  5. 1928 / 1930
    Astronomers standardize constellation boundaries
    The IAU approved Eugène Delporte's boundaries in 1928; they were published in 1930. They formalized regions for naming astronomical objects. They didn't move the Sun, add a new star pattern, or suddenly make tropical signs stop matching constellations.

Testing the prediction

Can a chart identify its person?

A full natal chart uses more information than a newspaper Sun sign. Testing only twelve birthday labels wouldn't fairly test that stronger claim. A useful experiment gives astrologers complete charts, hides identifying clues, and asks them to distinguish the people those charts describe.

In Shawn Carlson's 1985 experiment, astrologers helped design a blinded matching task. Each chart came with three California Personality Inventory profiles, one belonging to its subject. They expected at least 50 percent correct first choices. The result was 40 of 116, about 34 percent, close to the one-third chance rate.

That result counts against their claim under those conditions. It doesn't test every kind of astrology, and a personality inventory doesn't capture an entire life. Carlson also declined to draw a negative conclusion from the study's separate self-recognition test: participants had trouble identifying their own inventory profiles, which undermined that method.

The opening reading demonstrates another problem. A description can feel accurate while failing to distinguish you from anyone else. A conversation prompted by a horoscope may still be useful to its reader, but that usefulness doesn't establish that the planets supplied the information.

  • How did the matching test hide clues? blinded
    Names were replaced with codes. Charts omitted explicit birth dates and places, and competing inventory profiles were matched by sex. Advising astrologers approved the inventory as covering relevant traits. See the original paper's experimental design and double-blind procedures, pages 420 to 423.
  • What limits remain? scope
    The task used selected practitioners, a specific questionnaire, and the charts that were returned. Fewer participants completed the work than planned. It tests chart-to-profile matching without a consultation, so a claim about a different practice needs its own test. The limit doesn't turn this result into evidence that the matching claim worked.
