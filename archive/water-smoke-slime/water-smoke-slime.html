Water machines: progress

Updated October 8, 2026. The greedy cup is playable. Open the cup and choose Drop the slime to start the drain. Across three tests with different starting particle positions, 97.4%, 98.7% and 93.3% of the water above the intake reaches the lower tank within three simulation minutes. All particles are conserved. Measurements and limits.

The pipe walls exposed a fault in how pressure changes particle motion. Water could still move toward a closed wall, crowd against it and leave gaps in the column. The repaired transfer keeps that motion parallel to the wall. Air pressure also uses the measured size of each cavity, correcting a volume estimate that had drifted away from the actual gap.

All three cups hold before the drop. Lowering the fill or raising the bend prevents drainage after the same drop, in three tests each. The counts exclude the water that starts inside the tube and require passage through both the bend and the outlet. The cup keeps the same physical dimensions when the browser is resized.

Pressure and air-volume checks still fail during these runs. Contact corrections and local crowding remain, and smooth playback on every device is unverified. Two of the three release checks exceed the 95% delivery target. The third stops at 93.3%, leaving more water in the cup. The pressure model is still diagnostic.

Three engines, one box

The water flows around the same walls that hold the gel and deflect the smoke. Draw a wall and all three simulations see it. These are the engines from Sluice, my mining game, with the slime creatures reduced to their squishy material.

Pipes and trapped air

The cup uses the existing water particles. Drop the slime into the upper vessel and watch the lower tank fill. Instruments shows measured head, flow and sample age; pressure readings remain diagnostic.

Greedy cup
The low inlet leads up over a bend and down through the cup’s base. Below the bend, the cup holds its water. A rising level can flood that route and start a siphon, which drains until the inlet admits air. The University of Maryland’s apparatus shows the tube inside the central shaft.
How the simulation works
Water
Particles carry the liquid. A temporary grid exchanges their momentum and applies pressure, then sends them on their way. The MLS-MPM solver runs on WebGPU, adapted from saharan’s water demo.
Air
Machine mode adds a pressure solve while keeping the water particles. Room air sets zero gauge pressure. Each connected sealed pocket has one pressure at a fixed temperature, and compresses as water takes its space. Aanjaneya, Patkar and Fedkiw’s bubble model describes this uniform, isothermal approximation. Room pressure and sound speed are scaled for this toy; Instruments lists their current values. A pressure floor limits the modeled drop below room pressure, and column separation is still being tested. Airflow within a pocket, heat transfer and dissolved gas are omitted.
Smoke
A WebGL fluid solver moves velocity and dye through a grid, following Pavel Dobryakov’s work. The visible smoke is dye in that flow.
Slime
A mesh of elastic connections holds its volume while stretching and compressing. Sluice’s current material, skin contacts, and spring grip use XPBD on the CPU. Highlights attach to the deforming mesh. There are no creature brains or eyes here.

The instrument scale makes the box 30 inches wide and 1 inch deep. Its clock converts simulation time to seconds using Earth gravity. The energy display includes sampled water motion, height, pressure compression and available air work; its remainder includes numerical losses and sample error. Work done by a slime is not yet measured.

This is a game simulation tuned for interaction. The water, smoke, and gel exchange approximate boundary motion; they aren’t one unified physical solver.

Water needs WebGPU. Smoke needs WebGL. If either is unavailable, the other materials still work. The simulation pauses when you leave the tab or scroll it out of view.
