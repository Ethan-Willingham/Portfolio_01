Water machines: progress

Updated October 9, 2026. Air pressure now appears inside the sealed pockets. Gold means pressure above the room, rose means below; the small labels show the difference in psi. Room air and water stay clear. Use Air pressure beside the flow readout to hide or restore it. Water pressure remains available in Instruments.

The GPU can fall behind while the frame counter keeps climbing. A private scheduler brings the siphon's median queue delay down to 14 milliseconds and the fountain's to 20, from over a second in both. Both execute about 60 frames per second in these short trials. The cup reaches about 52; smaller batches reach 73 to 76 but advance the water more slowly. These are uncapped browser measurements, not proof of steady display FPS. Queue measurements and timing limits.

The scheduler still needs work before release. It can mishandle a settled pool, old elapsed time after a control change, and draws while paused. Very low slow-motion settings also expose an existing fault that can freeze the water. These trials leave the published simulation unchanged.

A private water-step revision passes all nine cup drainage and hold tests. On three seeds, it delivers 100.5%, 100.4% and 96.8% of the initially estimated drainable amount, with no rim spills and no lost particles. The first two also drain some water below that estimate. A version that reduces the work in crowded water matches the short tests, but its longer trials deliver 100.7%, 99.6% and 93.7%. The last misses the 95% target. Its two playback trials average 45.4 and 53 FPS. Both revisions remain experimental; the published water solver stays unchanged. Cup checks, ordering tests and frame measurements.

One six-second Retina browser check records 59 to 60 FPS for the fountain and siphon, and 35 to 60 for the cup. These short runs do not establish steady 60 FPS or phone performance. The default particle and pressure buffer hashes match the prior release in three scenes, on three seeds, at three checkpoints. Air-view checks and native comparisons.

The pressure image and apparatus labels are reused between measurement samples. Paused scenes get an initial sample, and pressure controls update the picture while paused. A further boundary cache reduced CPU work, but paired browser runs did not get faster, so that change stays out of the release.

A trial gas equation and vapor boundary pass 90 behavior and accounting checks across fifteen fountain runs, with no negative-volume events. Small pockets still lose stored gas, and pressure and air-boundary errors remain. A smaller GPU test now reproduces one cause: relabeling a cell as water can discard its gas while more than half its accessible space remains empty. Cavity trial and remaining limits.

A private fix retains that gas in eighteen small-pocket tests. In 96 further checks, its pressure, water flow and gas volume agree with an independent calculation. These tests prescribe the water entering one pocket; they do not establish natural bubble motion. Larger pockets, the reverse classification change and the shape of the water/air boundary still need work, so the fix stays out of the published scenes. Small-pocket checks and limits.

A second test gives the same water and air volumes two different cell labels. The old rule changed the calculated water inertia with the label. A private revision uses the known shape of that water instead; all 96 paired GPU comparisons give identical pressure, motion and gas volume. The test still prescribes the water's shape. Pressure comparisons, precision correction and limits.

Following the particles in a CPU calculation exposes another fault: that proposed fix can change air volume even as the time step approaches zero. A different pressure and particle-transfer calculation passes 124 CPU checks for volume and energy. It uses a different interpolation in the small test and has not run on the GPU yet. Particle calculations and remaining limits.

Heron's fountain now lifts water from its sealed source jar and throws it into the basin. Open the fountain and choose Open valve. Instruments lets you compare sealed jars with a vented lid, an empty source or a raised receiving jar. Restart repeats the selected setup.

The basin drains into the lower jar and compresses its air. A dry tube connects that air to the source jar, pushing its water up the nozzle. Falling basin water supplies the energy, as in the physical fountain experiment. The nozzle starts dry, and every drop stays in the particle solver.

Five behaviors were checked with three different starting particle positions. The fountain emits 82.2%, 83.1% and 82.2% of the original source water through its rising tube and nozzle within one simulation minute. Between 98.2% and 99.3% of that water returns to the basin. Raising the receiving jar lowers the early jet by 18% to 25%. An empty source and a vented lid emit no nozzle water. Opening a hole during a running jet stops later nozzle crossings after a short tail. All water particles are conserved. Fountain measurements, controls and limits.

The fountain's room pressure now corresponds to one standard atmosphere, about 14.7 psi, at the declared 30-inch scale. The earlier setting corresponded to about 0.39 psi and made the air too easy to compress. A separate sealed-jar test now preserves its gas amount as incoming water raises its pressure, on all three seeds.

Jet height remains far below the lossless prediction. Water below the intake stays in the source, and sloshing produces small later spurts after the main flow ends. Small air pockets can lose stored gas; four runs also report a transient pocket with nonpositive volume. The accounting includes those losses, but pressure, air boundaries and energy closure remain diagnostic. The fountain fits with its controls on a 14-inch MacBook screen. One Retina browser check shows a median of 49 FPS, ranging from 35 to 60; steady 60 FPS is unverified.

The siphon carries water from the higher tank, over the bend and into the lower tank. Open the siphon and choose Open valve. Closing the valve holds the water column; reopening it resumes the flow. Instruments lets you compare the filled tube with an empty tube or a raised outlet. Restart repeats the selected setup.

Six behaviors were checked with three different starting particle positions. The filled siphon delivers 51.4%, 50.7% and 50.3% of the original source water within two simulation minutes. Higher positive head, the height difference driving the water, produces 36% to 40% more speed in the comparison. The ideal calculation predicts about 47% more. Siphon measurements, checks and limits.

The two water levels approach the same height as the lower tank fills. Tests count the original source water only after it crosses both the bend and the outlet, excluding the water that starts inside the tube. An empty tube and an outlet above the source surface transfer no source water. Lowering the model's pressure capacity breaks the column; restoring it leaves the air gap in place. A fresh filled setup supplies the water needed to start again.

The siphon uses the cup's pressure transfer and keeps its physical dimensions when the browser is resized. Both machines fit with their controls on a 14-inch MacBook screen. Small forward and backward motions remain near equal water levels. One interrupted run misses the final ten-second speed target, though all three change the receiver's source-water inventory by less than 0.5% over their final minute. Pressure and air-volume checks still fail during working flow. Measured initial speed is about a quarter of the ideal calculation, so absolute flow speed is not calibrated.

The cup's giant slime starts as a ball and follows a drag with Grab. A smaller slime mesh and fewer pressure passes reduce the work per frame; water also draws at the size displayed on screen. The footer shows live FPS. Open the cup and choose Drop the slime to start the drain.

Average frame time fell from about 31 to 21 milliseconds in a Retina browser comparison. Across three tests with different starting particle positions, the current version delivers 96.4%, 99.4% and 99.7% of the water above the intake to the lower tank within three simulation minutes. All particles are conserved. Current measurements and limits.

The pipe walls exposed a fault in how pressure changes particle motion. Water could still move toward a closed wall, crowd against it and leave gaps in the column. The repaired transfer keeps that motion parallel to the wall. Air pressure also uses the measured size of each cavity, correcting a volume estimate that had drifted away from the actual gap.

All three cups hold before the drop. Lowering the fill or raising the bend prevents drainage after the same drop, in three tests each. The counts exclude the water that starts inside the tube and require passage through both the bend and the outlet. The cup keeps the same physical dimensions when the browser is resized.

Pressure and air-volume checks still fail during these runs. Five particles splashed over the rim in one run and are excluded from its delivery count. Contact corrections and local crowding remain, and smooth playback on every device is unverified. All three current runs exceed the 95% delivery target. The pressure model is still diagnostic.

How the simulation works
Water
Particles carry the liquid. A temporary grid exchanges their momentum and applies pressure, then sends them on their way. The MLS-MPM solver runs on WebGPU, adapted from saharan’s water demo.
Air
Machine mode adds a pressure solve while keeping the water particles. Room air sets zero gauge pressure. Each connected sealed pocket has one pressure at a fixed temperature, and compresses as water takes its space. Aanjaneya, Patkar and Fedkiw’s bubble model describes this uniform, isothermal approximation. Room pressure and sound speed are scaled for this toy; Instruments lists their current values. A pressure floor limits the modeled drop below room pressure, and column separation is still being tested. Airflow within a pocket, heat transfer and dissolved gas are omitted.
Smoke
A WebGL fluid solver moves velocity and dye through a grid, following Pavel Dobryakov’s work. The visible smoke is dye in that flow.
Slime
A mesh of elastic connections holds its volume while stretching and compressing. Sluice’s current material, skin contacts, and spring grip use XPBD on the CPU. Highlights attach to the deforming mesh. There are no creature brains or eyes here.

The instrument scale makes the box 30 inches wide and 1 inch deep. Its clock converts simulation time to seconds using Earth gravity. The energy display includes sampled water motion, height, pressure compression and available air work; its remainder includes numerical losses and sample error. Work done by a slime is not yet measured.

This is a game simulation tuned for interaction. The water, smoke, and gel exchange approximate boundary motion; they aren’t one unified physical solver.

Water needs WebGPU. Smoke needs WebGL. If either is unavailable, the other materials still work. The simulation pauses when you leave the tab or scroll it out of view.
