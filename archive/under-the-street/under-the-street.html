A creek under downtown

Bassett Creek flows underground for 1.7 miles before reaching the Mississippi below St. Anthony Falls. Above ground, it passes through Plymouth, Golden Valley, Crystal, and Minneapolis. Downtown, you can cross it without seeing any water. Bassett Creek WMC

Communications cables share space beneath the street with gas mains, electric ducts, water pipes, and drains. Some sewers run much deeper, through sandstone tunnels. Their depths and positions vary from block to block. The sections below move from shallow cables toward the deeper tunnels and the rock around them.

The spray paint before a street excavation tells you which utilities are there. Orange marks communications, yellow gas, red electric, green sewers and drains, and blue drinking water. Minnesota's locate process runs through Gopher State One Call. GSOC The section colors borrow from that code; stormwater gets blue here to distinguish it from sewage.

Orange paint

shallow conduit

Where the fiber goes

Street cables connect to buildings where networks exchange traffic.

Fiber often runs in shallow conduit, a plastic duct that lets crews install or replace cable without reopening the whole street. The small access boxes in the boulevard are called handholes. Unlike a water pipe, fiber doesn't need protection from freezing.

Several networks meet at the 511 Building, at 511 11th Avenue South near the stadium. It's a carrier hotel, a building where different providers connect their equipment. Cologix's MIN1 facility hosts MICE, the Midwest Internet Cooperative Exchange. Cologix PeeringDB When providers exchange traffic there, a connection between local customers can stay in Minneapolis rather than pass through another city.

During the 2007 I-35W bridge collapse, overloaded phone networks made communication difficult. Minneapolis's municipal Wi-Fi network helped responders communicate while it was still being rolled out. Computerworld, 2007

Yellow paint

street mains

Gas mains and frozen ground

The gas network has to meet its highest demand during the coldest weather.

CenterPoint Energy, formerly Minnegasco, supplies much of the metro's natural gas. Its street mains feed smaller service pipes into buildings. Natural gas has no smell of its own; utilities add an odorant to help people detect leaks. The familiar rotten-egg smell comes from that additive. CenterPoint

Minnesota's building code sets frost-protection depths for footings, the bases of foundations: 3 feet 6 inches in the southern zone, including the metro, and 5 feet in the northern zone. These are default construction requirements; actual frost depth varies, and utilities have their own burial requirements. MN Rules 1303.1600 In the street itself, water freezing in cracks helps break the pavement apart.

Gas demand rises as furnaces run longer. Pipe capacity and pressure have to cover that winter peak, including mornings when much of the metro is heating at once.

Red paint

poles, vaults, duct banks

Power under downtown

Overhead wires in many neighborhoods; ducts and vaults downtown.

Many residential blocks get electricity from overhead wires. Downtown, cables run through concrete duct banks, groups of conduits buried together. Vaults below the sidewalk give crews access to cable connections and equipment. Underground networks can have multiple feeds, allowing an alternate path when a cable fails.

Xcel Energy supplies both cities. Its Minnesota generating plants include Monticello, upstream on the Mississippi, and Prairie Island, downstream near Red Wing. Both are nuclear plants. Xcel overview

Local electric generation began at St. Anthony Falls in 1882 with a Brush central power station. The Hennepin Island hydro plant followed in 1908 and still generates power for Xcel. Falls history Hennepin Island

Both downtowns also have district heating: central plants send heat to buildings through buried pipes. District Energy St. Paul uses hot water, with wood waste supplying part of its heat. Minneapolis has its own steam and hot-water network. District Energy St. Paul District heating

pipes and tunnels

Storm drains go to the river

Rain and snowmelt usually reach a lake or river without passing through a sewage plant.

Storm drains collect rain and snowmelt from streets and carry it toward lakes and rivers. They're separate from the pipes that take household sewage to treatment plants. Runoff may pass through ponds or other stormwater controls, but it generally doesn't get sewage treatment.

Road salt follows that route. It dissolves in meltwater and carries chloride into lakes, streams, and groundwater. The Minnesota Pollution Control Agency says a teaspoon of salt can contaminate five gallons of water, and removing chloride once it's dispersed is impractical. MPCA

The Old Bassett Creek Tunnel carries stormwater from north Minneapolis. Between 2017 and 2020, crews cleared accumulated sediment and debris from it. MWMO Some local storm tunnels are large enough to walk through; Greg Brick documents them in Subterranean Twin Cities. Brick

Sewer tunnels in sandstone

The St. Peter Sandstone was soft enough to excavate by hand.

Along parts of the Mississippi gorge, hard Platteville limestone lies above the softer St. Peter Sandstone. The sandstone is mostly quartz sand, weakly cemented together. St. Peter Sandstone Crews excavated sewer and utility tunnels through it by hand. Brick's book also describes Schieks Cave, a natural cave beneath downtown Minneapolis reached through the sewers. Brick, Subterranean Twin Cities book source

City sewer pipes feed regional interceptors, the larger pipes and tunnels that carry wastewater to treatment plants. Much of the flow from Minneapolis and St. Paul reaches the Metro facility near Pig's Eye Lake. The Met Council reports an average of 172 million gallons a day there, serving 1.8 million people. Other parts of the metro drain to other plants, as the map shows. Met Council

Before treatment began, city sewers discharged directly into the Mississippi. By the 1920s, an estimated three million cubic yards of sewage sludge had settled above the Ford Dam. The plant opened in 1938, the first to serve a major metropolitan area on the Mississippi. MDH

The old combined sewers carried rainwater and sewage in the same pipe. Heavy rain could overload them and send untreated waste through overflow outlets to the river. Minneapolis began substantial separation work in the 1960s and accelerated it in 1986. By the end of that program, 8 of the original 34 overflow regulators remained. Later work addressed the remaining connections; in 2018 the city requested termination of its combined-sewer overflow permit. City of Minneapolis 2018 council record

In June 1987, a mayfly hatch near the airport left Interstate 494 covered in insects. The highway closed while snowplows cleared them. MDH describes the hatch as evidence of the river's improving water quality. MDH

Below the limestone

the riverbed

The wall beneath St. Anthony Falls

A concrete wall built in 1876 keeps water from eroding the sandstone beneath the falls.

The limestone ledge at St. Anthony Falls rests on sandstone that water can wash away. Erosion beneath the ledge threatened the falls and the waterpower used by Minneapolis's mills. In October 1869, the river broke into the Eastman tunnel, an excavation beneath the riverbed, and washed out sandstone around it. Part of Hennepin Island collapsed. Army Corps of Engineers

The Corps eventually built a concrete cutoff wall beneath the limestone. Finished in 1876, it stretched 1,850 feet across the river and reached about 40 feet into the sandstone. It blocked water from passing under the falls and cutting new channels. Army Corps of Engineers

The Corps also covered the falls with a sloping apron, completed in 1880 and later rebuilt in concrete. That's the slope you see from the Stone Arch Bridge. The cutoff wall remains buried beneath the limestone upstream. National Park Service Falls history
