A creek under downtown

Bassett Creek reaches the Mississippi through tunnels beneath Minneapolis. One route carries the creek's normal flow; an older route carries neighborhood runoff and takes some creek water during heavy rain. MWMO

The pipes under a street have different destinations. Drinking water arrives from treatment plants. Sewage leaves for another set of plants. Rainwater goes toward lakes and rivers. Cables and gas mains connect the same buildings to other networks.

The paint before an excavation identifies the utility: blue for drinking water, green for sewers and drains, orange for communications, red for electricity, and yellow for gas, oil, steam or propane. These are the actual marking colors. The map uses muted colors, with blue for stormwater and purple for geology. Its colors describe the layers here, while the paint outside follows the locate code. Gopher State One Call

There is no fixed order from shallow to deep. A city storm tunnel can run through sandstone far below a water main. The map shows the public records available for each system; the article explains what moves through it.

Blue paint

mains and service pipes

From the river to the tap

Both cities use the Mississippi. They take different routes to the street.

Minneapolis pumps river water into its treatment system in Fridley. It softens the water, filters it at Fridley or Columbia Heights, and disinfects it before delivery. Fridley uses granular activated carbon filters; Columbia Heights uses ultrafiltration, membranes that hold back small particles. The city's distribution system includes about 1,000 miles of water mains. Minneapolis

St. Paul pumps Mississippi water from Fridley through a chain of lakes and conduits before it reaches the McCarrons plant in Maplewood. Vadnais Lake is the last lake before the plant. This is still source water, before the treatment that makes it drinking water. SPRWS treatment process 2019 supply plan, figure I.C-2

McCarrons brought its new treatment facilities online in 2025. The plant softens and clarifies the water, filters it through carbon and sand, then adds chlorine and ammonia to make chloramine, a disinfectant that lasts through the distribution system. New equipment can also add ozone when needed. 2025 opening SPRWS

A service line is the smaller pipe between the street main and a building's meter. Some older ones are lead service lines. The Minneapolis inventory shown here contains 99,774 published connection points. Its classes describe pipe material, rather than the amount of lead in someone's drinking water. An unknown record means the material has not been established. City inventory St. Paul's replacement program has its own address map. Lead Free SPRWS

Orange paint

cables and conduit

Where the networks meet

Fiber in the street leads to buildings where providers connect.

The 511 Building, at 511 11th Avenue South, is a carrier hotel: several network operators keep equipment in the same building. Cologix's MIN1 facility there hosts MICE, the Midwest Internet Cooperative Exchange. An internet exchange lets participating networks pass traffic directly between them. Cologix MIN1

Eagan publishes fiber cable records separately from utility paths. Both are on this map. A path by itself does not establish that a cable has been laid or that a provider offers service along it. Eagan public map

Yellow paint

gas and heat pipes

Gas to the building, heat from a plant

A district heating pipe carries heat already made elsewhere.

CenterPoint supplies natural gas in Minneapolis; St. Paul gets gas from Xcel. Minneapolis utilities St. Paul utilities Street mains feed smaller service pipes into buildings. The gas itself has no smell; utilities add an odorant so a leak can be noticed. CenterPoint

Both downtowns also have district heating. St. Paul's system sends hot water through underground pipes. A heat exchanger transfers its heat to a building's own water circuit; the district water returns to the plant to be heated again. Wood waste supplies part of the heat. District Energy St. Paul Fuel sources

Downtown Minneapolis has a separate Cordia system. It supplies steam for heat and chilled water for cooling, with 39,000 feet of steam and hot-water distribution and 24,900 feet of chilled-water distribution. Those pipes carry thermal energy rather than drinking water. Cordia system profile

Red paint

overhead and underground

Two kinds of power line

Transmission links substations. Distribution reaches buildings.

The large lines on the map belong to the transmission system, which moves power between generating plants and substations. The smaller distribution system delivers it to customers. A transmission-line map does not show every street cable or say that every line is buried.

Minneapolis does have underground transmission. Xcel's North Loop project describes existing 115-kilovolt cables between the Aldrich and West River Road substations, with part of the system needing relocation for the proposed Blue Line extension. Xcel's published replacement routes are proposals. Xcel project record

Green paint

drains, culverts and tunnels

Rain has its own route

A deep tunnel can carry stormwater rather than sewage.

Storm drains collect rain and snowmelt from streets and carry it toward lakes and rivers. Runoff may pass through ponds or other controls, but it generally does not pass through a sewage treatment plant. Road salt follows the runoff: dissolved chloride reaches surface water and groundwater, where removing it is impractical. MPCA says a teaspoon of salt can contaminate five gallons of water. MPCA

Bassett Creek's old and new tunnels do different work. The old 1.5-mile tunnel was completed in 1923. It now mostly drains 870 acres of Minneapolis, with creek flow entering during heavy rain. Its 2018 and 2020 cleanouts removed 1,735 tons of sediment from 1,933 feet of tunnel. MWMO 1923 completion

The new system includes a double box culvert, two adjoining passages mostly 11 feet high and 11 feet wide. This part was built in 1992 near the Cedar Lake Trail, in an open excavation ranging from ground level to 20 feet deep. A roughly 30-foot drop takes water into the deeper Third Avenue Tunnel. Farther downstream, the Second Street section was excavated in sandstone as much as 80 feet below the street. Those depths describe different parts of the new system. 2025 engineering report, p. 6 BCWMC history

The Central City tunnels are another stormwater system. The city places them about 70 feet below the streets and says they collect runoff from nearly all of downtown. A new parallel tunnel beneath Washington Avenue South, between Nicollet Mall and Chicago Avenue, was completed in 2024. Minneapolis

Green paint

local pipes and regional interceptors

Where sewage goes

City sewers feed a larger regional network.

Local sanitary sewers feed regional interceptors, the larger pipes and tunnels that carry wastewater to treatment plants. The Metro facility near Pig's Eye Lake receives wastewater from 66 communities through 389 miles of interceptors. It serves about 1.8 million people and treats an average of 172 million gallons a day. Other parts of the region drain to other plants. Metropolitan Council

Before the plant opened in 1938, sewage went straight into the Mississippi. By the 1920s, an estimated three million cubic yards of sewage sludge had accumulated above the Ford Dam. MDH historical account

The older combined sewers carried rainwater and sewage in the same pipe. Rain could fill them faster than the interceptors could drain them, sending untreated overflow to the river. Minneapolis accelerated separation work in 1986. In 2018, after years of disconnecting roof drains from sanitary sewers, repairing pipes and building storm drains, it sought termination of its combined-sewer overflow permit. The city reported no river overflows in 10 of the preceding 11 years, with two small events in 2010. Minneapolis history 2018 council record

In June 1987, a mayfly hatch near the airport left Interstate 494 covered in insects. The highway closed while snowplows cleared them. MDH describes the hatch as evidence of the river's improving water quality. MDH

Limestone and sandstone

the riverbed

The buried wall at the falls

The Corps built it to keep water from washing away the rock beneath St. Anthony Falls.

At the falls, hard Platteville limestone lies above softer St. Peter Sandstone, with a thinner shale layer between them. Water can erode the sandstone and leave the limestone unsupported. In October 1869, the river broke into the Eastman tunnel, a waterpower excavation under the riverbed, and enlarged the opening until part of Hennepin Island collapsed. National Park Service

The Army Corps of Engineers built a concrete wall beneath the limestone. Finished in November 1876, it stretched 1,850 feet across the river and reached as much as 39 feet below the limestone. The wall remains buried at the falls, helping stop water from opening new passages through the sandstone. National Park Service

The Corps completed a sloping apron over the falls in 1880. Today the visible slope is a concrete spillway. You can see it from the Stone Arch Bridge. The cutoff wall remains buried beneath the limestone upstream. National Park Service
