What You Get Used To

Sleep in a really expensive hotel bed for a month and it stops feeling special. It's still a better bed, though.

So do we get used to everything? No. Here's where the line is. A lot of things lose their kick, like a nice bed or a drug's high. Some hit harder the more they repeat, like some people's pain and a problem drinker's craving. And feeling something less doesn't mean it's doing less to you.

Drugs really can end up feeling like decaf. That's tolerance: the same dose does less. But tolerance doesn't hit every effect equally. With opioids like morphine, the body builds a lot of tolerance to the high and the pain relief, and less to the slowed breathing. Slowed breathing is what kills people in an overdose. So someone chasing the old high takes more, and their breathing hasn't built up the same tolerance.

Alcohol didn't turn into decaf for everyone. A Chicago study gave 190 young drinkers alcohol in a lab, then did it again five and ten years later. About one in five ended up with a drinking problem. For them, alcohol got more stimulating over the decade, they wanted it more, and they liked it as much as ever.

Pain can go the other way too. In one set of heat-pulse experiments, 312 adults got short pulses of heat on the palm or foot, three seconds apart, each peaking at the same temperature, about 124 F. For some of them, the pain got worse as the pulses went on. Others felt about the same, or less. The whole test took about twelve seconds.

Plenty of things do fade. A loud noise makes you jump, and if it keeps coming, you jump less. Take a break and the jump comes back, at least partly. Pleasure can do something similar. In one experiment, people enjoyed a three-minute massage more when 20 seconds in the middle were a break, even though that meant less massage.

Then there's no sleep from having kids. I'd have said you get used to it. The closest test I found didn't involve kids. In a lab, people were allowed four or six hours in bed a night for two weeks. Their sleepiness went up at first and then mostly leveled off. Their attention didn't. By day 14, the six-hour group was doing about as badly on alertness tests as someone who'd been up all night, and the four-hour group as badly as someone up two nights straight. At their worst, they said they felt only slightly sleepy.

One reason exercise gets easier is that your body changes. In a twelve-week cycling study, the most oxygen the men's bodies could use went up 18 percent in the young ones and 31 percent in the older ones. It's like carrying the same box up the same stairs once you're in better shape.

Practice is a separate thing. It makes you notice more. A coffee roaster can see the exact shade the beans should be. A chef eyeballs ingredients. A race car driver hits the right line without thinking about it. Researchers call this perceptual learning, and they point to it in radiologists, chess masters and air traffic controllers. The experts often can't say what they're seeing, which is part of why it's so hard to teach.

Food goes both ways. You can get used to eating shitty, and you can get used to eating healthy. In one trial, parents gave their three-year-olds a taste of a vegetable the kid disliked every day for two weeks, with a sticker for trying it. Afterward, the parents said those kids liked it more and ate more of it than kids who skipped the program.

I think people really do get used to abuse. Nobody I found measured whether it actually hurts less over time. A study that followed about a thousand people in New Zealand from birth found that the ones who'd been maltreated as kids were more likely to have raised markers of inflammation in their blood at 32. That doesn't prove the abuse caused it, and it isn't something you'd feel.

For luxury, the best evidence I found is about money. In Sweden, researchers compared lottery players who won big prizes with players who won small prizes or nothing. Surveyed five to twenty-two years later, the big winners rated their lives higher, and the gap was no smaller for people who'd won longer ago. Asked how happy they were, all things considered, the two groups weren't clearly different.

Here's how I think about what lasts. Pleasure is what you get from doing what you want. Happiness is what you get from doing what's right. Pleasure fades, and you can't keep going back for more. Happiness fades too, but it never gets old, and you can always go back for more.

Some of the evidence above pushes back on the pleasure half. A break brought the massage back, and the problem drinkers liked alcohol as much after ten years as at the start.

The happiness half has one small test behind it. In two experiments, people got a little money over and over to spend on themselves or give away: $5 a day for five days in one, a nickel a round over ten rounds of a quick online game in the other. The good feeling from giving faded more slowly than the good feeling from getting, about half as fast in the game. It still faded some, and nobody followed it past five days.

The best objection to my answer is the word "eventually." Maybe every feeling fades at least a little if you wait long enough. The heat test lasted about twelve seconds, but the drinking study ran ten years, and for the people who developed a problem, the wanting was stronger at the end than at the start. If a longer study watched that wanting fade, I'd change my answer. Without a deadline, though, "eventually" can never be proven wrong, because you can always wait another year.
