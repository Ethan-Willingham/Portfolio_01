How to Think, and What's Real

David Deutsch

Earth's tilted axis explains opposite seasons in its two hemispheres. Deutsch asks what makes explanations like this so powerful, and whether the progress they make possible has to end.

The book

Why one account of winter explains more

When Europe has winter, Australia has summer. The Earth's tilted axis explains this: as the planet orbits the sun, one hemisphere receives longer days and more direct sunlight while the other receives less. NASA's account shows the mechanism.

In The Beginning of Infinity (2011), David Deutsch compares this with the Greek story of Demeter making the world cold while her daughter Persephone is in the underworld. That story predicts winter, but its particular gods and motives are replaceable. A different divine quarrel could account for the same weather. The explanation of Earth's tilt has fewer adjustable parts because its parts have jobs to do.

Deutsch calls explanations like this hard to vary. He means changing the account of what causes something, rather than changing the words you use to describe it. He asks whether a continuing search for explanations can make progress unbounded.

Deutsch is a physicist whose 1985 paper proposed a universal quantum computer. His account of learning comes from Karl Popper: propose an explanation, look for mistakes, and improve it. The book extends this into an optimism that makes further progress possible without making it inevitable.

Knowledge

Even our best explanations can contain mistakes.

Fallibilism is the view that knowledge remains open to correction. For Deutsch, no source, including perception or an expert, can certify that a theory is true or even probably true. This is his Popperian position; it doesn't mean every explanation is equally good.

We can prefer an explanation because it solves a problem and survives criticism that defeats its rivals. Chapter 1; official glossary, Fallibilism.

Knowledge

Observations don't supply their own explanation.

The book opens with stars. Our eyes receive light; they don't receive the explanation that stars are distant nuclear furnaces.

Deutsch opposes deriving theories from sensory experience, or establishing general laws from repeated observations. Those are his targets in empiricism and inductivism. Experience still matters. It can expose a failure in a proposed explanation and reveal a new problem; it cannot mechanically generate the theory. Chapter 1, opening.

How knowledge grows

An explanation improves through attempts to find its errors.

A conjecture is a proposed answer. Criticism can show that it contradicts an observation, conflicts with another explanation, or fails to solve the problem it was meant to solve.

Experiments are one form of criticism. Their interpretation can also be wrong: an apparent failure may come from an instrument or an auxiliary assumption. Popper's method requires examining those possibilities, rather than discarding a theory after any awkward result.

So theories, according to Popper, are always conjecture, and thinking about theories is always criticism.Deutsch, Joe Walker interview, April 25, 2022, discussion before 28:48

Explanation criterion

The details should be constrained by what they explain.

Demeter's grief and Earth's tilted axis both offer testable accounts of winter. Opposite seasons could refute a myth that predicts winter everywhere at once. Falsifiability, the possibility of an observation contradicting a theory, therefore doesn't distinguish the two by itself.

Deutsch's additional question is how easily the account can be rewritten. The myth can acquire another god or motive to accommodate the result. In the tilt explanation, the geometry determines which hemisphere receives more sunlight. Changing that prediction requires changes that must also account for the other observations. Testability and success in experiments still matter; being hard to vary alone cannot establish truth.

hard/easy to vary while still accounting for what it purports to account for.Deutsch, ch. 1, Terminology, Good/bad explanation; official glossary, excerpt

Further claims

An explanation can solve problems its creators weren't considering.

The tilt explanation also accounts for opposite seasons and the summer midnight sun near the poles. These consequences follow from its content, even if its creator hadn't encountered them. Deutsch calls this reach.

Reach belongs to a particular explanation, with a particular scope. A good explanation of Earth's geography doesn't tell us the geography of Mars. Any wider application remains open to testing. Chapter 1, discussion of reach.

Further claims

A finite set of rules can have an open-ended range.

A place-value numeral system can represent arbitrarily large integers without inventing a symbol for each one. A universal computer can run any computation in its class, given enough memory and time. Their universality has a defined domain.

Deutsch argues that people are universal explainers: we can in principle learn any explanation that can be understood. Tools can extend our memory and abilities. Human universality remains a philosophical claim; the existence of universal computers doesn't establish it. He connects it to our ability to build devices that perform physically possible transformations. Individual brains, machines, and projects still have limits. Chapter 6, Summary and concluding discussion.

Further claims

A present inability needn't be a permanent impossibility.

Deutsch argues that a physically permitted transformation can be achieved with the knowledge of how to achieve it. Chapter 3 also names necessary resources: matter, an energy supply, and evidence for testing theories. Knowledge changes what we can do with resources. It doesn't create energy from nothing.

Chapter 9 explicitly allows that eliminating a problem may be physically impossible within a given time and the available resources. Chapter 8 accepts insoluble mathematical questions. His maxim that problems are soluble concerns opportunities to improve explanations and conditions; it doesn't promise an answer to every question or a rescue before every deadline.

everything that is not forbidden by laws of nature is achievable, given the right knowledge.Deutsch, ch. 3, Meanings of “the beginning of infinity”; chapter text

Further claims

Optimism explains failure without predicting success.

Deutsch attributes suffering and failure to knowledge we haven't created or applied in time. This includes understanding how to improve institutions and choices. He also assumes that physical prohibitions needn't themselves be evils.

He rejects confidence that nothing bad will happen, which he calls blind optimism. He also rejects a blanket refusal to try anything not already known to be safe. Both pretend to know the consequences of future choices. His alternative requires creating knowledge while admitting that failure, including catastrophe, remains possible. Chapter 9, discussion of optimism.

All evils are caused by insufficient knowledge.Deutsch, ch. 9, The Principle of Optimism; official glossary

Further claims

Creating explanations gives people unusual physical powers.

Deutsch challenges the inference that, because Earth occupies no privileged cosmic location, people must be insignificant. An entity that creates explanatory knowledge can build things and transform environments in ways that its location alone doesn't explain.

His criticism of Spaceship Earth makes a related point: agriculture, sanitation, and shelter help make human life possible. He argues against treating the biosphere as a complete support system supplied for us. People still depend on clean water and functioning ecosystems. His claim of exceptional cosmic significance concerns the capacity to create knowledge. Chapter 3, The Spark.

The conditions

Why progress can stop

A society can have creative people and still punish them for changing anything. In chapter 15, Deutsch describes static societies as cultures that suppress criticism and change slowly enough for their members to experience the arrangements as fixed. A dynamic society allows ideas to survive by withstanding criticism, and allows better ideas to replace them. Chapter 15, static and dynamic societies.

Following Popper, Deutsch asks how bad rulers and policies can be removed without violence. Elections alone aren't enough if opposition can't criticize the government or dislodge it. Chapter 13, Popper's criterion.

He uses Enlightenment to name a continuing tradition of criticism. His sweeping contrast between static earlier societies and a dynamic West is his historical interpretation, not a neutral classification of every past culture. Those practices can be lost.

The limits

What the argument leaves open

Who decides what's hard to vary?

There's no measurement of how hard an explanation is to vary that settles every comparison. A rigid account can be false. Two rivals can each have tightly connected parts. Choosing between them still requires criticism and, where possible, discriminating evidence. The seasons example doesn't settle the book's later claims about beauty, creativity, or politics.

What about probabilities?

Deutsch rejects Bayesian epistemology, which represents degrees of belief in theories as probabilities and updates them when evidence arrives. He accepts Bayes' theorem and appropriate uses of conditional probability. He makes the distinction in his 2022 Walker interview, from 28:48.

Bayesian approaches offer a formal account of how evidence changes the relative credibility of specified hypotheses. Deutsch argues that this cannot explain the creation of a new hypothesis, or reduce explanatory knowledge to a number. A method for comparing proposed accounts needn't also invent them, so that objection alone doesn't refute Bayesian updating. Their disagreement concerns what makes an explanation worth accepting and what evidential support amounts to.

Can optimism tell us what to risk?

The possibility of a solution doesn't tell us when it will be found, what it will cost, or who will suffer while we look. Rejecting a universal demand for proven safety doesn't settle which precautions a particular project needs.

Deutsch's hope is that no final barrier prevents further improvement. We may still fail to find or apply a solution before the damage is irreversible.
