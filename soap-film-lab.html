The square frame is 16 cm across. Gravity points down; warm liquid rises. Model seconds track the solver, and may run slower on a busy device. Hole expansion is replayed 240 times slower. Touch the film to move the sample; it starts at the center.

Depth-averaged convectionSilent, self-running

A soap film’s colors tell you how thick it is. Light reflected from its two surfaces cancels at some wavelengths and adds at others. Here those colors come from the film’s thickness, the CIE color tables and daylight illumination. Heat enters at the lower edge. Liquid rises, cools and falls while gravity drains the film into the bottom of its frame.

What this model leaves out

This is a flat, depth-averaged fluid carrying a thickness field. It includes thermal buoyancy, viscosity, air drag, evaporation and conservative drainage. Real soap films also have surfactant transport, surface stresses, capillarity and molecular stabilization. Those effects are absent. The minimum-thickness rule for opening a hole is an assumed failure criterion; a real black film can remain intact. After a short rest, the apparatus supplies a fresh film.

The 2008 experiment below heated a curved half-bubble at its equator. This square frame uses a different geometry. Its rising plumes are thermal convection; this is not a Rayleigh-Taylor experiment.
