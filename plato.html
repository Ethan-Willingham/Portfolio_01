Socrates, Plato, and the cave

Athens sentenced Socrates to death. Plato, his younger associate, wrote about his defense and imagined a city ruled by philosophers.

14 min read

Socrates and Plato

In 399 BC, a court in Athens sentenced the philosopher Socrates to death. He had spent years questioning people about how they should live. His accusers said he was corrupting the young and offending the city's religion. He argued that Athens needed the questions he was asking. The trial of Socrates

Socrates lived in ancient Greece. He would ask someone what justice or courage meant, listen to the answer, then look for a case it couldn't explain. He wanted to know whether their beliefs held up when someone questioned them.

Plato was a younger Athenian who knew Socrates and became a philosopher himself. Socrates left no philosophical writings. Plato wrote conversations in which Socrates is usually the main speaker. These are called dialogues: people make claims, question each other, and sometimes finish without agreeing on an answer. Plato's way of writing

Plato also developed ideas of his own. Other writers who knew Socrates give different portraits, and we can't reliably separate the real man's words from the character's. The cave story and the proposal for philosophers to rule belong to Plato's book, the Republic. Having Socrates tell them doesn't make them a transcript of Socrates' beliefs. The historical Socrates

How Socrates questioned people

Near the beginning of the Republic, an older man links living justly with telling the truth and returning what you owe. Socrates tests that answer. Suppose a friend leaves weapons with you, then loses his reason and asks for them back. Returning them would meet the rule, but it would be dangerous. His companion agrees that it wouldn't be right. The proposed account of justice needs to change. Republic 331c to 331d

This is the questioning often called the Socratic method. Start with a claim, ask what it commits you to, and check whether you still accept it in a particular case. The example breaks the rule without yet telling us what justice is.

In the Apology, Plato's account of the courtroom defense, Socrates explains why he began testing people who had reputations for wisdom. Here, “apology” means a defense, not an expression of regret. A friend had consulted the oracle at Delphi, a sanctuary where a priestess was believed to give the god Apollo's answers. She said no one was wiser than Socrates. He didn't think he knew enough for this to be true, so he went looking for someone who could prove the oracle wrong. Apology 20e to 21b

He questioned politicians, poets, and craftsmen. The craftsmen understood their trades, but assumed this made them wise about more important matters too. Socrates thought he had a small advantage over the people who couldn't answer his questions: he recognized his ignorance, while they mistook confidence for understanding. Apology 21b to 23b

That is narrower than the popular slogan “I know that I know nothing.” Socrates doesn't deny every kind of knowledge. Later in the defense, he says he knows that doing wrong is shameful, while he doesn't know whether death is bad. Apology 29b

Why Athens sentenced him to death

Socrates' accusers charged him with corrupting the young, failing to acknowledge the gods Athens recognized, and introducing new divine beings. Religion was part of the city's public life, and the charges alleged wrongdoing against it. Apology 24b to 28a

He nevertheless says his questions helped create enemies. Some young men enjoyed hearing respected people fail to defend their answers, then tried the questioning themselves. The people they embarrassed blamed Socrates. He describes himself as a biting fly keeping a large, sluggish horse awake: Athens needs someone to disturb its confidence. Apology 23c to 23e; 30e to 31a

His defense is religious too. He says the god gave him a duty to question people. He describes a divine warning that sometimes stops him from acting. He disputes the accusation that his conduct is impious, meaning irreverent toward the gods. Apology 31c to 31d

Athens had recently lost a war against Sparta and suffered a violent takeover by a small group of rulers known as the Thirty. Socrates knew men associated with attacks on its democracy. This helps explain suspicion, but doesn't prove the religious charges concealed a political prosecution. Plato also has him describe refusing the Thirty's order to help seize a man for execution. We lack the accusers' speeches. The trial's setting; Apology 32c to 32e

After finding Socrates guilty, the jury has to choose a penalty. He first says he deserves public meals, an honor awarded to Olympic victors. He considers imprisonment and exile, then proposes a fine his friends will guarantee. The jury chooses death. Apology 36b to 38b

Living quietly would defeat the purpose of his life, he says. The daily examination he means is discussion about becoming a good person: whether an action is just, what makes courage different from recklessness, and whether money deserves more attention than character. He tells the jury:

Apology 38a / Benjamin Jowett

“the life which is unexamined is not worth living”

That is a much stronger claim than saying reflection is useful. Many people live generously without spending their days in philosophical argument. Socrates doesn't establish here that their lives have no value. He does choose to risk his own rather than give up the questioning he thinks makes it worth living.

Why he refused to escape

Plato's Crito takes place in prison while Socrates awaits execution. Crito, a friend, arrives before dawn with a plan to get him out. Socrates has children to raise; Crito fears people will blame his friends for failing to save him. Crito 43a to 46a

Socrates asks whether escaping would be right. The answer should follow the principles they accepted before the sentence. If the city has wronged him, that alone doesn't give him permission to wrong it in return:

Crito 49c to 49d / Benjamin Jowett

“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to anyone, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”

But why would escaping an unjust sentence be a wrong? Socrates imagines the Laws of Athens addressing him as if they were a person. They say the city made his upbringing possible. As an adult he could have left, but he stayed and accepted its legal arrangements. If people can set aside judgments whenever they dislike them, the courts lose their authority. The Laws say he should persuade the city that it is wrong or accept its decision. Crito 50a to 52d

This argument depends on more than the principle against revenge. Escaping might save a victim without destroying the legal system. Staying in your birthplace also seems a weak substitute for freely agreeing to every judgment its courts might make.

There is a further difficulty. In his defense, Socrates says he would disobey an order to stop philosophizing. Here, he accepts the city's punishment. A possible distinction is that he won't obey a command to do wrong, but he won't escape punishment by breaking a commitment either. Whether a state that sentences someone unjustly can still demand that commitment remains a problem. The tension between the two books

The last conversation

Another book, the Phaedo, describes Socrates' last day. Phaedo is a companion recounting the scene afterward. He says Plato was ill and absent. Athens allowed no executions while its sacred ship traveled to Delos, an island associated with Apollo. The ship's return means the sentence can be carried out. Phaedo 58a to 59b

Socrates spends much of the day arguing that the soul survives death. By soul, he means what gives a body life and does the thinking, capable, in this account, of existing apart from the body. He thinks philosophy prepares it for that separation by teaching it to pursue understanding instead of being ruled by bodily desires. Phaedo 64c to 67b

His companions challenge him. One compares the soul to the harmony of a musical instrument: a beautiful pattern could disappear when the instrument breaks. Another asks whether a soul might outlast several bodies and still eventually perish. Socrates' final argument says the soul brings life to a body and therefore cannot admit its opposite, death. But ruling out an opposite doesn't automatically prove that something will survive: fire can't be cold, yet it can go out. The dialogue also needs the claim that what is deathless cannot be destroyed. Socrates' companions accept that additional step. Phaedo 85e to 107b

At the end, Socrates drinks the poison. His companions weep; he asks them to be quiet. As his body grows cold, he reminds Crito that they owe an offering of a rooster to Asclepius, the god of healing. The dialogue doesn't explain what the debt was for. Phaedo 117b to 118a

The city Plato imagined

In the Republic, Plato puts Socrates in a different setting: a conversation at a private house, before the trial. The book asks what justice is and why we should want to be just. Glaucon, one of the speakers and Plato's brother, sharpens the question. Suppose someone does wrong, becomes wealthy, and earns a good reputation by hiding it. Why would being just be better than that? Praise and rewards for looking good won't answer him. Republic 357a to 367e

Socrates proposes examining justice in a whole city first, then looking for the same pattern in a person. He compares this with reading large letters before trying to make out small ones. The speakers build a city in words, deciding what it would need and how its people should live together. This is the ideal city: an imagined society used to investigate what a good social order would be. It isn't an account of a place Plato visited. Republic 368c to 369b

They divide its work among people who produce what it needs, people who defend it, and people who govern. Socrates eventually identifies justice with these groups doing their own work without taking over the others' roles. Republic 433a to 434c

He compares the city with a person. Our appetites are desires for things like food, drink, and possessions. We can want another drink while also judging that we should stop. Socrates thinks reason should direct these desires with the whole life in view. What he calls spirit, the part that becomes angry or defends our honor, should support that direction. Justice in a person means these motives cooperating under reason, just as the city's groups cooperate under its rulers. Republic 436a to 444a

This is his answer to Glaucon: an ordered life is good for the person living it. Someone dominated by greed or fear can gain power while losing control of their own life. He argues that a just person is more free and happier than a tyrant, even when the tyrant appears successful. Republic 577c to 580c

That comparison has political consequences. If the whole city's well-being depends on good judgment, who should govern it? Socrates argues for philosophers: people who can give a reasoned account of what is good, rather than chase wealth or popularity. This is the philosopher-ruler proposal. The cave story explains what their education should achieve. Republic 473c to 480a

The cave is an invented story

Socrates asks Glaucon to picture prisoners who have been chained inside a cave since childhood. They can face only a wall. A fire burns behind them. Between the fire and the prisoners, people carry objects above a low barrier, casting shadows on the wall. The prisoners can see the shadows but can't turn around to see what produces them. Republic 514a to 515b

When the people carrying objects speak, echoes make the voices seem to come from the passing shadows. The prisoners think the shadows are the things themselves. They give them names, predict which will appear next, and reward the people who make the best predictions. They can become skilled within this restricted view while misunderstanding what they are looking at.

One prisoner is released and made to stand up and turn around. Looking toward the fire hurts his eyes. He finds the objects harder to recognize than their familiar shadows and wants to turn back. Then he is dragged up the steep passage into daylight. At first, he can barely see. Gradually he can look at reflections, the things outside, and finally the sun. Republic 515c to 516c

This is a picture of learning, not a story about prehistoric humans leaving cave homes. Plato imagines someone discovering how badly their familiar view of the world misled them. Learning is painful here because a person has to give up the standards that previously made them feel competent.

When the prisoner returns, his eyes need to adjust to the dark. He performs badly at identifying the shadows, so the others think the journey ruined his sight. They conclude that leaving is dangerous. Socrates says they would kill someone trying to free them if they could get hold of him. That threat may recall Socrates' fate, but the story doesn't identify the prisoner as Socrates or explain his trial. Republic 516e to 517a

What the daylight stands for

The cave is an allegory, a story whose people and places represent ideas. The climb stands for education: moving from the changing things we see toward an understanding reached through reasoning. Plato's claim goes further than being suspicious of rumors or checking whether a picture is fake. He thinks we can know stable realities beyond the physical things our senses encounter. Republic 517b to 518d

He also wants education to change what someone values. A clever wrongdoer may know exactly how to get what he wants; becoming cleverer won't make him good. Republic 518e to 519a

The comparison with equality in the Phaedo helps explain this. Two sticks can seem equal from one perspective and unequal from another. Yet we can judge that they fall short of perfect equality. Socrates argues that the standard we use, equality itself, is different from either stick. He takes our ability to make this judgment as evidence that we grasp a standard beyond the particular examples. Plato calls these stable realities Forms: Equality itself, Justice itself, Beauty itself. The capitalized word doesn't mean a physical shape. Phaedo 74a to 75d

Plato thinks these realities exist independently of our thoughts about them. Particular just actions can be imperfect, disputed, or mixed with something unjust. Justice itself supplies the reality by which they are judged. A philosopher should learn to explain what makes those actions just, rather than accumulate examples or repeat the judgments of the crowd.

The sun represents what the Republic calls the Good. As sunlight makes sight possible, the Good makes knowledge and truth possible. It is the source of what makes things understandable and good in Plato's account. Knowing how to win a war or make a city wealthier wouldn't be enough: a ruler needs to judge whether those ends are worth choosing and what should be sacrificed for them. Understanding the Good is supposed to make that judgment possible. Republic 507b to 509b

Recognizing a standard doesn't, by itself, establish that it exists independently of minds and physical examples. We might explain equality through concepts without accepting Plato's Forms. He explores objections to Forms in another dialogue, the Parmenides, including how one Justice itself could relate to many particular just actions. Plato's objections to Forms

Why the philosophers must go back

Going back means returning to ordinary civic life and governing the imagined city. Plato isn't asking people to move into underground homes. He is asking the people who have learned to understand the good to take responsibility for a community still largely guided by appearances.

The imagined city selects its prospective rulers for character and ability. Their training includes mathematics, questioning assumptions, and years of public service; they are tested before receiving authority. These philosophers have to be able to reason well and resist the temptations of power. Republic 535a to 540b

Glaucon objects. If the philosophers would be happier studying than holding office, why force them into a worse life? Socrates answers that the city educates them for everyone's benefit. People trained at its expense owe it a share of their work. Philosophers elsewhere, who learned without the city's help, don't have the same debt. Republic 519c to 520c

Republic 520c / Benjamin Jowett

“must go down to the general underground abode, and get the habit of seeing in the dark.”

The cave's image now explains a political duty. The philosophers must learn to deal with public affairs after their education, just as the returning prisoner must learn to see in the dark again. Plato values rulers who would prefer something better than office: they have less reason to turn government into a source of private wealth. Republic 520e to 521b

But being well educated and entitled to rule are different claims. Plato gives a selected group authority on the strength of its supposed knowledge of the good. The people they govern don't have an equal political right to reject their judgment. If the rulers mistake their own convictions for knowledge, the same confidence Socrates challenged in his conversations becomes a basis for government.

Earlier, Glaucon asks Socrates to explain the Good itself. Socrates says he can't give the account he wants and offers the comparison with the sun instead. The city requires rulers who understand the Good, but the reader doesn't receive the full account Glaucon asks for. Republic 506d to 507a
