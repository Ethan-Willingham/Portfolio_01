Plato's cave and the death of Socrates

Socrates won't promise to stop asking questions to save his life. In Plato's cave, learning brings another obligation: going back to govern the people still inside.

8 stops through four dialogues / excerpts in Grube and Jowett / 11 min read

Plato's Socrates

In Plato's ideal city, the people who escape the cave must go back and govern. They would rather stay in the daylight. Socrates says their education obliges them to serve the people who made it possible. Republic 519c to 520e

Athens convicted Socrates in 399 BC on charges of impiety and corrupting the young. The Apology is Plato's version of his defense speech. Socrates defends questioning people about how they live, even if the city orders him to stop. In the Crito, he refuses to escape prison because he thinks it would wrong the city. The Phaedo gives him a final conversation about whether the soul survives death.

The Republic describes prisoners who recognize shadows without understanding what produces them. Education turns them toward what Plato calls Forms: realities such as justice and goodness that thought can investigate even when appearances mislead us. He wants the people who understand the good to rule.

We are reading Plato's Socrates throughout. Socrates left no philosophical writings, and Plato, Xenophon, and Aristophanes give us different portraits. Plato's speeches can't be independently checked. The Apology and Crito are often treated as early works; the Phaedo and Republic belong to the usual middle-period grouping. That scholarly model of Plato's development can't reliably separate everything Socrates said from everything Plato added. Plato's development

Stop 1 / Apology 21d

Knowing what you don't know

Apology 21d / Grube, revised by Cooper

"I am wiser than this man; it is likely that neither of us knows anything worthwhile, but he thinks he knows something when he does not, whereas when I do not know, neither do I think I know; so I am likely to be wiser than he to this small extent, that I do not think I know what I do not know."

A friend asks the oracle at Delphi whether anyone is wiser than Socrates. The answer is no. Socrates tells the jury how he tried to find someone who could disprove it. He questioned politicians, poets, and craftsmen. The craftsmen knew their trades; the trouble came when they assumed that made them wise about everything else. Apology 20e to 23b

He tests definitions against a person's other beliefs. In the Euthyphro, a man says that piety is what the gods love. Socrates asks how that can work if the gods disagree. The questioning ends without a satisfactory definition. Euthyphro 7a to 8b

At Apology 29b, though, he says he knows that wrongdoing is shameful, while he doesn't know whether death is bad. His admission of ignorance leaves room for some firm moral judgments.

Did he say “I know that I know nothing”?

That is a popular paraphrase. The actual comparison is narrower: Socrates thinks the man he has questioned mistakes ignorance for knowledge.

JowettApology 21d
"I neither know nor think that I know."

Jowett's sentence concerns what they have been discussing. Socrates isn't claiming that neither man could know anything.

Stop 2 / Apology 30e

An irritating gift to Athens

Apology 30e / Grube, revised by Cooper

"I was attached to this city by the god ... as upon a great and noble horse which was somewhat sluggish because of its size and needed to be stirred up by a kind of gadfly."

The formal accusation says Socrates corrupts the young, fails to acknowledge the city's gods, and introduces new divine beings. He answers Meletus, one of his accusers, by defending his belief in divinity. He says a divine sign warns him away from certain actions. Questioning people is a religious duty as he understands it. Apology 24b to 28a and 31c to 31d

A gadfly bites livestock. Socrates says he does Athens a service by asking why people care so much about wealth and reputation, and so little about becoming good. He offers this account to the citizens who will decide whether to kill him.

The trial followed Athens' defeat in war and the violent rule of the Thirty. Socrates had connections with men who damaged the democracy, including Critias and Alcibiades. That history helps explain suspicion, but doesn't prove the religious charges were a disguised political prosecution. We lack the accusers' speeches. Plato also has Socrates describe refusing the Thirty's order to help seize a man for execution. The trial's setting; Apology 32c to 32e

Stop 3 / Apology 38a

The life he won't give up

Apology 38a / Grube, revised by Cooper

“the unexamined life is not worth living for men”

The jury has found him guilty and must choose a penalty. Socrates first says he deserves public meals in the Prytaneum, an honor given to Olympic victors. He considers exile and imprisonment, then proposes a fine his friends will guarantee. The jury chooses death. Apology 36b to 38b

He explains why he won't agree to live quietly. The examination he means is daily discussion of virtue, testing his own beliefs along with other people's. Virtue concerns being a good person: whether your actions are just, whether courage is different from recklessness, and what deserves your care.

Many people lead generous lives without spending their days in philosophical argument. Socrates doesn't establish here that their lives have no value. He does choose to risk his own rather than abandon the activity he regards as its greatest good. We don't know whether a more conciliatory speech would have saved him.

Stop 4 / Crito 49c to 49d

Refusing an escape

Crito 49c to 49d / Jowett

“Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to anyone, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.”

Crito arrives before dawn with money and friends ready to help Socrates escape. Socrates has children to raise, and his friends will be blamed for failing to save him. Socrates asks whether escape would be just. The city's injustice, he says, cannot by itself justify an injustice in return. Crito 43a to 49d

He gives the Laws of Athens an imaginary speech. They say the city made his upbringing possible, he could have left, and his continued residence amounts to accepting its legal arrangements. If individuals set aside verdicts whenever they dislike them, the courts lose their force. He should persuade the city that it is wrong or accept its decision. Crito 50a to 52d

An escape might protect a victim without doing the harm the Laws describe. Remaining in your birthplace is also a doubtful substitute for freely agreeing to every judgment it issues. Those are weaknesses in this argument even if we accept the principle against retaliation.

In the Apology, Socrates says he would disobey an order to stop philosophizing. One way to reconcile the works is to distinguish obeying a command to do wrong from accepting punishment rather than breaking a commitment. That reading explains his refusal to help the Thirty and his refusal to escape. It leaves the question of when a state's injustice releases someone from an obligation to it. The dispute between the dialogues

Stop 5 / Phaedo 118a

The last conversation

Phaedo 118a / Grube

"Crito, we owe a cock to Asclepius; make this offering to him and do not forget."

Athens permits no executions while its sacred ship travels to Delos. When it returns, Socrates' friends join him in prison. The Phaedo presents the day as Phaedo's later recollection. Its narrator says Plato was ill and absent. Phaedo 58a to 59b

Socrates argues that death separates soul from body and that philosophy prepares the soul for this separation. Simmias compares the soul to the harmony of an instrument, which might disappear when the instrument breaks; Cebes asks whether a soul that outlasts several bodies might eventually perish. His final reply says the soul brings life to a body and therefore cannot admit death. Cebes then accepts that what is deathless is also indestructible, the additional premise needed to establish survival. Phaedo 64c and 85e to 107b

He drinks the poison calmly. His companions weep; he asks them to quiet themselves. As his body grows cold, he reminds Crito of an offering to Asclepius, the god of healing. Some readers take this as thanks for release from bodily life. Gregory Nagy reads it through healing rituals and the survival of philosophical conversation. The dialogue supplies no explanation. Nagy on Socrates' last words

Stop 6 / Republic 514a to 514b

The cave

Republic 514a to 514b / Jowett

“here they have been from their childhood, and have their legs and necks chained so that they cannot move, and can only see before them, being prevented by the chains from turning round their heads. Above and behind them a fire is blazing at a distance”

Socrates asks Glaucon to imagine prisoners who can only face a wall. Behind them, people carry objects above a low barrier, and a fire casts their shadows. The prisoners attribute echoes to the shapes they see. They learn to predict the shapes and honor the people who do it best. Their explanation of what they are seeing is wrong. Republic 514b to 516d

A freed prisoner has to stand, turn, and look toward the fire. He finds the objects harder to recognize than their shadows and wants to return to the familiar view. When he is dragged up into daylight, he is blinded again. He needs time to adjust before he can see the things outside, and eventually the sun.

Coming back, his eyes are no longer accustomed to the dark. The prisoners conclude that the journey damaged him; they would kill anyone trying to free them if they could. This may allude to Socrates' fate. The text doesn't identify the prisoner with Socrates or offer an explanation of his trial. Republic 516e to 517a

Stop 7 / Republic 517b to 517c

What the daylight represents

Republic 517b to 517c / Jowett

"...the prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun ... in the world of knowledge the idea of good appears last of all, and is seen only with an effort."

Plato distinguishes the changing things we perceive from stable realities we can reason about. In the Phaedo, Socrates uses equal sticks and stones: they can appear equal in one comparison and unequal in another, yet we judge them by a standard of equality itself. He argues that this standard cannot be reduced to those objects. Phaedo 74a to 75d

A Form is such a reality, rather than a private thought or an unusually perfect physical object. Justice itself is different from a particular just action. The Republic makes the Good the source of intelligibility and truth, as the sun makes sight possible. “Idea” in Jowett's translation can mislead a modern reader: Plato isn't saying goodness is an opinion in someone's head. Republic 507b to 509b

We might explain shared standards through concepts without accepting separately existing Forms. Plato subjects Forms to objections in the Parmenides. How a Form relates to its instances, and how we could know it, remain difficulties within his own dialogues. Plato's criticism of Forms

Stop 8 / Republic 520c

Going back to govern

Republic 520c / Jowett

“must go down to the general underground abode, and get the habit of seeing in the dark.”

Plato turns from the threat of death to education and government. Learning requires turning the whole soul toward the good. A calculating wrongdoer may see very clearly how to get what he wants. The proposed education must change what a person pursues. Republic 518b to 519a

Glaucon objects to making the philosophers return. If contemplation is their best life, why force them into a worse one? Socrates answers that the city's laws aim at the whole community's welfare. Philosophers trained at its expense must take their turn governing. Philosophers in other cities who received no such education owe no comparable debt. Republic 519c to 520c

The rulers should value something more than office. People hungry for power treat government as a way to enrich themselves; the reluctant philosopher has a reason to resist that temptation. But the proposal gives a selected group authority on the strength of its claimed knowledge of the good. The people they govern have no equal right to decide whether the philosophers really possess it. Republic 520e to 521b

Earlier, Glaucon asks Socrates to explain the Good itself. Socrates says he can't give the account he wants. He offers a comparison with the sun instead. Even within the dialogue, the knowledge that qualifies someone to rule is difficult to put into words. Republic 506d to 507a
