The Sacred Books

The Quran

The Quran moves between the duties of ordinary life and the day everyone will answer to God. Reading its early preaching alongside its later laws helps explain those changes in voice.

20 min read

01 Reading the book

The Quran, in plain English

The chapters are not arranged by date. The broad Meccan and Medinan phases help explain the changes in subject and tone, though the exact sequence is disputed.

The words traditionally identified as the Quran’s first revelation appear in chapter 96. The book itself opens with a seven-verse prayer, followed by its longest chapter, which includes laws for an established community. Its 114 chapters, called suras, run roughly from long to short. A first-time reader meets much of the later material before the early preaching.

The standard Muslim account places the first revelation around 610 CE. Muhammad, a merchant in Mecca, is about forty when the angel Gabriel appears to him in a cave outside the city. The cave scene and his wife Khadija’s reassurance come from later reports, including Sahih al-Bukhari 3. The Quran gives the command to read or recite, but does not tell that scene as a continuous story.

Many suras assigned to early Mecca are short and sharply rhymed. They announce one God, resurrection, and an accounting of every deed. Rain, livestock, sleep, and the growth of a human being become evidence of the creator’s care. The accusations are often specific: neglecting orphans, refusing to feed the poor, hoarding wealth, killing children.

The earlier prophets appear in recurring episodes. Moses confronts Pharaoh. Abraham challenges idols and, elsewhere, raises the sanctuary with Ishmael. Mary gives birth beneath a palm tree; Jesus speaks from the cradle and heals. The Quran calls him Messiah and prophet while rejecting divine sonship. It presents itself as confirming earlier revelation and judging disputes about it.

In the traditional chronology, Muhammad preaches in Mecca until the migration to Medina in 622. This move, the hijra, supplies the starting year of the Muslim calendar. In Medina he leads a religious and political community. The distinction helps explain the text, but it does not date every verse: scholars disagree about particular passages, and some suras contain material assigned to different periods.

The Medinan material includes Ramadan fasting, required giving, marriage and divorce, inheritance, contracts, punishments, and war. It addresses disputes with Jews, Christians, polytheists, and people described as hypocrites within the community. The subject can change from theology to a household rule within a few verses.

The fighting passages vary in scope. Some prohibit aggression or require protection for an enemy seeking refuge. Others command fighting polytheists or People of the Book, particularly Jews and Christians. Later jurists disagreed about the relationship between these commands. Some invoked abrogation, the view that a later revelation replaces an earlier rule.

The traditional account ends with Muhammad’s death in 632, after his return to Mecca and the removal of its sanctuary’s idols. The Quran does not end there. It moves among divine commands, prayers, stories, questions, and replies to opponents. Much of it addresses a listener directly.

Judgment includes bodily resurrection, paradise, and hell. The descriptions are concrete: gardens, shade, food and drink, fire and boiling water. Sura 47:15 puts the two destinations in the same verse.

The promises of mercy are just as explicit. Sura 39:53 tells people who have wronged themselves not to despair of forgiveness. The next verse calls them to repent and surrender before punishment comes.

02 History and transmission

What can actually be known

What the evidence can establish

Early manuscripts support a seventh-century Quran. Two Birmingham leaves contain parts of Suras 18 to 20; radiocarbon testing dated their parchment to 568 to 645 CE with 95.4 percent probability. That dates the animal’s death, not the writing. The early script provides another clue, but the test alone cannot place the copy within Muhammad’s lifetime.

In a study of fourteen early manuscripts, Marijn van Putten found the same unusual spellings at the same locations. That strongly supports descent from a common written exemplar, rather than independent transcription from memory. He argues that the evidence fits standardization near the reign of Uthman, the third caliph. It supports an early common text more directly than any particular compilation story.

What remains a reconstruction

Sahih al-Bukhari 4987, collected in the ninth century, reports that Uthman borrowed sheets from Muhammad’s widow Hafsa, commissioned standard copies, sent them to the provinces, and ordered other Quranic materials burned. This is the traditional account. Shared manuscript spellings cannot verify the borrowing, the committee, or the burn order.

The Sana’a palimpsest, parchment erased and reused, preserves a lower text with wording and chapter order that differ from the standard version. It is the only known surviving manuscript witness to a separate recension, or text form, though scholars debate its history. Within the standard tradition there are also qira’at, accepted readings of the Arabic. Ten are canonical in the dominant Sunni tradition. At 1:4, for example, the Hafs reading has “master” or “owner” of Judgment Day; Warsh has “king.”

How safely did this reach us?

An early common text, with surviving variants.

The date, the wording, and the translation need separate judgments.

Early witnesses Strong evidence

Seventh-century manuscript witnesses survive. Precise dating remains harder than establishing that the text circulated early.

Arabic wording Stable, with variants

Most known early copies share a consonantal text, the letters before full vowel marking. The Sana’a lower text differs more; accepted readings also preserve differences in wording and meaning.

English meaning Interpretation required

Basic commands are often clear in English. Arabic sound and wordplay are harder to carry over, and a disputed word can produce substantially different translations.

03 Expand the ideas

Twelve recurring ideas

Excerpts use Marmaduke Pickthall’s 1930 translation. The notes connect the verses to common practices; they do not describe every Muslim’s life.

Where the details come from

The Quran commands prayer without setting out the full five-prayer routine. Muslims also use hadith, reports about Muhammad’s words and actions; tafsir, Quranic commentary; and fiqh, legal interpretation. Sunni and Shia traditions differ over which reports and authorities to trust. A practice described below may depend on all of these sources.

The five pillars

A familiar Sunni summary names the testimony of faith, prayer, zakat, Ramadan fasting, and the pilgrimage to Mecca. Shia teaching also requires these practices, though it organizes its main duties differently. The Quran commands the hajj for those able to make the journey (3:97); the obligation once in a lifetime is specified in hadith.

01God is one and has no equal.Sura 112 and the Throne Verse

Tawhid means the oneness of God. Sura 112 says that God has no parent, child, or equal. The Throne Verse describes a creator who neither sleeps nor tires. Worship belongs to him alone. Assigning God a partner is shirk, which the Quran treats as a grave wrong. Its warning against unforgiven shirk in 4:48 is read alongside its calls to repent.

In practice The shahada, the testimony of faith, states that there is no god but God and Muhammad is his messenger. The first claim also appears in prayer. Insha’Allah, “if God wills,” is a common phrase when speaking about future plans.

Say: He is Allah, the One! Allah, the eternally Besought of all!Sura 112:1-2

He begetteth not nor was begotten. And there is none comparable unto Him.Sura 112:3-4

Allah! There is no deity save Him, the Alive, the Eternal. Neither slumber nor sleep overtaketh Him.Sura 2:255

02Prayer asks for guidance.The opening prayer, al-Fatiha

Al-Fatiha, the opening chapter, begins with praise and asks God to guide the speakers to the straight path. Its pronouns are plural: we worship, we ask, guide us. The chapter is short enough to learn before much of the rest of the Quran, and its repeated use in prayer makes it familiar even to Muslims who have not memorized the whole book.

In practice In the standard Sunni routine, the five obligatory prayers have two, four, four, three, and four cycles, called rak’at. Al-Fatiha is recited throughout that routine. Rules differ about reciting behind an imam, while Shia practice allows a different recitation in the later cycles.

The path of those whom Thou hast favoured; Not the (path) of those who earn Thine anger nor of those who go astray.Sura 1:7

03Give thanks and remember God.Gratitude, loss, and remembrance

The Quran repeatedly connects disbelief with ingratitude. The Arabic root behind kufr, disbelief, also carries meanings of covering and denying a benefit. The surrounding verse determines the sense; disbelief and ordinary thanklessness are not interchangeable everywhere. Here the connection is direct: Sura 14:7 promises more to those who give thanks. Sura 103 joins belief to good work, truthfulness, and endurance.

In practice Dhikr means remembrance of God. It includes repeating names and phrases such as alhamdulillah, “praise belongs to God.” Muslims say this in prayer and ordinary conversation, including when expressing thanks.

If ye give thanks, I will give you more; but if ye are thankless, lo! My punishment is dire.Sura 14:7

Therefore remember Me, I will remember you. Give thanks to Me, and reject not Me.Sura 2:152

Rivalry in worldly increase distracteth youSura 102:1

04Prayer and fasting are regular obligations.Prayer and Ramadan

The Quran commands prayer at appointed times and says it serves remembrance of God (4:103; 20:14). It prescribes fasting to cultivate taqwa, often translated as piety or consciousness of God. These are regular obligations. The movements, recitations, and detailed timetable of prayer depend on prophetic practice and later interpretation as well as the Quran.

In practice Muslims pray five prescribed prayers each day. During Ramadan, those obliged and able to fast abstain from food, drink, and sexual activity from dawn to sunset. Illness and travel can excuse the fast, with missed days made up later. Other exemptions have their own rules.

05Giving is part of righteousness.Zakat, charity, and Sura 2:177

Sura 2:177 begins with prayer direction, then describes righteousness through belief, generosity, prayer, promises, and endurance. The recipients are named: relatives, orphans, poor people, travelers, those who ask, and enslaved people whose freedom can be bought. Elsewhere the Quran criticizes worshippers who neglect the orphan and the hungry (Sura 107). Ritual and treatment of other people are judged together.

In practice Zakat is obligatory giving from qualifying wealth; sadaqa commonly refers to voluntary charity. Eligibility, rates, and collection rules differ by type of wealth and legal tradition. The Quran names recipients in 9:60, while many details come from hadith and law.

Ye will not attain unto piety until ye spend of that which ye love.Sura 3:92

06Justice includes your family and your enemies.Witness, judgment, and enemies

The instructions address two familiar reasons to bend a verdict: loyalty to family and hatred of an enemy. Sura 4:135 requires truthful witness even against yourself or your parents. Sura 5:8 forbids hatred from leading to injustice. Sura 16:90 pairs adl, justice, with ihsan, doing good or acting with excellence. Pickthall translates the pair as justice and kindness.

In practice These verses are used in Muslim discussions of courts, government, and personal conduct. Sura 16:90 is also commonly recited in Friday sermons. They state an obligation; the conduct of any particular ruler or community needs its own evidence.

O ye who believe! Be steadfast witnesses for Allah in equity, and let not hatred of any people seduce you that ye deal not justly. Deal justly, that is nearer to your duty.Sura 5:8

Lo! Allah enjoineth justice and kindness, and giving to kinsfolk, and forbiddeth lewdness and abomination and wickedness.Sura 16:90

07Repentance remains possible, with conditions.Mercy and the return to God

Tawba means repentance or turning back to God. Sura 39:53 tells people who have wronged themselves not to despair, and 39:54 immediately calls them to return and submit. Forgiveness has conditions: 4:18 rejects repentance postponed until death arrives. The promise of mercy is substantial, but it does not say that every action will be forgiven automatically.

In practice Astaghfirullah means “I ask God’s forgiveness.” Muslim teaching commonly describes repentance as stopping the wrong, regretting it, resolving not to repeat it, and making restitution when another person has been harmed.

O My slaves who have been prodigal to their own hurt! Despair not of the mercy of Allah, Who forgiveth all sins.Sura 39:53

In the name of Allah, the Beneficent, the Merciful.Sura 1:1

The good deed and the evil deed are not alike. Repel the evil deed with one which is better,Sura 41:34

08Muhammad stands at the end of one line of prophets.Abraham, Moses, Mary, Jesus, Muhammad

The Quran identifies its God with the God of Abraham, Moses, and Jesus. It affirms earlier revelation while challenging claims made about it. Muhammad is called the seal of the prophets, understood in mainstream Islam as the final prophet. Jesus is Messiah and a messenger, born of Mary, but not divine. Sura 4:157-158 also denies that his enemies killed or crucified him and says God took him up. The disagreement with Christianity includes the crucifixion as well as divine sonship.

In practice Earlier prophets appear in Muslim names, teaching, and prayer. Muhammad’s name is often followed by a blessing asking for peace upon him. This honors him as a messenger; worship is reserved for God.

09The Arabic text is recited and memorized.Sound, memory, and the Arabic text

Quran means recitation or reading. Rhyme and repeated sounds are part of its form, and worship uses the Arabic text. Muslims therefore distinguish the Quran from a translation of its meanings. The text also challenges doubters to produce a sura like it (2:23). Muslim theology develops this into i’jaz, the doctrine that the Quran is inimitable and evidence of revelation. That is a religious claim, rather than a result manuscript dating can establish.

In practice Tajwid is the system of rules for Quranic recitation. A hafiz has memorized the whole text. In many Sunni mosques, additional Ramadan night prayers include a complete recitation across the month. The amount and prayer practice vary.

10Faith creates duties to God and to other people.Mutual care, counsel, and correction

Sura 9:71 describes believing men and women as allies who encourage right and resist wrong. Sura 42:38 praises consultation in shared affairs. These obligations reach beyond private devotion. What they authorize is a further question: Muslim legal traditions have debated who may correct others, by what means, and under whose authority.

In practice Congregational prayer, shared Ramadan meals, funerals, giving, and pilgrimage bring people together. Their organization varies across Sunni and Shia communities and across countries. These verses do not prescribe one complete system of government.

11Ancestry does not determine worth before God.Peoples, religions, and good works

Sura 49:13 says peoples and tribes should know one another, and places honor before God in taqwa, piety or consciousness of him. It rejects ancestry as the measure of worth. Sura 5:48 describes communities with different laws and tells them to compete in good works. Those passages sit alongside exclusive claims for Islam; their implications for other religions are disputed.

In practice Sura 60:8 permits kindness and just dealing with people who neither fought Muslims for their religion nor expelled them. It gives a concrete instruction about relations with peaceful outsiders. The separate question of who is saved is discussed below.

O mankind! Lo! We have created you male and female, and have made you nations and tribes that ye may know one another. Lo! the noblest of you, in the sight of Allah, is the best in conduct.Sura 49:13

Had Allah willed He could have made you one community. But that He may try you by that which He hath given you … So vie one with another in good works.Sura 5:48

Allah forbiddeth you not those who warred not against you on account of religion … that ye should show them kindness and deal justly with them.Sura 60:8

12Judgment includes even the smallest deeds.Resurrection, responsibility, and God’s will

The Quran promises resurrection and a judgment in which even the smallest deeds count. Pickthall’s “atom” renders dharra, a tiny particle, rather than a claim about modern physics. The murdered girl in Sura 81 is asked why she was killed. The descriptions include bodily reward and punishment, not merely a guilty or peaceful conscience.

In practice Al-Fatiha names the Day of Judgment during prayer. Funeral prayers ask God to forgive the dead. Alongside personal responsibility, the Quran repeatedly states that guidance and human willing depend on God; how those claims fit together is a major theological dispute.

Shared themes, different traditions

These themes recur across Sunni and Shia teaching, with differences in authority, law, and practice. Sufism, Islamic traditions of spiritual discipline and mysticism, overlaps with both. The selection above gives more space to ethics and ordinary worship than to mystical readings or the details of law.

04 Do not skip

The difficult passages

The verses, the rules later jurists drew from them, and the disputes over those readings.

Fighting, treaties, and tribute

Sura 2:190 commands fighting those who fight you and prohibits aggression. Sura 9:4-6 protects unbroken treaties and requires safe passage for a polytheist seeking protection, but also commands killing polytheists after the sacred months. Sura 9:29 commands fighting specified People of the Book until they pay jizya, tribute under Muslim rule. Classical Sunni law commonly authorized war to expand that rule. Some modern interpreters argue that the commands apply only to aggression and treaty violations. That reading is disputed, including by scholars who reject modern militant interpretations.

Men have greater authority in family law

Sura 33:35 promises men and women the same spiritual reward. In 4:11, a son receives twice a daughter’s share when they inherit together; this is not a formula for every inheritance case. Sura 4:3 permits up to four wives, conditional on just treatment, and directs a man who fears injustice to marry one. Sura 4:34 gives men authority tied to financial support and includes a disciplinary verb Pickthall renders “scourge them.” Premodern commentators generally understood striking, with restrictions. Some modern interpreters reject that meaning; others retain it or prohibit its use through broader arguments against harm.

Slavery and concubinage remain permitted

The Quran praises freeing enslaved people and requires it as compensation for some wrongs. It also recognizes ownership of people. Classical jurists read 23:5-6 and 4:24 as permitting men sexual access to enslaved women, including some married captives. This became the law of concubinage, sexual relations based on ownership rather than marriage. Modern descriptions that insert mutual consent into that legal entitlement misrepresent the classical rule. The commands to free people did not amount to a general abolition of slavery.

Lashes and amputation are prescribed

Sura 24:2 prescribes one hundred lashes for zina, unlawful sexual intercourse. Sura 5:38 prescribes cutting off a thief’s hand. Later law defined qualifying offenses and evidence, and developed rules for avoiding fixed penalties when doubt existed. The classical stoning penalty for adultery draws on hadith, including Muslim 1690a, rather than the wording of 24:2. A description of these punishments needs both the verses and the legal conditions; neither alone describes the whole system.

“No compulsion” and apostasy law

Sura 2:256 says there is no compulsion in religion. The Quran condemns abandoning belief but sets out no explicit earthly penalty for apostasy, leaving the faith. Bukhari 3017 reports a command to kill a Muslim who discards his religion. Most classical jurists prescribed death for at least some categories of apostate, differing over women, repentance, and other conditions. Some modern Muslim scholars reject this penalty or confine it to political betrayal. The freedom verse has not produced one agreed rule.

Who can be saved?

Sura 2:62 promises reward to believers, Jews, Christians, and Sabaeans who believe in God and the Last Day and do right. Sura 5:48 tells communities to compete in good works. Sura 3:85 says a religion other than islam, surrender to God, will not be accepted. Interpreters dispute whether 2:62 refers to earlier faithful communities, people who accept Muhammad, or a wider possibility of salvation. Translating islam as “surrender” does not by itself settle that dispute.

People choose, and God controls their willing

Sura 76:29 invites whoever wills to take a way to God. The next verse says that people do not will unless God wills. Elsewhere the Quran holds people responsible for their deeds and says God guides or leaves astray whom he wills. Muslim theologians have proposed different accounts of human freedom and divine decree, often discussed as qadar.
