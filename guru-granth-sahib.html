The Sacred Books

The Guru Granth Sahib

Sikhs bow before this book as their Guru. Its hymns speak of loving God, losing the ego, and living honestly among other people.

First compiled in 1604/20 min read

01What the book is

Hymns received as Guru

The Guru Granth Sahib is opened in the morning and put to rest at night. In a gurdwara, a Sikh place of worship, people bow before it and listen to its hymns. Sikhs receive it as the eternal Guru, the continuing spiritual authority after the ten human Gurus. Its contents are bani, sacred utterance, mostly composed as poetry for singing.

The standard volume has 1,430 angs, the numbered pages whose name means “limbs.” It opens with Ik Oankar, the One, and the Mul Mantar, the opening statement of divine reality. Japji, Guru Nanak’s morning prayer, follows. Then come So Dar, So Purakh, and Sohila, compositions used in evening and bedtime prayer.

Most of what follows is arranged under thirty-one main ragas, melodic frameworks for singing. Within them, headings identify authors, verse forms, and musical directions. Near the end are shorter verses called saloks, praise poems, and compositions outside the main raga sequence. The final piece is Ragmala, a list of ragas whose status Sikhs dispute. This is an anthology: a hymn can stand on its own, and the same concerns return in different voices. Structure and contents

The contributors include the first five Sikh Gurus and the ninth, Guru Tegh Bahadur. Alongside them are fifteen Bhagats, devotional poets, eleven Bhatts, praise poets, and other Sikh contributors. Kabir, Sheikh Farid, Ravidas, and Namdev came from different religious and social backgrounds. Their presence gives those voices scriptural authority within Sikh tradition; it doesn’t make their every belief identical. The Gurus also use the name “Nanak” in their verses. An author heading identifies which Guru is speaking.

Guru Arjan supervised the compilation of the Adi Granth in 1604, with Bhai Gurdas as scribe, and installed it at Harmandir Sahib in Amritsar. Guru Tegh Bahadur’s compositions entered the later form associated with Guru Gobind Singh. Sikh tradition locates the conferral of enduring Guruship at Nanded in 1708. It also recognizes the Guru Panth, the committed, initiated Sikh community acting collectively, as a bearer of authority. SGPC account

Morning

Prakash

The volume is ceremonially opened beneath a canopy. A hymn is read as the day’s hukam, guidance received from the Guru.

Gathered life

Kirtan and hukam

The sangat, congregation, gathers for kirtan, the singing of hymns, and readings. The Guru Granth Sahib also has a central place in naming children, weddings, and funerals.

Evening

Sukhasan

The volume is closed, covered, and placed at rest. A separate resting room is common, though arrangements vary with the space available.

This care expresses reverence for the Guru’s shabad, the divine Word or teaching. The Sikh conduct code specifies how the volume should be attended and also rejects superstitious ceremonies. Following the teaching is part of that reverence. Sikh Rehat Maryada

02Text and translation

How the hymns reached us

The hymns circulated in singing and written collections before the compilation of 1604. The Goindval pothis, manuscript collections associated with Guru Amar Das’s family, are among the early witnesses. Surviving manuscripts differ in wording and arrangement. Jeevan Singh Deol’s study describes concurrent oral repertoires and the later emergence of more standardized written versions. Text and lineage

The Kartarpur manuscript is closely associated with Guru Arjan’s compilation. Scholars have disagreed over aspects of its production and the relationship between early collections. Access to important manuscripts is limited. The broad compilation history is easier to establish than an account of every change to every hymn. Pashaura Singh’s study of canon formation is one influential scholarly account, rather than an uncontested verdict.

The received volume

The standard text includes Guru Tegh Bahadur’s bani. The traditional account says Guru Gobind Singh dictated the final recension at Damdama. The details of that account are harder to verify than the contents of the canon Sikhs now use.

Ragmala remains printed at the end. The Sikh conduct code allows a complete reading to end at Mundavani or include Ragmala, according to local convention. It also forbids printing a volume that omits Ragmala. The disagreement concerns its scriptural status, not whether it appears in the standard edition.

Gurmukhi and English

Gurmukhi is the script. The hymns use several North Indian literary languages and registers, including Punjabi and Braj forms, with Persian, Arabic, and Sanskrit vocabulary. Translating them requires decisions about grammar, imagery, and words whose meanings shift with context.

English “Name” only partly explains naam; “command” only partly explains hukam. The Guru Granth Sahib Project distinguishes literal translation from interpretive “transcreation.” The excerpts below use Sant Singh Khalsa’s English rendering. They give one reading of each line. The translation problem

How safely did this reach us?

The standard text is stable. Early manuscript history and English interpretation need separate judgments.

Manuscript evidence, present wording, and translation.

Early witnesses Strong, qualified

The Kartarpur volume is associated with the compilation of 1604, and earlier collections survive. The relationships between manuscripts and the stages of compilation remain subjects of scholarship and dispute.

Received wording Highly stable now

Worship and publication use a standardized 1,430-ang text. That present stability should not be projected back onto every early manuscript. Ragmala is printed even where it is not recited.

English meaning Deeply interpretive

Love of the Divine, remembrance, and truthful living are recurring concerns. Compressed verse and terms such as naam, shabad, haumai, and hukam leave substantial room for interpretation.

These judgments draw on the Oxford study of canon, meaning, and authority, the study of concurrent early repertoires and recensions, the linguistic and philological account, and the practice and translation sources below. They concern the transmission and interpretation of the text. Revelation is a religious claim.

03What shapes a Sikh life

The recurring teachings

The hymns return to these concerns across different authors and compositions. The passages below are a selection, with links to their context.

Much of this book speaks in the voice of someone in love. The speaker longs for the Divine, feels the pain of separation, and rejoices in meeting the beloved. A recurring image makes the human being a bride and the Divine a husband. Men as well as women occupy the bride’s voice. In Guru Nanak’s hymn on ang 765, finding the beloved becomes recognition that the One is present in every heart. Read the hymn and commentary

This is bhakti, devotion. Honest work and service matter within a life directed toward the Divine. A summary of the ethics can explain what a person should do and still miss whom the poetry loves.

01The One is present throughout creation.Ik Oankar

Ik Oankar opens the book. The Mul Mantar describes the One as true, creative, without fear or hatred, timeless, beyond birth, self-existent, and known through the Guru’s grace. Kabir draws a social consequence from that oneness: everyone comes from the same Light. Religious identity or inherited rank cannot give a person a different divine origin.

In practice The hymns address the Divine through names including Hari, Ram, and Allah. Sikh worship centers on the One; that devotion carries an obligation to recognize the same Light in other people.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

One Universal Creator God. The Name Is Truth. Creative Being Personified. No Fear. No Hatred.Mul Mantar, ang 1

From the One Light, the entire universe welled up. So who is good, and who is bad?Kabir, ang 1349

02Live within hukam.Divine order and human action

Japji asks how a person becomes truthful. Its answer is to live within hukam, divine command or order. Everything exists within it, including things the self cannot control. The following verse connects understanding hukam with the end of speaking in ego. Translating this as acceptance captures part of the idea, but hukam is also a claim about the Divine and creation.

In practice The hymn received from the opened Guru Granth Sahib is also called a hukam, or hukamnama. Sikhs listen to it for guidance. Accepting divine order has to coexist with the duties to work, serve, and resist injustice.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

O Nanak, it is written that you shall obey the Hukam of His Command, and walk in the Way of His Will.Japji, ang 1

O Nanak, one who understands His Command, does not speak in ego.Japji, ang 1

03Remember the Divine through naam.The Name, remembrance, and presence

Naam means Name. In the hymns it can refer to the Divine and divine presence as well as the Name uttered in devotion. In Sukhmani, Guru Arjan describes naam as sustaining creatures and worlds. Remembrance is the practice associated with it; translating naam simply as attention loses that larger claim. Sukhmani, ang 284

In practice Simran, remembrance of the Divine, includes repeating or singing the Name and holding it in mind during daily life. The hymns describe this with love, gratitude, and longing. The relationship with the One matters as much as the discipline of returning attention.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

Those who have meditated on the Naam, the Name of the Lord, and departed after having worked by the sweat of their browsJapji closing salok, ang 8

O Nanak, if I am blessed with the Naam, I live, and my body and mind blossom forth.Guru Arjan, ang 1429

04The Guru teaches through shabad.The Word and the disciple

Shabad means Word and also refers to a hymn. Guru Nanak calls the Shabad Guru and his attentive consciousness the disciple. Guru Ram Das identifies bani with Guru. These lines help explain why Sikhs receive the Granth as their continuing Guru. A recurrent distinction is between the gurmukh, oriented toward the Guru, and the manmukh, governed by the self.

In practice Sikhs approach the teaching through recitation, singing, explanation, and reflection. Bowing before the Guru Granth Sahib accompanies a commitment to learn from it and follow its teaching.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

The Shabad is the Guru, upon whom I lovingly focus my consciousness; I am the chaylaa, the disciple.Guru Nanak, ang 943

The Word, the Bani is Guru, and Guru is the Bani.Guru Ram Das, ang 982

05Haumai keeps the self at the center.Ego and the five thieves

Haumai, often translated as ego, is the self-centered condition that separates a person from recognition of the One. The traditional five thieves are lust, anger, greed, attachment, and pride. The hymns describe how these bind a person to desire and repeated suffering. Guru Angad calls ego a chronic disease and says its cure is also found within it; the next lines locate that cure in grace and practicing the Guru’s Word.

In practice Remembrance, service, sharing, and willingness to accept correction work against self-importance. Religious activity can also become a source of pride. The hymns repeatedly criticize that failure.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

Ego is a chronic disease, but it contains its own cure as well.Guru Angad, ang 466

On the twelfth day, the five thieves are subdued, and then, O Nanak, the mind is pleased and appeased.Guru Amar Das, ang 1245

06Liberation depends on grace.Gurprasad and nadar

The Mul Mantar ends with gurprasad, by the Guru’s grace. Japji distinguishes the embodied life shaped by karma from liberation through nadar, the gracious divine glance. Effort matters, but the teaching does not present release as something earned through a sufficient number of prayers or good deeds.

In practice Sikh worship includes disciplined daily prayer and Ardas, the congregational or personal prayer of petition. Asking for grace acknowledges a dependence that discipline alone cannot remove.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

Beyond Birth, Self-Existent. By Guru’s GraceMul Mantar, ang 1

By the karma of past actions, the robe of this physical body is obtained. By His Grace, the Gate of Liberation is found.Japji, ang 2

07Live truthfully.Conduct and religious observance

Guru Nanak puts truthful living above truth itself. Other hymns recast familiar rituals: compassion becomes a mosque, and the sky becomes the plate for a lamp offering. These images ask what worship means when conduct contradicts it. The criticism falls on observance used to conceal dishonesty or claim superiority.

In practice The question follows a person out of the gurdwara: how was the money earned, how were others treated, and were promises kept? Sikh prayer and ceremony belong alongside those obligations.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

Truth is higher than everything; but higher still is truthful living.Guru Nanak, ang 62

That which is pleasing to Him is the lamp-lit worship service.Guru Nanak, ang 13

08Earn honestly and share.Kirat karni and vand chhakna

A common summary of Sikh life joins remembrance of the Name with kirat karni, honest work, and vand chhakna, sharing what one has. Guru Nanak’s line on ang 1245 links earning a living to giving. The familiar three-part formulation is a summary of the teachings, rather than a numbered list presented in the Granth.

In practice Sharing includes supporting the community kitchen, giving time, and helping people in need. Dasvandh, giving a tenth of earnings, is a traditional discipline also specified in the Sikh conduct code. It should not be confused with a percentage commanded in the quoted hymn.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

One who works for what he eats, and gives some of what he has
O Nanak, he knows the Path.Guru Nanak, ang 1245

Those who have meditated on the Naam, the Name of the Lord, and departed after having worked by the sweat of their browsGuru Nanak, ang 8

09Serve other people.Seva and langar

Seva is service undertaken without seeking personal reward. It includes cooking, carrying water, washing dishes, and caring for people. Guru Arjan sings of carrying water and grinding grain for the Guru’s household. Langar, the free community kitchen, makes service and equal access part of the same institution.

In practice People volunteer to prepare and serve food, clean up, and maintain the gurdwara. Langar is open to people of any faith. Serving and eating together put the teaching about equality into an ordinary shared activity.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

you shall find peace, doing seva (selfless service).Guru Nanak, ang 25

I carry water for the Guru’s household; … I grind the corn for the Guru’s household.Guru Arjan, ang 239

10Gather and sing together.Sangat and kirtan

Sangat is the congregation; sadh sangat is holy company. The hymns urge the listener to seek such company and remember the Divine there. Kirtan gives that gathering a common activity: people hear and sing the same words. A rahau, or pause marker, often identifies the line that carries a hymn’s central thought.

In practice A gurdwara gathering may include kirtan, katha, explanation of the teaching, Ardas, a hukam, and langar. Participation includes listening and learning as well as helping with the work.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

Join the Saadh Sangat, the Company of the Holy; vibrate and meditate on the Jewel of the Naam.Guru Arjan, ang 12

Sing, and listen, and let your mind be filled with love.Japji, ang 2

11Live the spiritual life at home.The household and detachment

The Sikh path places spiritual practice within work and family life. Guru Amar Das asks the mind to remain detached while living in a household. Detachment here concerns the attachment that makes possessions and relationships a source of bondage. It does not require abandoning the people who depend on you.

In practice Earning, caring for children, and keeping a household are compatible with devotion. The conduct code includes altruistic service among a Sikh’s personal duties. Renunciation is not the required route to liberation.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

O mind, remain detached in the midst of your household.Guru Amar Das, ang 26

Even as householders, they lovingly attune themselves to the True Lord.Guru Amar Das, ang 230

12Caste cannot determine spiritual worth.One Light in every person

Guru Nanak tells the listener to recognize the divine Light rather than ask about caste. He also identifies himself with the lowest of the low. The book includes Ravidas, whose community was treated as untouchable. His hymns have scriptural authority alongside those of the Gurus.

In practice The Sikh conduct code rejects caste discrimination in the congregation and langar. Women and men may read scripture, lead prayer, receive initiation, and serve. These are religious commitments whose observance in Sikh institutions and homes has been uneven.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

Recognize the Lord’s Light within all, and do not consider social class or status; there are no classes or castes in the world hereafter.Guru Nanak, ang 349

Nanak seeks the company of the lowest of the low class, the very lowest of the low.Guru Nanak, ang 15

13Neither frighten others nor submit to fear.Fearlessness and responsibility

The Mul Mantar describes the One as without fear and without hatred. Guru Tegh Bahadur praises a person who neither frightens others nor fears them. Farid asks the listener not to strike back at someone who strikes them. These passages belong in an account of Sikh courage, alongside the later history of armed resistance and defense.

In practice The kirpan, the sword carried by initiated Sikhs, expresses an obligation to defend justice and the vulnerable. Its place in the Khalsa, the initiated Sikh community, comes from Guru Gobind Singh’s institution of that community. The Granth alone does not supply its full rules or history.

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

One who does not frighten anyone, and who is not afraid of anyone else … says Nanak, listen, mind: call him spiritually wise.Guru Tegh Bahadur, ang 1427

Fareed, do not turn around and strike those who strike you with their fists.Sheikh Farid, ang 1378

14Freedom is possible while alive.Karma, rebirth, and jivan mukti

The hymns speak of karma, repeated birth and death, and mukti, liberation from that cycle. They also describe jivan mukti, liberation while living. Freedom from greed, attachment, and the opposition of friend and enemy is part of that state. Union with the One gives these changes their religious meaning; being calmer or kinder is only part of what is claimed.

In practice Guru Arjan presents human life as an opportunity to meet the Divine. The evening prayer on ang 12 warns against wasting that opportunity in attachment to maya, the worldly entanglement that draws a person away from the One. Death makes the opportunity urgent. Guru Arjan, ang 12

From the hymnsSant Singh Khalsa

One who is not affected by pleasure or pain, who looks upon friend and enemy alike … says Nanak, listen, mind: know that such a person is liberated.Guru Tegh Bahadur, ang 1427

Meeting the True Guru, eternal liberation is obtained; He has banished attachment from within.Guru Nanak, ang 466

04Questions and limits

Where teaching and history differ

Caste

The hymns reject inherited spiritual superiority. Sikh society has nevertheless retained caste-based marriage, status, and discrimination, including caste-associated gurdwaras. Jodhka’s study shows why describing this only as a failure to follow the teaching is inadequate: kinship, land, and social power also sustain caste. Scriptural equality gives Sikhs grounds to challenge those arrangements; it has not by itself removed them. Caste study

Gender

Guru Nanak asks why woman should be called bad when kings are born through her. The canon’s named composers are men. Its bride imagery lets a male speaker take a feminine devotional role, while drawing on a marriage world that was socially unequal. The conduct code permits women to read scripture, sing kirtan, and receive initiation. Religious permission and actual authority in institutions or households are different questions. Ang 473; Gender and householding

Other faiths

The book includes poets from different religious backgrounds and addresses the Divine as Allah and Ram. It also criticizes Hindu, Muslim, and yogic observances. That combination does not establish that every religion teaches the same thing. Sikhism has its own account of the Guru and liberation. The conduct code welcomes visitors of any faith into a gurdwara while defining Sikh religious allegiance distinctly. Conduct code

Violence

The Granth condemns oppression and praises courage. To understand armed defense, the kirpan, and the saint-soldier ideal, a reader also needs the history of the Gurus and other Sikh texts. Guru Gobind Singh established the Khalsa after the original compilation of the Adi Granth. A hymn about overcoming the ego cannot automatically be read as a rule for warfare. Nor does Farid’s counsel against retaliation settle the whole tradition’s position on force. Sikhism and war

Hukam

If everything falls within divine order, why protest suffering? Guru Nanak does protest. In a hymn about Babur’s invasion, he describes the killing and asks God about the victims’ pain. The text itself gives grief and accusation a voice. Hukam therefore needs to be read alongside the demands for truthful action and care for others. It should not be used to declare that victims deserved what happened. Guru Nanak, ang 360

Text

The authority Sikhs give the Guru Granth Sahib is a religious commitment. Manuscript comparison asks how particular written forms developed. Present-day standardization is well established; it does not settle every question about earlier collections. Claims about editorial changes should identify the manuscript and the scholar making the argument, especially where scholars or Sikh communities dispute the interpretation.

This selection gives only short English excerpts. It cannot reproduce a complete hymn’s movement, its sound in Gurmukhi, or its place in worship. The linked passages let you read the surrounding lines. Listening to kirtan from Harmandir Sahib brings back something a written explanation leaves out.
