The Dhammapada

An early Buddhist book about how we act, why we suffer, and what freedom would require.

01 Summary

The Dhammapada, in plain English

The Dhammapada compares someone who recites teachings without practicing them to a cowherd counting someone else's cattle. You can know the words and still have none of their benefits. The book asks what changes in the way you live. Verses 19-20

This is a collection of 423 verses in 26 chapters, preserved in Pali, a language of early Buddhist scripture. It belongs to the canon of Theravada, one of the main Buddhist traditions. The verses are attributed to the Buddha, but he didn't write this book. Buddhist communities passed the teachings on and collected them by subject: the mind, anger, old age, happiness, craving.

The advice ranges from how to speak to someone who's angry to how to end rebirth. Some passages address ordinary household life. Others address monks who have left it behind.

02 Context and transmission

How this text reached us

The Dhammapada belongs to an oral tradition. Related collections survive in other Buddhist languages, with different selections and arrangements of verses. The Pali book is one received version. Its exact date of compilation is uncertain, and its attribution to the Buddha doesn't give us a transcript of a single occasion. Thanissaro's historical notes

A later Pali commentary supplies stories about the occasions behind the verses. These stories matter to traditional reading, but they're a separate layer. When verses 153-154 are presented as the Buddha's words at awakening, that setting comes from the commentary. The verses themselves describe repeated birth and the ending of craving. We can read what they say more confidently than we can reconstruct when each was first spoken.

03 The teaching

What the book teaches

01 What you do matters

Verse 183 gives the book's own short account of the teaching: avoid harmful action, develop what is good, and purify your mind. The last part means working on the greed, hatred, and confusion that shape your choices. Being outwardly well behaved while nursing those habits leaves the work unfinished. Verse 183

The moral instructions are concrete. Don't kill or cause others to kill. Don't steal, lie, or commit sexual misconduct. Avoid intoxicants that undermine care and restraint. The reason for avoiding violence is especially direct: other beings fear pain and death, just as you do. Verses 129-130; 246-247

The book assumes karma, called kamma in Pali: intentional action has moral consequences, including consequences beyond the present life. Verses 15-18 describe wrongdoing and good action bearing fruit both now and after death. These are religious claims within the text. They go beyond the ordinary observation that habits affect your life. Verses 15-18

02 The mind can be trained

The opening pair of verses connects the mind you act from with what follows. Speech and action rooted in a corrupt mind bring suffering; speech and action rooted in a clear mind bring happiness. The images are a cartwheel following the animal that pulls it, and a shadow following a person. The verses concern the moral quality of action. They don't establish that thinking alone controls everything that happens to you. Verses 1-2

Chapter 3 describes a mind that wanders, resists restraint, and is difficult to guard. An arrow-maker straightens an arrow; a practitioner works on the mind. The book treats this as something learned through effort. It praises heedfulness, sustained care about what you're doing and where it leads. Verses 33-36; 21-23

That includes how you handle anger. Verses 3-4 repeat a person's grievances: someone insulted me, hurt me, defeated me, robbed me. Holding onto that account keeps hostility alive. Verse 5 says further hatred won't settle it. Translators use words such as non-hatred, non-hostility, and love for the alternative. The practical direction is consistent: don't keep the conflict going by answering hatred with more of it. Verses 3-5

03 Why getting what you want isn't enough

The book repeatedly returns to craving, taṇhā in Pali. It's the thirst that keeps reaching for pleasure or a particular kind of existence. Verse 216 connects it with sorrow and fear. If your peace depends on keeping something you can lose, the fear of losing it comes with the pleasure of having it. Verse 216

The Buddhist word dukkha is usually translated as suffering. It includes obvious pain, but the teaching also concerns the instability of experiences we enjoy. Verses 277-278 say that things arising through conditions are impermanent and subject to dukkha. A pleasant experience can be real and still fail to provide lasting security. Verses 277-278

The book encourages learning, effort, generosity, and friendship with wise people. Its target is the craving that keeps a person attached and dissatisfied. Working toward freedom is itself something the book asks you to do. Verse 76; 223; 276

04 What the way out involves

Verses 190-192 name the four truths that organize Buddhist teaching: suffering, its origin, its ending, and the path to that ending. The longer discourse SN 56.11 explains the origin as craving and the ending as release from it. The Dhammapada assumes much of that background rather than teaching every step from scratch.

It also names the noble eightfold path. The eight parts work together. Right view and intention concern understanding and purpose. Right speech, action, and livelihood concern how you treat others and earn your living. Right effort, mindfulness, and concentration concern the training of attention and the mind. The full definitions are in SN 45.8. Verses 273-276

The goal is nibbāna, better known in English by the Sanskrit form nirvana. In this book, freedom means ending the craving that sustains suffering and repeated birth. Verses 153-154 describe a search through many lives for a house-builder, followed by broken rafters, a shattered ridgepole, and the end of craving. The traditional explanation identifies craving as the builder. Verses 153-154; 348

Chapter 25 speaks directly to monks, and verse 185 recommends moderation in eating, a secluded dwelling, and meditation. Other passages praise contentment, caring for parents, and good companions. The text offers guidance within ordinary life while holding up release from rebirth as its final aim. Verse 185; 204; 332

05 What “not-self” means here

Verse 279 introduces anattā, usually translated as not-self. It changes the wording of the two preceding verses: where they speak of conditioned things, it speaks of all things. The claim is that nothing can be identified as a permanent self. It doesn't say that the world is unreal, or that your actions don't matter. Verse 279

A related discourse makes the question concrete. Can you make your body stay exactly as you wish? Can you keep a feeling from changing? In SN 22.59, the Buddha applies this reasoning to the body and the processes of experience. Their changeability is a reason to stop treating them as a lasting identity. Buddhist interpreters differ over how best to explain not-self; a short verse doesn't settle every philosophical question about it.

The final chapter uses brahmin, the name of a hereditary priestly status, for someone who has achieved spiritual freedom. Verse 393 rejects birth and outward appearance as sufficient qualifications. Verse 396 says ancestry doesn't make someone worthy of the name. The chapter judges the person by freedom from attachment. Verses 393 and 396

Sources and translation notes

The explanations use the Pali text and Bhikkhu Sujato's translation as their base, with comparisons against the other editions below. Sujato's text is dedicated to the public domain. The explanations are newly written summaries.

For context, see Bhikkhu Bodhi's introduction and Thanissaro Bhikkhu's introduction, historical notes, and endnotes. The Pali Text Society lists the commentary as anonymous; its traditional attribution to Buddhaghosa is not certain.
