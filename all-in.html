Leading a team

All In: Managing Professionals

You can require someone to do the job. Earning their best work takes more: clear expectations, useful feedback, and enough trust for them to use the judgment you hired them for.

scroll

Being good at a job doesn't tell you how to manage the people doing it. An engineer can design a system and struggle to coach another engineer. A good analyst can become the bottleneck for an entire team by checking every decision.

The work changes when you become a manager. You choose priorities, help people improve, and take responsibility for what the team delivers. Much of that depends on expertise you don't have. These sections draw on management research and a few useful books, with a development guide at the end.

Four terms used here

1

Professional (knowledge worker)

Someone whose work depends on specialized knowledge and judgment: an engineer, analyst, designer, or customer success manager. Peter Drucker called them knowledge workers.

2

Engagement

How involved someone feels in their work and how much they care about doing it well.

3

Discretionary effort

The extra attention and initiative someone chooses to put into a job beyond its requirements.

4

All in

A person who wants the work to succeed and takes responsibility for helping it succeed.

Act I
Two kinds of worker
01

The man with the stopwatch

A useful method for a task with a known answer.

At Bethlehem Steel, Frederick Taylor studied how laborers loaded pig iron. He prescribed when to lift and when to rest, selected workers suited to the task, and paid more for meeting the target. His account reported a large increase in output.

47 tonsThe daily pig-iron target in Taylor's account, up from an average of 12.5 tons. The result depended on worker selection, prescribed rest, and higher pay.Taylor, The Principles of Scientific Management, 1911

That approach fits work whose steps can be observed and repeated. It also gives the manager a particular role: work out the method, then make sure people follow it. The trouble starts when you carry that role into a job where finding the method is part of the work.

The manager's move

Before prescribing a method, ask whether the task has a known answer or needs someone to work one out.

02

The worker who owns the factory

Expertise changes what a manager needs to provide.

An experienced engineer may understand a technical problem better than their manager. The manager still needs to set expectations and judge the result, but prescribing each step can waste the expertise they hired. Drucker described the difference this way:

Knowledge workers own the means of production. It is the knowledge between their ears, and it is a totally portable and enormous capital asset.Peter Drucker

Give people the outcome, the constraints, and the information they need to make decisions. Keep fixed procedures where consistency matters, such as lockout rules, hazardous-material handling, and critical handoffs. People can follow those rules and still have room to diagnose a problem or improve a process.

The manager's move

For each assignment, make clear which boundaries are fixed and which decisions belong to the person doing it.

03

What you expect from people

Your assumptions shape the work you give them.

Douglas McGregor described two views of workers in The Human Side of Enterprise. Theory X assumes people avoid work and need close control. Theory Y assumes people can direct their own effort toward goals they accept.

These assumptions affect how you manage. If you make every decision and give someone only routine tasks, you leave them little opportunity to show initiative. Their dependence on you may be a result of the arrangement.

The manager's move

When someone seems passive, check what they are actually allowed to decide.

Act II
The thing you cannot command
04

The gap

Meeting the requirements leaves plenty of judgment unaccounted for.

An engineer can complete the assigned fix or also investigate why the problem keeps returning. A customer success manager can answer the ticket or notice that several customers are struggling with the same setup. That extra attention is discretionary effort.

The bars illustrate a distinction, not measured amounts. Job requirements cover part of the work; people choose how much additional attention and initiative to give.

70%of the variation in engagement scores across business units is attributed to managers in Gallup's estimate. This describes differences among teams, not a manager's share of one person's motivation.Gallup, State of the American Manager

A committed team can still work on the wrong priorities or deliver poor results. Engagement helps people do the work; the manager also has to choose the work, set a quality standard, and address misses.

The manager's move

Ask what makes it hard to do good work on your team. Start with the obstacles you can change.

05

Pay and the work itself

Fixing a bad working condition may leave the job just as dull.

Frederick Herzberg's account of motivation separates the things people complained about from the things they found satisfying. Pay, policies, and working conditions appeared in one group. Achievement, responsibility, and growth appeared in the other.

Herzberg's two groups

Hygiene factors

  • Salary and benefits
  • Company policy
  • Working conditions
  • Job security
  • A reasonable boss

Motivators

  • Achievement
  • The work itself
  • Responsibility
  • Recognition
  • Growth and advancement

Herzberg called the first group hygiene factors and the second motivators. This is a useful distinction to consider when improving a job, not a rule that pay can never motivate.

In an AT&T example Herzberg described, shareholder-correspondence clerks gained authority to sign their own letters and handle more of each inquiry. Pay stayed the same. The reported service index rose from the 20s to the 90s. The change gave them more responsibility for a complete piece of work.

I can charge a man's battery, and recharge it, and recharge it again. But it is only when he has his own generator that we can talk about motivation.Frederick Herzberg

The manager's move

Check whether people have a problem they can own from start to finish. Keep fair pay and decent conditions part of the job.

06

Autonomy, mastery, purpose

A reward can change how someone approaches a problem.

In Sam Glucksberg's 1962 experiment, a cash incentive slowed people solving a puzzle that required them to rethink an object's use. When the setup made the solution more obvious, the incentive helped. A later review of 128 studies found that some expected, tangible rewards reduced intrinsic motivation, the desire to do an activity for its own sake.

Daniel Pink organized his account around autonomy, control over how you work; mastery, getting better at the work; and purpose, understanding why it matters. These are useful questions about a job. The research doesn't establish that every bonus harms creative work.

The manager's move

Before adding an incentive, ask what behavior it will reward. A target for speed can discourage the investigation you need.

07

Small wins

People notice whether their effort leads anywhere.

Teresa Amabile and Steven Kramer examined nearly 12,000 workday diary entries. Progress on meaningful work was closely associated with better days. A small step forward could matter; blocked progress and setbacks could spoil the day.

An experiment by Dan Ariely, Emir Kamenica, and Dražen Prelec tested something more basic: whether anyone acknowledged the work.

15¢ vs 27.5¢Average pay at which participants stopped doing a repetitive task. Those whose pages were acknowledged continued to 15¢; those whose pages were ignored stopped at 27.5¢. The group whose work was shredded stopped at 29¢.Ariely, Kamenica & Prelec, 2008

The manager's move

Use a one-on-one to ask what is stuck and look at what was finished. Acknowledgment is more useful when you have actually read the work.

Act III
The craft
08

Match the support to the task

An expert can be a beginner on a new assignment.

Andy Grove called this task-relevant maturity: how much experience and confidence someone has with the particular work in front of them. A senior engineer may need little direction on a design and close support when managing people for the first time.

Four ways to support the work

Too much direction limits an experienced person. Too little can leave a beginner stuck. Treat these stages as a guide to the conversation.

The four styles come from Hersey and Blanchard's Situational Leadership model. Grove's related idea appears in High Output Management.

The one-on-one gives you a place to find out what support is needed. Let the person bring their agenda. Ask about decisions, obstacles, and work you would otherwise miss. Grove made the case for the time this takes:

Ninety minutes of your time can enhance the quality of your subordinate's work for two weeks, or for some eighty-plus hours.Andy Grove, High Output Management

The manager's move

Agree on check-ins when you assign new work. Adjust them as the person gains experience.

09

Ask before giving advice

Find out how the person understands the problem.

In Google's original Project Oxygen findings, coaching ranked first among the behaviors of its best managers; technical expertise ranked last. Google reported that median manager-favorability scores rose from 83% to 88% between 2010 and 2012 as it put the findings to use.

Michael Bungay Stanier calls the urge to supply an answer the "advice monster." His questions, "What's on your mind?" and "And what else?", give the other person time to explain. Ask what they have tried and where they want help. Offer an answer when it is useful, especially when they lack information you have.

Coaching is no longer a specialty. You cannot be a good manager without being a good coach.The leadership handbook of Bill Campbell

The manager's move

Before solving the problem, ask what the person thinks the next step should be.

10

Care personally, challenge directly

Useful feedback names something the person can change.

Kim Scott's Radical Candor puts care for the person alongside direct feedback about their work. Her term ruinous empathy describes a familiar failure: avoiding a clear message because it might hurt. The person then keeps making a mistake you could have helped them correct.

Make praise specific too. Tell someone what they did and why it helped. Buckingham and Goodall's case for noticing strengths is useful here. It doesn't remove the need to correct errors or enforce standards.

The manager's move

Describe the behavior, its effect, and what needs to change. Give the person a chance to explain what happened.

11

Leave room for other people to think

Helping can become taking over.

Liz Wiseman calls leaders Multipliers when they make use of other people's capabilities, and Diminishers when their own interventions limit them. A manager can do the latter while trying to help: answer every question, rescue every project, or propose more ideas than the team can pursue.

2×The difference in capability people said their leaders used in Wiseman's study. This was participants' assessment of their capacity, not a measured doubling of team output.Wiseman, Multipliers
It isn't how much you know that matters. What matters is how much access you have to what other people know.Liz Wiseman

The manager's move

In your next meeting, ask for other people's assessment before giving yours. Notice whether you leave them a decision to make.

Act IV
The conditions you build
12

Make it safe to report a problem

Reported errors depend on whether people feel able to report them.

In her hospital research, Amy Edmondson found that teams judged to work well together sometimes reported more medication errors. Better reporting could explain the apparent contradiction: people felt able to admit mistakes. A low reported error rate can also mean problems stay hidden.

Psychological safety is the shared belief that speaking up won't bring punishment or humiliation. Google's team study also identified it as an important factor. It matters when someone has to question a plan, flag a risk, or ask for help.

85%of respondents in one exploratory study said they had stayed silent about a work concern at least once. Fear of being labeled negatively was the most common reason.Milliken, Morrison & Hewlin, 2003

The manager's move

When someone brings bad news, start by understanding it. Admit your own mistakes and make sure reported problems lead to action.

13

Give people the context to decide

Delegation needs a named owner and enough information.

Netflix's culture memo calls this context, not control. Explain the strategy, the constraints, and the risks so the person closest to the work can make an informed decision.

Then say who owns the decision and when it needs to be escalated. Removing an approval step helps only if the person has authority to act. Keep review where the risk requires it, and explain why it is there.

If you dummy-proof the system, only dummies will want to work there.Reed Hastings, Netflix

The manager's move

Choose a recurring decision you currently approve. Decide what information and authority would let someone else own it.

14

Specific goals

Agree on what a good result looks like.

In Gary Latham and James Baldes's logging study, trucks were loaded to about 60% of the legal weight limit. The company replaced "do your best" with a goal of 94%, agreed with drivers and their union. Loads rose to around 90%. The company estimated savings of $250,000 in trucks it no longer needed to buy.

Locke and Latham's review of goal-setting research describes why specific, challenging goals often help: they direct attention and make progress easier to assess. People also need the skills, feedback, and commitment to pursue them.

46%of U.S. employees strongly agreed they knew what was expected of them at work in Gallup's August 2024 measure, down from 56 percent in January 2020.Gallup, 2024

Choose the measure carefully. A speed target can sacrifice quality. For unfamiliar work, a learning goal may be more useful: test the plausible approaches and explain which one should proceed. Agree on what must be learned before setting an output quota.

The manager's move

Ask each person what result they are working toward and when it is due. Resolve any disagreement about what counts as success.

Company examples

These practices exist outside research studies.

The figures are historical accounts and company reports. They do not establish that the management practices caused the results.

Company histories cannot isolate the effects of culture from products, markets, or other decisions. They do show different ways to give employees authority. Before copying one, identify the decision you would actually let someone make.

The manager's move

Choose a practice that changes the work itself. A new title or office layout does little on its own.

Act V
The hard part
16

When someone is falling short

Address the problem while there is still time to improve.

A missed standard needs a conversation. Describe the gap and ask what is causing it. The answer might be skill, workload, unclear expectations, or a poor fit for the role. You need to know which before choosing a remedy.

Working conditions can change performance substantially. Toyota rehired about 85% of the former workforce when it reopened GM's troubled Fremont plant as NUMMI. The same people worked differently in a different system. Check the system you have given someone before concluding they cannot do the job.

Three questions before deciding
the seat
Wrong role?
Would a different role make better use of their skills?
the mirror
Did I cause this?
Were the expectations clear? Did they have the support and resources to meet them?
the call
Wrong person?
If the mismatch remains after a fair effort, decide what happens next.

Use the answers to choose between support, a change of role, and ending the arrangement. Set a review date when you choose an improvement plan.

If improvement is possible, agree on the required change, the support you will provide, and a date to review it. If the role is a poor fit, address that directly. Waiting without a plan leaves the person uncertain and the team carrying unfinished work.

Judge the effect on the team as well as individual output. Housman and Minor's study of toxic workers found substantial costs from serious misconduct, even among productive employees. A sales total or delivery count can miss the damage someone does to colleagues.

>2×Estimated savings from avoiding a toxic worker compared with hiring a top-1% performer in Housman and Minor's study: $12,489 in avoided turnover costs versus $5,303 from additional output. The comparison applies to their data and cost assumptions.Housman & Minor, Toxic Workers, 2015

This calls for a decision about the person and the job. A forced ranking that requires dismissing a fixed share of the team cannot tell you whether any particular person has failed a reasonable standard.

The manager's move

For the performance issue you keep postponing, write down the gap, the next action, and the review date. Then have the conversation.

Act VI
The job changes again
17

Managing through other managers

Several teams need decisions that one team cannot make alone.

A director cannot inspect every decision or coach every person. Doing so creates a queue and leaves the team's managers unsure of their authority. The job includes choosing priorities across teams and helping their managers lead.

Three responsibilities at this level
direction
Choose what matters
Choose outcomes and make the tradeoffs between teams clear.
system
Make work visible
Agree on ownership, measures, and regular reviews of progress and risk.
leaders
Build the bench
Coach managers and prepare people who can take on critical roles.

Watch whether the managers you lead can make good decisions without waiting for you.

This transition is the manager-of-managers passage in The Leadership Pipeline. It extends Andy Grove's definition of managerial output in High Output Management.

Agree with each manager on the outcomes, resources, and decisions they own. Review how their team is doing and how they are developing people. Use skip-level conversations to understand the work; avoid giving instructions that create a second chain of command.

A broad goal such as "improve retention" needs choices. Which customers need attention? What work will stop? Where will quality take precedence over speed? Put people and budget behind those choices. Warn executives about risks early and explain changes to the teams affected.

Cross-team disagreements need a shared outcome and someone responsible for resolving the decision. Bring the evidence and explain the tradeoff. Escalate when the people involved cannot resolve it, with the disagreement clear enough for someone else to decide.

Check who can cover critical roles. If one person's week away stops decisions, give someone else practice making those decisions while there is still help available.

The director's move

Review the decisions waiting for you. Delegate the ones a team can own and spend time on the choices that require several teams.

Act VII · The Playbook

For Your Improvement

A manager needs practice too. It helps to name the skill you're working on and find a way to use it in your current job. FYI: For Your Improvement is a reference book built for that process. The wheel and reader below let you explore its competency framework and plan an assignment.

FYI: For Your Improvement comes from the work of Michael Lombardo and Robert Eichinger and is published by Korn Ferry. It is organized for reference: each chapter covers a competency, describes what different levels of skill look like, and suggests ways to practice. Start with feedback about your work, find the relevant chapter, and choose an assignment. You can return to the same chapter as the job changes, or use it to help someone else plan their development.

The book describes leadership in terms of competencies, skills expressed through observable behavior. That gives you something specific to work on. "Become a better manager" is hard to practice. "Give clear feedback when a commitment is missed" is an action you can try, review, and improve. People differ in how easily they learn a skill, and some jobs fit them better than others. The framework helps identify which behavior needs attention and what practice might be useful.

The Map
The competency framework, on one wheel

The book uses the Korn Ferry Leadership Architect framework: 38 competencies grouped into 12 clusters and 4 factors. The inner ring shows factors, the middle ring clusters, and the outer ring competencies. Hover or tap a segment to see its description and where it belongs. The chapter reader below has its own navigation, so you can browse the framework here and read a chapter independently.

Much of the advice in this essay falls under People and Self. People has the widest wedge because it contains the most competencies. Each competency has four descriptions: less skilled, skilled, talented, and overused. The overused description is especially useful when something you normally do well is causing trouble. Careful planning, for example, can delay action if you keep revising the plan. A related skill can help you balance that tendency; the book calls it a compensator. If a skill is difficult to develop directly, another competency may partly substitute for it. These connections give you more than one way to address a problem, and they are included in the reader.

A chapter also asks why someone might struggle with the skill. Open "How to build it" to see possible causes, practice tips, an assignment, and related competencies. Use the causes as questions to investigate, rather than assuming every item describes you.

The assignments connect the book to a broader approach to development: learning through work, with feedback and formal study to help. The Center for Creative Leadership describes this in its 70-20-10 framework, drawn from research in which executives looked back on the experiences that helped them learn. It groups those experiences into challenging assignments, developmental relationships, and coursework or training.

The percentages are a guideline, based on retrospective accounts of learning. They do not tell you how to divide every week or what share of your own improvement came from a particular source. A course can help you understand an experience; a coach can help you interpret it. Reading the chapter is preparation. Choose work where you can try the behavior, get useful feedback, and make another attempt. An assignment should stretch you while allowing enough support to learn from a mistake.

Start with feedback from people who see your work. A structured 360-degree review can include your manager, peers, and reports; a few candid conversations can also identify a useful pattern. Pick the competencies most relevant to the job you have or the next one. Choose tips you can use and an assignment inside your current role, what the book calls developing in place. Agree with someone on what they will observe, ask for feedback during the assignment, and review what changed afterward.

Learning from experience
Learning agility

Learning agility is the willingness and ability to learn from experience and apply the lesson in an unfamiliar situation. Lombardo and Eichinger used it in their work on leadership potential. It asks how someone learns when their existing expertise no longer supplies the answer. That is relevant to promotion: being good at the current job gives you evidence about performance there, but leaves questions about a different role. In its 2005 survey, the Corporate Leadership Council reported that 71 percent of high performers had limited potential at the next level, citing gaps in ability, aspiration, or engagement. That figure belongs to the survey and its definitions. When considering a larger role, look for experience with the demands it introduces and opportunities to test the skills still untried.

This is a commercial framework, and the assessments sold with it are proprietary. Its categories provide a vocabulary for discussing work; the wheel cannot establish whether someone is ready for promotion or how much they will improve. The framework, competency names, and book language retained in the reader belong to Korn Ferry and the cited FYI guide. Some descriptions and assignments are adapted for this page. Consult the book for its complete treatment, and judge a development plan by changes people can observe in the work.

A useful development plan names the behavior you want to improve, gives you a chance to practice it, and includes someone who can tell you whether the change helped. A competency label gives that conversation a starting point.

The evidence has limits.

These sources include controlled experiments, workplace studies, company histories, and advice from practitioners. They answer different questions. No one study establishes a complete way to manage a team, and a useful practice still has to work for the people and the job in front of you.
