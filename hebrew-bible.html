The Sacred Books

The Torah

God promises Abraham a land. Generations later, Moses dies outside it. Between those moments, a family becomes a people with laws governing its worship and daily life.

Genesis to Deuteronomy / 21 min read

01 Summary

The Torah, in plain English

The Torah ends with Moses dead and Israel camped outside the land promised to Abraham. Its story begins with the creation of the world. God calls that world good and makes human beings in his image. In Eden, the first humans disobey him and are expelled from the garden. Cain kills his brother Abel. Violence spreads until God destroys almost all life in a flood, saving Noah’s household and animals in an ark. Afterward God makes a covenant with Noah and every living creature: he will never again destroy them by flood. The rainbow is its sign. At Babel, people build a city and tower to keep themselves together; God confuses their language and scatters them.

Genesis then turns from humanity to one family. God tells Abram, later renamed Abraham, to leave Haran for a land he will show him. Abram goes to Canaan, roughly the region of modern Israel and the Palestinian territories. God promises him descendants, land, and blessing that will reach other families. Abraham and his wife Sarah are childless when the promise begins. Genesis 18 connects Abraham’s calling with teaching his household righteousness and justice, but the story also gives him a special relationship with God. It never explains the first choice of him. Abraham welcomes visitors and argues with God against destroying Sodom if innocent people are there.

The promise passes through a troubled family. Before Sarah bears Isaac, Abraham has Ishmael with Hagar, Sarah’s enslaved Egyptian woman. God promises a future to Ishmael too, but names Isaac as the covenant heir. Hagar and Ishmael are later sent away. In another story, described as a test, God tells Abraham to sacrifice Isaac, then stops him before the killing. Isaac and Rebekah have Esau and Jacob; Jacob gains the blessing intended for his older brother through deception and eventually receives the name Israel. His sons, born to Leah, Rachel, Bilhah, and Zilpah, become the ancestors of Israel’s tribes. The narrative follows inheritance, rivalry, infertility, and reconciliation within these households. Its chosen ancestors are capable of cruelty and deceit.

Joseph, one of Jacob’s sons, is sold by his brothers and taken to Egypt. After interpreting Pharaoh’s dreams, he is put in charge of storing grain against a coming famine. His brothers arrive to buy food, and he eventually reveals who he is and brings the family to Egypt. Joseph saves lives, but his administration also concentrates land and control over the population in Pharaoh’s hands. Genesis 47 records that part of the rescue too.

Exodus opens after Joseph’s generation has died. A new king fears the growing Israelite population, forces it into construction labor, and orders its newborn boys drowned in the Nile. Moses’ mother hides him in a basket among the river reeds. Pharaoh’s daughter finds him and raises him as her son. As an adult, Moses kills an Egyptian he sees beating a Hebrew and flees to Midian. There, God speaks to him from a burning bush and sends him back to confront Pharaoh.

Pharaoh repeatedly refuses to release the Israelites. The account also says God hardens his heart, prolonging the confrontation. God sends plagues, culminating in the death of Egypt’s firstborn. The Israelites mark their doorways with the blood of a slaughtered animal and eat the meal that becomes Passover. They leave, cross a sea after its waters divide, and see the pursuing Egyptian army drowned. Exodus counts about 600,000 men on foot, besides children. That is the text’s figure; the scale and historical basis of the departure are separate questions, discussed below.

In the third month after leaving Egypt, the people reach Mount Sinai. The mountain smokes and shakes as God gives the commandments. The people fear the voice and ask Moses to speak with God for them. They enter a covenant, a binding relationship: they agree to obey God, and God promises to bring them to the land and dwell among them. Moses receives laws and stone tablets. The Ten Commandments prohibit other gods, idols, misuse of God’s name, murder, adultery, theft, false testimony, and coveting; they also command Sabbath rest and respect for parents.

While Moses is away, the people ask Aaron to make them a god to lead them. He makes a golden calf. Aaron declares a festival to the LORD, so the scene is more complicated than a simple switch to an unrelated deity: they are worshipping through an image the covenant forbids. Moses returns and breaks the tablets. At his command, the Levites kill about three thousand people; a plague follows. Moses pleads for the people, God allows the covenant to continue, and new tablets are made. Forgiveness here comes alongside lethal punishment.

Much of the Torah now concerns how this people will live with God in its midst. They build the Tabernacle, a portable sanctuary containing the ark and its tablets. Aaron and his sons serve as priests. Leviticus gives rules for offerings, food, bodily impurity, sacred festivals, and the annual Day of Atonement, alongside laws about wages, courts, sexual conduct, and care for the poor. These rites occupy a large part of the work. Ritual impurity can follow childbirth, sex, or contact with a corpse; it does not by itself mean someone has done wrong.

Numbers organizes the camp by tribe around the sanctuary. A cloud above it signals when to stay and when to move. After about a year at Sinai, they set out toward Canaan. The journey is marked by hunger, complaints, disputes over leadership, and punishments. Some people want to return to Egypt. Korah and other leaders challenge Moses and Aaron, and the rebellion ends in deaths by fire and the opening earth.

At Canaan’s border, Moses sends twelve scouts to inspect the land. Most return convinced that its inhabitants are too strong to defeat. The people refuse to enter. God sentences the counted generation of men aged twenty and older to die in the wilderness, with Joshua and Caleb as the exceptions. Their children will enter instead. The forty-year journey is presented as a punishment for this refusal. Later, Moses and Aaron also lose permission to enter after failing to uphold God’s holiness at a rock where the people demand water.

By the end of Numbers, Israel has already fought wars and taken territory east of the Jordan. The war against Midian includes Moses’ order to kill captive boys and women who have had sex, while sparing virgin girls. Some tribes receive eastern land before the crossing. The conquest is therefore already under way within the Torah, although the campaign west of the river belongs to Joshua. Deuteronomy orders the destruction of the Canaanite peoples it names and forbids making treaties with them. Its command to protect the resident stranger exists beside commands to destroy other peoples.

Deuteronomy is largely Moses’ farewell address on the plains of Moab. He retells the past and restates laws for life in the land. Loyalty to God is to be taught at home, remembered in worship, and expressed in conduct. Moses warns that disobedience will bring defeat and exile, and also describes a return to God after exile. He hands leadership to Joshua, sees the land from Mount Nebo, and dies without entering it. The promise made in Genesis remains only partly fulfilled.

Joshua continues the story, but it belongs to the Prophets, the second division of the Hebrew Bible. The third is the Writings, which include Psalms and Job. This page covers the Torah alone: Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. In rabbinic Judaism these books hold a distinctive authority, and their interpretation continues through the Mishnah, Talmud, and later teaching. A guide to the five books can introduce that tradition; it cannot stand in for all of it.

02 Context and transmission

What can actually be known

The surviving work

The five books are written in Biblical Hebrew. Torah means teaching or instruction; the word also has a broader use for Jewish teaching beyond these books. The Greek translation of the Torah dates to the third century BCE, and Hebrew copies survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls from the last centuries BCE. The work was in circulation before Christianity. These witnesses establish the existence of the text, rather than the truth of the events it narrates.

A synagogue Torah scroll still presents it as one continuous document with four book breaks and smaller paragraph spaces. It has no modern chapter or verse numbers. Jewish verse divisions are old, but their numerals came later. The chapters used today came through the medieval Latin Bible and were adopted in printed Hebrew Bibles because shared coordinates are useful. The familiar annual Jewish reading cycle divides it into 54 portions, sometimes combined to fit the calendar. Other reading cycles have also existed.

Authorship and history

Jewish tradition attributes the Torah to Moses. The Torah itself never names the author of the whole work, and its final chapter narrates Moses’ death. Historical scholarship generally treats it as a work assembled from sources and revised over time. Scholars disagree about the dates and the process. The familiar four-source theory is a model of composition, not a verified list of four individual authors.

Archaeology has not established the mass Exodus described in the narrative. Whether a smaller departure from Egypt lies behind it remains disputed; Richard Elliott Friedman’s proposal of a Levite exodus is one hypothesis. Nor can Mount Sinai or the wilderness route be located securely. The story can be followed closely without treating its population figures, speeches, or itinerary as independently confirmed history.

How safely did this reach us?

Early copies survive. No original does.

Manuscripts, wording, and translation need separate judgments.

Early witnesses Strong

The Greek Torah was translated in the third century BCE, and Hebrew Qumran fragments let us compare copies more than 2,000 years old. No author’s original survives.

Hebrew wording Strong, not perfect

The Masoretic Text is the Hebrew base of most modern editions. Some Qumran copies are close to it; others resemble different textual traditions. The Samaritan Torah and Greek translation preserve further variants, including differences that change the meaning. Stability within a tradition does not establish every original reading.

English meaning Plot strong, key words mixed

The main narrative is readily understandable in English. Wordplay, ambiguous grammar, and terms with several meanings require choices. The Shema below is one example: its Hebrew can be read as affirming God’s oneness or Israel’s exclusive loyalty to him.

Verdict based on the Dead Sea Scrolls, the history of the Greek Pentateuch, and Emanuel Tov’s account of the surviving Torah text families. This measures transmission, not whether its claims are true.

03 Expand the concepts

Twelve themes in the five books

A reading of each theme, with passages to check it against. Later Jewish interpretations are identified separately.

01Humans are made in God’s image.Genesis 1, 5, and 9

Genesis applies the image of God to humanity, male and female. Royal imagery in the ancient Near East helps explain why many readers see significance in giving this status to everyone. Genesis 9 invokes the image as the reason to punish murder with death. It provides a basis for human dignity, though it does not make the Torah’s laws socially equal or rule out execution and warfare. In a later rabbinic debate, Ben Azzai calls the account of humanity in Genesis 5 a greater principle than Rabbi Akiva’s choice of love your neighbour. That is one Jewish reading of its importance.

And God created man in His own image, in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them.Genesis 1:27

In the day that God created man, in the likeness of God made He him; male and female created He them, and blessed them.Genesis 5:1-2

Whoso sheddeth man’s blood, by man shall his blood be shed; for in the image of God made He man.Genesis 9:6

02Genesis follows disobedience and violence.Eden, Cain, flood, Babel

Adam blames Eve, Cain denies responsibility for his brother, and violence fills the earth before the flood. Babel adds the effort to make a name and avoid dispersal. These episodes have different concerns, but together they describe a world repeatedly damaged by human action. God warns Cain that sin desires him and that he can rule over it. Later Jewish teaching develops the idea of the yetzer, the human inclination toward good or evil. That teaching should be distinguished from what these early chapters explicitly say.

Sin coucheth at the door; and unto thee is its desire, but thou mayest rule over it.Genesis 4:7

Every imagination of the thoughts of his heart was only evil continually.Genesis 6:5

Let us make us a name; lest we be scattered abroad upon the face of the whole earth.Genesis 11:4

03God chooses Abraham and binds his descendants.The Abraham cycle, then Deuteronomy’s courts

God promises Abraham descendants, land, and a great name. Genesis 18 also charges him with teaching righteousness and justice. Election includes both favor and obligation: Deuteronomy 7 calls Israel God’s treasured people and attributes the choice to God’s love and his oath to their ancestors. The blessing of other families in Genesis 12 has more than one reading. Rashi understands it as people blessing their children by wishing them to be like Abraham. The text does not give Israel a general command to convert the world, but neither does it describe the chosen relationship as an ordinary job open on equal terms to everyone.

PassagesExcerpts from JPS 1917

I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and be thou a blessing ... in thee shall all the families of the earth be blessed.Genesis 12:2-3

They may keep the way of the LORD, to do righteousness and justice.Genesis 18:19

In thy seed shall all the nations of the earth be blessed.Genesis 22:18

Justice, justice shalt thou follow, that thou mayest live.Deuteronomy 16:20

04Egypt is a reason to protect the vulnerable.Exodus retold in every later book

The laws repeatedly appeal to Israel’s experience in Egypt. Having known forced labor, the people must give servants rest and must not oppress a resident stranger. The Hebrew ger usually means a person living outside their own ancestral community, not necessarily a convert or every foreigner everywhere. Deuteronomy commands love for this person and names food and clothing among God’s provisions for them. Passover makes the memory a household practice: Exodus 13 tells a parent to explain what God did for me when I came out of Egypt. The later seder develops that first-person telling.

PassagesExcerpts from JPS 1917

I have surely seen the affliction of My people ... and have heard their cry.Exodus 3:7

I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage.Exodus 20:2

A stranger shalt thou not wrong, neither shalt thou oppress him; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt.Exodus 22:20

Love ye therefore the stranger; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt.Deuteronomy 10:19

05Israel accepts a covenant at Sinai.Sinai and the plains of Moab

At Sinai the people agree to obey the laws Moses reads to them, and he marks the agreement with sacrificial blood. God offers them the status of a kingdom of priests and a holy nation. Genesis 17 gives Abraham’s covenant a bodily sign, male circumcision, including males enslaved in his household. The Sinai covenant is collective and extends to later generations; it is not an account of individual consent in the modern sense. Exodus 24 says the people will do and hear, or obey. Later rabbis celebrate the order of those verbs as readiness to act before full understanding. In the passage itself, Moses has just read the covenant document aloud.

PassagesExcerpts from JPS 1917

If ye will hearken unto My voice indeed, and keep My covenant ... ye shall be unto Me a kingdom of priests, and a holy nation.Exodus 19:5-6

All that the LORD hath spoken will we do, and obey.Exodus 24:7

Neither with you only do I make this covenant ... but ... also with him that is not here with us this day.Deuteronomy 29:13-14

06God is to dwell among the people.Tabernacle, sacrifice, atonement, priestly blessing

Exodus explicitly connects the rescue from Egypt with God dwelling among Israel. The sanctuary, priesthood, offerings, and purity rules explain how that presence is approached and protected. Offerings have several purposes, including gifts, shared meals, and atonement; they are not all payments for wrongdoing. Leviticus 16 describes an annual rite that cleanses the sanctuary as well as the people. Numbers gives the priests a blessing still used in Jewish worship. Silver amulets from Ketef Hinnom, usually dated around the end of the seventh century BCE, preserve wording parallel to parts of that blessing. They attest the blessing’s antiquity, not a complete Torah at that date.

Let them make Me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them.Exodus 25:8

I ... brought them forth out of the land of Egypt, that I may dwell among them.Exodus 29:46

The cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of the LORD filled the tabernacle.Exodus 40:34

On this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you.Leviticus 16:30

The LORD bless thee, and keep thee; the LORD make His face to shine upon thee, and be gracious unto thee; the LORD lift up His countenance upon thee, and give thee peace.Numbers 6:24-26

07Holiness includes worship and fair treatment.The whole of Leviticus, concentrated in chapter 19

To be holy is to be set apart for God. Leviticus applies this to food and sanctuary rules, and also to conduct toward others. Chapter 19 prohibits withheld wages, dishonest judgment, and mistreatment of the deaf and blind. It commands love for a neighbour and for the resident stranger. Ritual purity is a related but different category: ordinary bodily processes can make someone temporarily impure without making them morally guilty. Later rabbis give the ethical commands special weight. Akiva names love your neighbour as a great principle, and Hillel tells a prospective convert to avoid doing to others what he finds hateful, then go and learn the rest.

PassagesExcerpts from JPS 1917

Sanctify yourselves therefore, and be ye holy; for I am holy.Leviticus 11:44

Thou shalt not curse the deaf, nor put a stumbling-block before the blind ... thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself.Leviticus 19:14, 18

The stranger that sojourneth with you shall be unto you as the home-born ... and thou shalt love him as thyself.Leviticus 19:34

Ye shall be holy unto Me ... and [I] have set you apart from the peoples.Leviticus 20:26

08Sabbath and release laws limit work and ownership.Sabbath, sabbatical year, Jubilee

The seventh day brings rest for household members, servants, resident strangers, and livestock. Exodus grounds it in creation; Deuteronomy in the memory of slavery. Other laws give fields a sabbatical year, release debts, or return ancestral land at the Jubilee. The protections differ by legal collection and by who qualifies. Leviticus gives Israelites a protection against permanent slavery while allowing foreigners to be held and inherited. These are limits within an unequal social order. The commands also do not tell us how consistently ancient communities followed them. Sabbath observance continues in Jewish life, with substantial differences among households and movements.

God blessed the seventh day, and hallowed it; because that in it He rested from all His work.Genesis 2:3

Thy man-servant and thy maid-servant may rest as well as thou. ... therefore the LORD thy God commanded thee to keep the sabbath day.Deuteronomy 5:14-15

Proclaim liberty throughout the land unto all the inhabitants thereof; it shall be a jubilee unto you.Leviticus 25:10

The land shall not be sold in perpetuity; for the land is Mine.Leviticus 25:23

09Love of God is taught and practiced.The Shema and Deuteronomy’s teaching program

Deuteronomy joins love of God to repeated teaching: at home, on the road, at bedtime, and on rising. The Shema, named for its opening word, hear, became a central daily prayer. Its first line can be translated as the LORD is one or the LORD alone; the wording supports different emphases on oneness and exclusive allegiance. Elsewhere Deuteronomy explicitly says there is no other God. Traditional practice gives the teaching physical forms: tefillin, leather boxes worn on the arm and head, contain four Torah passages; a mezuzah holds two Deuteronomy passages on parchment at a doorpost. Observance varies, so this should not be read as a description of every Jewish person.

Thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children ... when thou liest down, and when thou risest up.Deuteronomy 6:7

What doth the LORD thy God require of thee, but ... to walk in all His ways, and to love Him?Deuteronomy 10:12

That ye may remember and do all My commandments, and be holy unto your God.Numbers 15:40

10God forgives, and also punishes.Golden Calf, spies, return

After the golden calf, Moses intercedes and God renews the covenant. Exodus 34 describes God as merciful, patient, and forgiving, then says he does not clear the guilty and visits wrongdoing on later generations. Both statements belong to the passage. Numbers likewise pairs pardon with the wilderness generation’s exclusion from the land. Deuteronomy 30 describes people returning to God after exile and God restoring them. Later Jewish teaching develops teshuvah, repentance or return, and uses the attributes of mercy in penitential prayer. Forgiveness is central to these readings, but it does not erase the text’s punishments.

PassagesExcerpts from JPS 1917

The LORD ... merciful and gracious, long-suffering, and abundant in goodness and truth.Exodus 34:6

Pardon, I pray Thee, the iniquity of this people according unto the greatness of Thy lovingkindness.Numbers 14:19

And shalt return unto the LORD thy God ... with all thy heart, and with all thy soul.Deuteronomy 30:2

11The wilderness generation refuses to enter.The wilderness generation in Numbers

Numbers describes people who have left Egypt but fear hunger, regret their lost food, and distrust the prospect of conquest. After the scouts’ report, they propose a leader who will take them back. Reading this as the difficulty of learning freedom is a plausible later interpretation. The book itself frames the refusal as failure to trust God and gives it a sentence: that generation will die in the wilderness. Their children inherit the opportunity to enter, but the later laws and warnings do not assume they will be free of their parents’ failures. Moses and Aaron are also held accountable.

We remember the fish, which we were wont to eat in Egypt for nought ... but now our soul is dried away.Numbers 11:5-6

We were in our own sight as grasshoppers, and so we were in their sight.Numbers 13:33

Let us make a captain, and let us return into Egypt.Numbers 14:4

Because ye believed not in Me ... ye shall not bring this assembly into the land.Numbers 20:12

12Moses dies outside the promised land.Moses’ last speech and death

Deuteronomy says the command is accessible, in your mouth and heart, and urges the people to choose life. It also predicts their failure and exile, so the ending offers a warning alongside a choice. Moses dies within sight of the land; Joshua will continue the narrative. We can describe this effect without claiming to know why the editors chose that boundary. Later rabbis apply not in heaven to a legal dispute in which a heavenly voice is rejected in favor of established rules of interpretation. On Simchat Torah, congregations finish Deuteronomy and begin Genesis again. These are later uses of the text, rather than explanations supplied by its ending.

Do not skip The hard parts

Slavery, patriarchy, violence, contradiction

Slavery is limited, not abolished

The rescue from Egyptian forced labor does not produce a general ban on owning people. Exodus 21 regulates slaveholding and permits an owner to beat an enslaved person, subject to specified penalties. Leviticus 25:44-46 permits permanent, inheritable slavery of foreigners. Release protections for Israelites cannot be generalized to everyone.

The legal world is patriarchal

The narratives give women decisive roles, including the midwives who defy Pharaoh and Miriam, a prophet. The law usually assumes male household authority. Numbers 30 lets fathers or husbands annul women’s vows; Numbers 27 gives daughters inheritance when there is no son. These provisions grant some protections within a hierarchy.

Some violence is commanded

Numbers 31 includes the killing of Midianite captives. Deuteronomy 7 and 20 command destruction of named Canaanite peoples, and Deuteronomy 25 commands the erasure of Amalek. Some later Jewish interpretations restrict when such commands could apply. They remain part of the Torah and resist a wholly humane account of it.

The laws do not always agree

Exodus 21 gives a Hebrew male slave release after six years and treats a daughter sold into slavery differently. Deuteronomy 15 explicitly gives six-year release to Hebrew men and women. Leviticus 25 describes release at the Jubilee. Later interpreters reconcile these rules; historians also study their differences as evidence of separate legal collections.

  1. Genesis. Creation, flood, and Babel are chapters 1-11; Abraham’s call is chapter 12. Hagar, Ishmael, and circumcision appear in chapters 16-17 and 21; the binding of Isaac in 22; Jacob’s blessing and new name in 27 and 32. Joseph’s story begins in 37; his famine policy is 47:13-26.
  2. Exodus. Enslavement and Moses’ early life are chapters 1-2; the bush is 3; Passover and the departure count are 12. Sinai and assent are 19-24; the calf, killings, and renewal are 32-34.
  3. Sanctuary and wilderness. The cloud is Exodus 40:34-38 and Numbers 9:15-23; departure is Numbers 10:11-12; the scouts and sentence are Numbers 13-14; Korah is 16; the rock is 20:1-13.
  4. War and the ending. Eastern conquest, Midian, and settlement are Numbers 21, 31, and 32. Destruction commands are Deuteronomy 7:1-8 and 20:16-18; the command against Amalek is 25:17-19. Choice, predicted failure, and Moses’ death are chapters 30-34.
  5. Unequal legal protections. Compare Exodus 21, Deuteronomy 15:12-18, and Leviticus 25 on slavery and release. Daughters’ inheritance is Numbers 27:1-11; women’s vows are Numbers 30. The midwives and Miriam appear in Exodus 1:15-21 and 15:20-21.
