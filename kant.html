Kant and the Categorical Imperative

An honest shopkeeper can do the right thing for a self-interested reason. Kant asks what makes a will good, whether its principles could hold for everyone, and why persons have a worth that cannot be exchanged.

Reading guide / 7 passages through the Groundwork / translation: T.K. Abbott / 13 min read

Scroll

Kant's shopkeeper charges a child the same price as everyone else. But if his only reason is that fair dealing protects his business, Kant says the action has no moral worth. The child gets the right price; the difference lies in why the shopkeeper gives it.

The Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals, published in 1785, tries to identify and justify the principle behind that distinction. It moves from good will and duty to the categorical imperative, a requirement that binds independently of our chosen goals. Kant gives it several formulations: universal law, humanity as an end, and autonomy. He thinks they express the same moral principle. Whether his arguments establish their equivalence is disputed.

The passages use Thomas Kingsmill Abbott's translation, first published in 1873. Citations give Academy volume and page for comparison with other translations. Ellipses mark omissions; explanations are paraphrases.

1 / 7

Stop 1 / First Section, the good will

Good even when it fails

Abbott / First Section

Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will.

Even if it should happen that ... this will should wholly lack power to accomplish its purpose, if with its greatest efforts it should yet achieve nothing, and there should remain only the good will ... then, like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light, as a thing which has its whole value in itself.

Groundwork, First Section (Ak. 4:393 and 4:394, excerpts)

Intelligence and courage can help a person do good or make a villain more effective. Wealth and health can be put to bad use. Kant argues that these goods need a further condition: the will directing them must be good. A good will is a commitment to what morality requires, without allowing another advantage to outweigh it.

A person who uses every available means to do the right thing may still accomplish nothing. The jewel comparison gives that will a worth independent of usefulness. Kant excludes a mere wish: the omitted parenthesis requires the use of all means within our power.

Consequences still matter to deciding what to do. Helping someone requires attention to what would actually help; Kant makes other people's happiness an end we ought to promote. His claim here is that a favorable result cannot supply the will's unconditional value. Ak. 4:394; 4:430.

Stop 2 / First Section, acting from duty

Doing the right thing for its own sake

Abbott / First Section

For example, it is always a matter of duty that a dealer should not over charge an inexperienced purchaser; and wherever there is much commerce the prudent tradesman does not overcharge, but keeps a fixed price for everyone ... Accordingly the action was done neither from duty nor from direct inclination, but merely with a selfish view.

Groundwork, First Section (Ak. 4:397, excerpt)

Acting in accordance with duty means doing what duty requires. Acting from duty means doing it because you recognize that requirement. Kant stipulates that self-interest supplies the shopkeeper's motive. His conduct is right; it does not express the good will Kant is trying to identify.

The next example is harder. Someone helps others because their happiness gives him pleasure. Kant calls the conduct amiable and says it deserves encouragement, yet denies it the particular value he calls moral worth. He then imagines grief extinguishing that sympathy. If the person continues helping from duty, the action now has moral worth. The contrast isolates a motive that can remain when sympathy disappears. Ak. 4:398.

Why shouldn't concern for another person itself count as a moral reason? Kant's distinction can seem to undervalue love. His examples also leave room for disagreement when sympathy and duty act together. One reading requires duty to be decisive while allowing enjoyment of the act. The stricter reading excludes inclination from the determining motive. The interpretation remains contested.

Kant does not recommend becoming indifferent. In the later Metaphysics of Morals, he says we have a duty to use sympathy to support active benevolence. What he rejects is making an obligation depend on whether a feeling happens to be present. And duty here means respect for moral law, not obedience to any order a government or employer gives. Ak. 6:456; 4:400.

Stop 3 / Second Section, universal law

Could your principle hold for everyone?

Abbott / Second Section

There is therefore but one categorical imperative, namely, this: Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law.

Groundwork, Second Section (Ak. 4:421)

A hypothetical imperative tells us to take the necessary means to an end we have chosen. Learning a skill requires practice; abandon that end and the instruction no longer binds. A categorical imperative commands independently of such choices. A moral prohibition does not lapse when obeying it stops serving your plans. Ak. 4:414 and 4:417.

A maxim is the principle on which you act. Spell out the situation, action, and purpose. Kant's loan example can be reconstructed as: when I need money, I will promise repayment without intending to repay, to obtain a loan. The test asks whether you can conceive this rule holding for everyone in those circumstances, then whether you can rationally will it. Liking the imagined world is a different question.

The false promise fails the first question. It secures the loan by being believed. If everyone made false promises whenever money was needed, lenders would no longer believe that assurance. The means would defeat itself. This is a contradiction in conception: the maxim relies on a practice its universalization would undermine. Kant is not predicting that this particular liar will be caught. Ak. 4:422.

Refusing all assistance fails differently. A world where nobody helps anyone is conceivable. Kant argues that we cannot rationally will it, because we also will ends whose pursuit can require other people's help. Universal refusal would deny means we may need. This contradiction in the will requires an argument about what rational agents must will, beyond showing that the world would be unpleasant. Ak. 4:423.

The distinction yields two kinds of duty. A perfect duty, such as the prohibition of false promising, allows no exception merely to suit an inclination. An imperfect duty requires adopting an end, such as helping others, while leaving latitude over when and how much to do. Imperfect does not mean optional.

The examples are arguments to assess, not settled outputs of a procedure. In the suicide case, Kant assumes that self-love's natural function is to preserve life, so using it to destroy life contradicts that function. That premise needs defending. The loan case is clearer, but even there the result depends on how the maxim and the practice of promising are understood.

The universal-law formula in three translations

All three translate Ak. 4:421, preserving the requirement that one be able to will the maxim as universal law.

T.K. Abbott featured1873
"Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
H.J. Paton1948
"Act only on that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law."
Mary Gregor1996
"Act only in accordance with that maxim through which you can at the same time will that it become a universal law."

Kant also gives a law-of-nature formulation on the same page: imagine the maxim governing action as consistently as a natural law. That formulation structures the four examples above.

Stop 4 / Second Section, humanity

Never merely as a means

Abbott / Second Section

So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only.

Groundwork, Second Section (Ak. 4:429)

By humanity, Kant means the rational capacity to set ends and act on principles. Such a being has ends of its own; its value cannot be reduced to how useful it is to someone else's project. Kant presents this as another formulation of the categorical imperative. Ak. 4:428 and 4:429.

A lender and borrower can serve each other's purposes through a loan both understand and accept. False promising changes the relation: the borrower conceals the plan not to repay, so the lender cannot share in the decision. Kant uses the same loan example to explain what treating someone merely as a means involves.

Consent helps reveal the problem, but a spoken yes does not settle every case. Someone deceived about the proposal has not agreed to the actual plan. The formula also applies to your own humanity, including things you might willingly do to yourself. And it requires more than leaving others alone: Kant says we should make their ends, as far as possible, our own. His duty of beneficence follows here as well. Ak. 4:429; 4:430.

Someone willing to receive no help might use the golden rule to justify refusing help to others. Kant points out that this preference cannot remove a duty. Nor must we assist every project someone chooses: their ends remain subject to moral requirements.

Stop 5 / Second Section, the kingdom of ends

A worth without an equivalent

Abbott / Second Section

In the kingdom of ends everything has either value or dignity. Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity.

Groundwork, Second Section (Ak. 4:434)

A kingdom of ends is Kant's ideal of rational beings joined under common laws. Each member gives laws and is subject to them; each treats the others as ends. Universal law is connected here with respect for those who must live under it. Ak. 4:433.

Within that ideal, Kant distinguishes price from dignity. Abbott uses value where the German has Preis, price. A thing with a price admits an equivalent that can replace it. Dignity is an unconditional worth that admits no equivalent. Kant places morality, and humanity insofar as it is capable of morality, in the second category. The basis is the capacity to participate in giving universal laws. Ak. 4:434 to 4:436.

Advantages gained elsewhere cannot compensate for treating a person merely as an instrument. But basing dignity on rational agency raises a question about human beings who lack that capacity. The Groundwork gives no adequate account of their standing. This remains a problem for Kantian ethics.

Stop 6 / Second and Third Sections, autonomy and freedom

Giving yourself a universal law

Abbott / Second Section

Autonomy of the will is that property of it by which it is a law to itself ...

Autonomy then is the basis of the dignity of human and of every rational nature.

Groundwork, Second Section (Ak. 4:440, excerpt; 4:436)

Autonomy means that the will gives itself the moral law. Kant specifies universal law: a principle valid for every rational being. You are subject to it through the same reason by which you recognize it. A private rule granting yourself a privilege would fail this condition.

He calls the alternative heteronomy. The will takes its governing principle from an object it wants, whether happiness, reward, or another benefit. Kant objects to making moral authority depend on this interest: do this to obtain that. Having desires, or satisfying one, does not by itself make a person unfree. Ak. 4:441.

Section III asks why this account should bind us at all. Kant starts with freedom: a rational will must regard its judgments as its own, rather than as decisions imposed by an external cause. It must act under the idea of freedom. He then argues that a free will must have a law of its own, and that this law is the moral law. The difficult step is showing that independence from external determination entails the particular law he has derived. Ak. 4:446 to 4:448.

Kant distinguishes two standpoints on the same agent. In the world we experience, our actions fall under natural laws. As rational agents deliberating, we must regard ourselves as free. This is meant to justify the standpoint from which we act, rather than provide scientific knowledge of an uncaused event. Whether it establishes our obligation remains disputed. Ak. 4:450 to 4:455.

Stop 7 / Objections and limits

What the argument still has to answer

Abbott / the last sentence

And thus while we do not comprehend the practical unconditional necessity of the moral imperative, we yet comprehend its incomprehensibility, and this is all that can be fairly demanded of a philosophy which strives to carry its principles up to the very limit of human reason.

Groundwork, Third Section (Ak. 4:463)

The maxim test needs an honest description of the principle guiding the act. Adding a date merely to make an exception pass does not establish a different reason for acting. The harder question is which features belong in the maxim. Defrauding a lender and giving an assurance under a violent threat are different cases. The formula alone does not tell us how to describe every relevant difference.

The famous murderer example belongs to a later essay, On a Supposed Right to Lie from Philanthropy (1797), responding to Benjamin Constant. It is not the Groundwork's lying-promise example. Constant asks about lying to someone pursuing a friend who has taken refuge in your house. Kant considers the case in which a yes-or-no answer cannot be avoided and denies a right to lie even then.

His defense concerns right and the reliability of declarations on which contracts depend. He also argues that we cannot control the consequences: the friend might leave unnoticed, so the lie could send the pursuer straight to him. Kant even claims that the liar could bear legal responsibility for that death, while a truthful speaker would not. These are arguments in the essay, not claims about the law today. Abbott's translation, appendix I; Ak. 8:426 and 8:427.

The strongest objection grants the case its hard assumptions: silence and resistance are unavailable, you have strong evidence that the truthful answer will expose an innocent person, and a lie is the only credible protection. Why should fidelity to truthfulness outweigh that person's life? Uncertainty can explain why a lie might fail; it does not show why one should disregard a large, foreseeable difference in danger. And a rule permitting deception to obstruct murder need not undermine the assurances on which ordinary contracts rely.

The essay's legal setting matters to its scope. Jens Timmermann's account treats its conclusion as uncompromising. That refusal does not mean every questioner is entitled to an answer when silence is possible.

The last sentence of the Groundwork names a different limit. Kant says reason cannot explain how an unconditional moral law is possible. He regards that limit as consistent with justifying the law, rather than an admission that morality has failed. In the Critique of Practical Reason (1788), he proceeds from consciousness of moral obligation, which he calls a fact of reason, to our practical warrant for freedom. He does not simply declare freedom itself a fact. Scholars dispute whether this changes the earlier argument or develops it. The dispute concerns whether rational deliberation gives us enough to justify the moral law's authority, even without an explanation of how freedom works.
