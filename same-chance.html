The Same Chance

You throw a piece of trash at a garbage can and miss. Nobody's looking. Do you go back and pick it up?

I believe everyone gets exactly the same chance at contentment and happiness, including folks whose lives end too early. By contentment I mean feeling connected to other people and less restless inside.

Life shows each of us the truth about the harm we're doing. Then it's our choice: do the uncomfortable thing we know is right, or keep doing the comfortable thing we know is wrong. I think pain is usually what finally turns us around.

The trash is a small version of that choice. You know it's yours, and you know what you should do. Walking away is easier.

Pleasure is getting what I want. It feels good for a while, and it can leave me cut off from people. What's actually made me content is unselfish action, doing something that helps somebody besides me.

I want to be comfortable, and I chase it like it's the only thing that matters. I did that until I hurt so badly that my choices were death or doing the uncomfortable things I knew were right.

Even then, I didn't believe being less selfish would make me feel better. So I tested it, for a long time. I'd act less selfishly and feel more connected. Then I'd get some of my comforts back, get selfish again, and watch the restlessness and disconnection come back. That happened over and over. I kept growing by learning more and, more importantly, by doing more constructive, unselfish things.

I call this karma: the harm I do to other people also harms me. The opposite is just as real. Unselfish action brings connection and contentment back, and I call that reverse karma. I believe both happen because we're all part of one spiritual world. Nobody has to catch me for it to count.

It isn't permanent. What I have is a daily reprieve, relief that lasts as long as I keep living this way. I can go back to the old way any day. I can pick up the trash today and walk right past it tomorrow.

I believe there's a spiritual compass, a sense of right and wrong, in all life, including us. We know right from wrong, and I don't think there's any denying it. Someone can sincerely believe something harmful is right. That's natural. It's part of their journey, and anyone could be on that part of it, me included.

I think arguing about right and wrong just separates us. When somebody tells me I hurt them, I want to argue. I'm better off shutting up and listening, because part of it is usually true.

Genes and upbringing are part of the journey. Nobody picks their family or the brain they're born with. Some of the best people in the world had the worst upbringings, and some of the worst people had some of the worst upbringings too. If you were raised wrong or born with bad genes, I think you're kind of innocent for a while. At some point, though, life shows you the truth: you're hurting other people, and it's hurting you.

However different our lives look, I believe we're all exactly the same underneath, the way every wave in the ocean is the same. And I believe the chance is exactly equal. I can't explain how it comes out even for a kid with every advantage and a kid with none. I think it's infinitely complex in the way it's spread out, and nobody will ever prove it with math. How it works out is beyond us.

The strongest evidence against me is a housing lottery. In the 1990s, a federal experiment called Moving to Opportunity let 4,604 low-income families in public housing sign up for a chance to move to a less poor neighborhood. Ten to fifteen years later, Jens Ludwig and colleagues found that the adults whose families won were happier on average, even though winning hadn't raised their incomes. Happiness is the exact thing I'm saying everyone has the same chance at, and a lottery changed it.

Emmy Werner's study is closer to what I believe. She followed all 698 children born on the Hawaiian island of Kauai in 1955, from birth to age 40. Some had four or more serious risks by age two, such as poverty, a hard birth or a family in constant conflict. Two-thirds of those kids had problems with school, the law or their mental health by 18. The other third grew into what Werner called "competent, confident and caring" adults.

Most of the teenagers who'd had serious problems had recovered by midlife. Their turning points included going back to school as adults, the military, a stable marriage, a religion that asked something of them, and surviving a life-threatening illness or accident. Those were mostly help or a second chance, and you have to live long enough to get one. The study can't tell me how much of it was choice, or what was possible for the ones who died.

Pain shows up in the drinking research too. Helen Matzger and colleagues followed 659 problem drinkers in Northern California who had started cutting back. Three reasons for cutting back predicted who would stay out of problem drinking over the next five years: hitting rock bottom, going through something traumatic and having a spiritual awakening. Those were reasons people gave after they'd already cut back, so it doesn't prove the pain did it.

Pain doesn't simply make people more ready to change, though. Carlo DiClemente and colleagues studied about 1,400 people starting treatment for alcohol dependence. The ones who said drinking had cost them more were a little more ready to change. The ones under more stress were a little less ready. It's one snapshot, so it can't show what caused what.

I still think pain is usually what turns people around. It's what turned me. I can't show it's true for most people, and these are only drinking studies. I'm not saying anybody has to hurt like I did.

Viktor Frankl survived Auschwitz and wrote a famous book about finding meaning in suffering. Even he said that if suffering can be avoided, you remove its cause: "To suffer unnecessarily is masochistic rather than heroic." I'd never tell someone to wait for their life to get worse before they accept help. If I can help someone's pain stop, that can be the right thing for me to do, whatever they might have learned from it.

I don't get to look at somebody who's hurting and decide it's their karma. I can't see their life from the inside. Good people suffer. When they do, I can't explain it, and I'm not going to blame them for it.

If somebody's hurting people, that may be part of their journey, and you still stop them. Punishing them isn't my job. Karma takes care of that.

I suspect the connection between us goes all the way down to the quantum level, the physics of atoms and particles. That's a hunch. Experiments with entangled particles, pairs that share one state however far apart they are, won the 2022 Nobel Prize in physics. They don't test right and wrong or happiness, and I have nothing that links the two.

I can't test the equality either. It's a spiritual commitment, and I'd keep it without proof. I might explain it differently someday. I'll never decide some people didn't get the chance.

The hardest case is a baby who dies before they're old enough to know right from wrong, or a person who never develops the ability. I believe they got exactly the same chance too. I can't tell you what their chance looked like or how it could be equal to mine. I still won't make an exception. If I made one, I'd be saying I'm different from them, and we're all exactly the same.
