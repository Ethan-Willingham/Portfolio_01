The Same Chance

You throw a piece of trash at a garbage can and miss. Nobody's looking. Do you go back and pick it up?

Everyone gets exactly the same chance at contentment and happiness, including folks whose lives end too early. Contentment here means feeling connected to other people and less restless inside.

At some point, life shows each of us the truth about the harm we're doing. Then it's our choice: do the uncomfortable thing we know is right, or keep doing the comfortable thing we know is wrong. Pain is usually what finally turns people around.

The trash is a small version of that choice. You know it's yours, and you know what you should do. Walking away is easier.

Pleasure is doing what you want. Contentment comes from doing what you know is right, and that usually means unselfish action: doing something that helps somebody besides yourself.

Most of us want to be comfortable, and some of us chase it like it's the only thing that matters. Almost no one stops until they've felt a lot of pain, sometimes so much that the choices come down to death or doing the uncomfortable things they know are right.

Karma means the harm you do to other people also harms you. The opposite is just as real. Unselfish action brings connection and contentment back, and that's reverse karma. Both happen because we're all part of one spiritual world.

Contentment lasts only as long as you keep living this way. Get comfortable and selfish again, and the restlessness and disconnection come back.

There's a spiritual compass, a sense of right and wrong, in all life, including us. We know right from wrong, and there's no denying it. Arguing about it just separates people. Even so, someone can sincerely believe something harmful is right. That's natural, part of their journey, and anyone could be on that part of it.

Genes and upbringing are part of the journey. Nobody picks their family or the brain they're born with. If you were raised wrong or born with bad genes, you're kind of innocent for a while. Eventually, though, you're faced with the truth that you're harming others, and that it's harming you.

Some of the best people in the world had the worst upbringings, and some of the worst people had some of the worst upbringings too. Oftentimes people with really bad genes and really bad upbringings, if they feel enough pain, turn their lives around and become some of the best people out there.

However different our lives look, we're all the same underneath, the way every wave in the ocean is the same. That's why the chance is exactly equal, even between a kid with every advantage and a kid with none. It's infinitely complex in the way it's spread out, and how it works out is beyond us.

The strongest evidence against this is a housing lottery. In the 1990s, a federal experiment called Moving to Opportunity let 4,604 low-income families in public housing sign up for a chance to move to a less poor neighborhood. Ten to fifteen years later, Jens Ludwig and colleagues found that the adults whose families won were happier on average, even though winning hadn't raised their incomes. Happiness is the very thing the chance is about, and a lottery changed it. Experiences vary. The chances still come, to the families who lost too.

Emmy Werner's study is closer to the belief. She followed all 698 children born on the Hawaiian island of Kauai in 1955, from birth to age 40. Some had four or more serious risks by age two, such as poverty, a hard birth or a family in constant conflict. Two-thirds of those kids had problems with school, the law or their mental health by 18. The other third grew into what Werner called "competent, confident and caring" adults.

Most of the teenagers who'd had serious problems had recovered by midlife. Their turning points included going back to school as adults, the military, a stable marriage, a religion that asked something of them, and surviving a life-threatening illness or accident. The study can't say how much of it was choice, or what was possible for the ones who died.

Pain shows up in the drinking research too. Helen Matzger and colleagues followed 659 problem drinkers in Northern California who had started cutting back. Three reasons for cutting back predicted who stayed out of problem drinking through a five-year follow-up: hitting rock bottom, going through something traumatic and having a spiritual awakening. Those were reasons people gave after they'd already cut back, so it doesn't prove the pain did it.

Pain doesn't simply make people more ready to change, though. Carlo DiClemente and colleagues studied about 1,400 people starting treatment for alcohol dependence. The ones who said drinking had cost them more were a little more ready to change. The ones under more stress were a little less ready. It's one snapshot, so it can't show what caused what.

Viktor Frankl survived the Nazi camps and wrote a famous book about finding meaning in suffering. Even he said that if suffering can be avoided, you remove its cause: "To suffer unnecessarily is masochistic rather than heroic."

Nobody gets to look at somebody who's hurting and call it their karma. You can't see their life from the inside, and good people suffer too.

If somebody's hurting people, that may be part of their journey, and you still stop them. Punishing them isn't anybody's job. Karma takes care of that.

The connection between us goes all the way down to the quantum level, the physics of atoms and particles. Experiments with entangled particles, pairs that share one state however far apart they are, won the 2022 Nobel Prize in physics.

None of this tests the belief itself. Entanglement experiments don't measure right and wrong or happiness, and the pain studies above are only about drinking. The equality will never be proven with math. It's a spiritual commitment, kept without proof.

The hardest case is a baby who dies before they're old enough to know right from wrong, or a person who never develops the ability. They got exactly the same chance too. There's no telling what their chance looked like, or how it could be equal to anyone else's. There's still no exception. An exception would mean some people are different from the rest of us, and we're all exactly the same.
