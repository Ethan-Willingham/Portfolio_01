The Bhagavad Gita: A Guided Reading

Arjuna sees his relatives across the battlefield and refuses to fight. Krishna asks him to act without clinging to the result. The difficulty is what that asks of him here.

01 The whole conversation

The Bhagavad Gita, in plain English

The Gita teaches freedom from attachment while persuading a man to fight his own relatives.

Two branches of one royal family have brought their armies to Kurukshetra. Arjuna asks his charioteer Krishna to stop between them. He sees relatives, teachers, and friends on both sides. His mouth dries, his limbs fail, and his bow slips from his hand. He wants no kingdom bought by killing these people.

Arjuna argues that the war will destroy families and their inherited rites. He fears women becoming “corrupt,” the mixing of social classes, and ancestors losing the offerings their descendants owe them (1.40-42). His objections include both affection for his kin and defense of a hierarchical social order. He would rather be killed unarmed. In chapter 2 he admits that he cannot tell what duty requires and asks Krishna to teach him.

The conversation takes place inside the Mahabharata, the Sanskrit epic about this family war. The blind king Dhritarashtra asks what is happening on the field. His counselor Sanjaya, granted supernatural sight, reports Krishna and Arjuna’s exchange. These eighteen chapters are the Bhagavad Gita, the “Song of the Lord.” Krishna is both Arjuna’s friend and a divine teacher, traditionally identified with Vishnu.

Krishna begins by distinguishing the body from atman, the enduring Self. The body dies; the Self takes another body as a person puts on new clothes. He then argues that Arjuna’s duty as a warrior requires him to fight this battle. Arjuna must act without making victory or personal gain his motive, and remain steady in success and failure. Dharma means duty or right order here; yoga is the discipline by which a person becomes free.

The goal is moksha, liberation from samsara, the cycle of birth and death. Actions can bind a person to further births. Even heaven is temporary: when the merit that earned it runs out, the person returns (9.20-21). Krishna teaches ways of acting, knowing, and loving that release this bondage. The advice about work and calm belongs to that religious aim.

Chapters 3 through 6 explain how action can lead toward freedom. Karma means action, and karma yoga means doing one’s duty without attachment to its rewards. Nobody can avoid all action, and people with influence must consider the example they set. Krishna also teaches jnana yoga, the path of knowledge: understanding the Self and its relation to Brahman, ultimate reality. Readers disagree about how these paths fit together and which finally liberates.

In chapter 4 Krishna says he takes form whenever dharma declines, to protect the good and destroy evildoers. Chapter 5 praises both renunciation and karma yoga, then calls karma yoga the better of the two (5.2). Chapter 6 gives instructions for meditation: sit steadily, regulate food and sleep, and bring the wandering mind back. Arjuna says controlling it seems as hard as controlling the wind. Krishna recommends practice and dispassion, the loosening of attachment. When Arjuna asks what happens to someone who fails at yoga, Krishna says the effort is not lost and can continue in another birth.

Chapters 7 through 10 explain who Krishna is. He claims to be the source and support of the universe. Bhakti, devotion to him, becomes central. He accepts a leaf, flower, fruit, or water offered with devotion, and tells Arjuna to offer his ordinary acts too. Chapter 8 connects a lifetime of remembrance with remembering Krishna at death and reaching him beyond rebirth. Release depends on God’s help as well as the practitioner’s effort.

In chapter 11 Arjuna asks to see Krishna’s cosmic form. Given divine sight, he sees countless faces and mouths swallowing the warriors. Krishna identifies himself as Time, the destroyer, and tells Arjuna to be his instrument. Arjuna is terrified. He asks to see the familiar form again, and Krishna grants it. Chapter 12 describes the devotee Krishna loves: friendly, compassionate, forgiving, and steady in pain and pleasure.

Chapters 13 through 17 examine the body, the Self, and nature. The body is a field; the Self is its knower, and Krishna says he knows every field (13.2-3 in Besant’s numbering). Three gunas, qualities of nature, bind the embodied Self: sattva through clarity and happiness, rajas through restless desire, and tamas through ignorance and inertia. Chapter 15 calls Krishna Purushottama, the Supreme Person, beyond both the perishable and the imperishable. Chapters 16 and 17 examine character, faith, food, sacrifice, and giving. These chapters ask what keeps a person bound and what helps them become free.

Chapter 18 returns to action, knowledge, and devotion, and applies the gunas to work, judgment, resolve, and pleasure. It also assigns duties to the four social classes. Krishna tells Arjuna to reflect on the teaching and act as he chooses (18.63). Three verses later he tells him to abandon all duties and take refuge in him alone, promising release from sin (18.66). How to reconcile those commands is one of the book’s lasting disputes.

Arjuna answers that his confusion is gone and he will do as Krishna says (18.73). Sanjaya closes by recalling the conversation and predicting victory where Krishna and Arjuna are together. The Gita ends before the fighting. In the larger epic, the war they are about to join destroys much of their family.

02 History and transmission

What can actually be known

What the evidence can establish

The Gita belongs to the Bhishma Parva, the sixth book of the Mahabharata. Scholars usually place its composition in the late centuries BCE or early centuries CE, within an epic that grew over centuries. Its exact date and stages of composition remain disputed.

The standard text has eighteen chapters and 700 verses. The twentieth-century critical edition, assembled by comparing manuscripts from different regions, retains that count. Regional versions differ, including additional verses in Kashmiri transmission. Shankara’s influential commentary, usually dated to the eighth century, is an early witness to the familiar text; its detailed exposition begins at 2.11.

The Gita became one of Vedanta’s three foundational texts, alongside the Upanishads and the Brahma Sutra. Vedanta is a family of Hindu philosophical schools, and their disagreement over these shared sources helps explain why the Gita has so many commentaries.

What remains a reconstruction

Hindu tradition attributes the epic to Vyasa, who also appears in its story. Historians cannot identify a single author of the Gita or recover its first manuscript. Surviving copies are much later than its likely composition. Language, quotations, commentaries, and manuscript comparisons provide evidence for reconstructing earlier stages.

For Shankara, liberating knowledge reveals that the Self is Brahman. For Ramanuja, souls remain real and distinct, forming part of God’s body along with the world. Madhva insists that God and souls are eternally different. English translations often follow one of these readings. Besant, whose version is quoted here, distinguishes a personal “self” from the universal “Self” and translates Brahman as “the Eternal.” Those are interpretive choices, explained in her preface.

How safely did this reach us?

The familiar Sanskrit text is well established. Its origins and interpretation are less settled.

Manuscripts, wording, and translation need separate judgments.

Early witnesses Limited

No manuscript survives from the likely period of composition. Shankara’s commentary provides early evidence for the text, centuries after its composition. Its date is not the date of the physical copies through which we know it.

Sanskrit wording Stable core

The critical edition retains the familiar eighteen chapters and 700 verses. Regional versions add verses or differ in wording and numbering.

English meaning Key terms disputed

The plot and many instructions are clear. Words such as dharma, yoga, and atman carry meanings that one English word cannot capture. Rival schools also disagree in Sanskrit, before translation begins.

Based on the Oxford overview, research on recensions and verse counts, and the IIT Kanpur text and commentary archive. These judgments concern the surviving text. They cannot establish its religious claims.

03 Expand the ideas

Thirteen ideas in the Gita and Hindu practice

Each entry explains the idea, gives examples of practice, and quotes Annie Besant’s 1922 public-domain translation. The examples come from different traditions; no single school represents them all.

01Arjuna asks for teaching when he cannot decide what to do.Arjuna’s collapse and request

Arjuna has reasons for refusing to fight, but he cannot reconcile them with his duty as a warrior. At 2.7 he asks Krishna to teach him and calls himself a disciple. His questions continue throughout the book: why act if knowledge is better, how can he control his mind, and what happens if he fails?

In practice The first chapter is traditionally called the yoga of Arjuna’s despondency. A study group or teacher can use his predicament to examine a student’s own conflict. The starting point is admitting where the answers they already have fail.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Seeing these my kinsmen, O Krishna, arrayed, eager to fight…1.28

If the sons of Dhritarâshtra, weapon in hand, should slay me, unresisting, unarmed, in the battle, that would for me be the better.1.46

My heart is weighed down with the vice of faintness; my mind is confused as to duty. … I am thy disciple, suppliant to Thee; teach me.2.7

02The Self survives death; liberation ends rebirth.Atman, samsara, and moksha

Krishna says the Self is unborn and cannot be killed. It passes from one body to another. The goal is release from that cycle, not an endless succession of better lives. Knowledge of the Self is central to this release. Shankara understands it as recognition that the Self is Brahman; devotional schools understand liberation through the soul’s relation to God.

In practice Study and meditation examine the difference between the enduring Self and a changing body or mind. Devotees cultivate remembrance of Krishna, including at death (8.5-7). Traditions disagree about what liberation is, but the prospect of release from rebirth gives these practices their purpose.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

He is not born, nor doth he die; nor having been, ceaseth he any more to be; unborn, perpetual, eternal and ancient, he is not slain when the body is slaughtered.2.20

As a man, casting off worn-out garments, taketh new ones, so the dweller in the body, casting off worn-out bodies, entereth into others that are new.2.22

Having come to Me, these Mahâtmâs come not again to birth, the place of pain, non-eternal; they have gone to the highest bliss.8.15

03Do your duty without making its reward your motive.Karma yoga and the fruit of action

The “fruit” of an action is its result or reward. At 2.47 Krishna tells Arjuna to give up that reward as his motive, while also warning him against attachment to inaction. Success and failure should not decide whether he performs his duty. Within the book’s account of karma, this discipline helps free action from the attachment that binds the doer.

In practice Work, study, and family responsibilities can become karma yoga when performed as duty or offered to God. A person still plans and checks the result. Verse 18.25 condemns action undertaken without regard for its consequences or the injury it causes.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Thy business is with the action only, never with its fruits; so let not the fruit of action be thy motive, nor be thou to inaction attached.2.47

Perform action, O Dhananjaya, dwelling in union with the divine, renouncing attachments and balanced evenly in success and failure: equilibrium is called yoga.2.48

Therefore, without attachment, constantly perform action which is duty, for, by performing action without attachment, man verily reacheth the Supreme.3.19

04Your own duty takes priority over someone else’s.Svadharma, nature, and role

Svadharma means one’s own duty. Krishna says it is better to perform that duty imperfectly than someone else’s well. For Arjuna, it includes fighting as a warrior. Chapter 18 links duties to nature and to varna, the fourfold social order. A reading about personal vocation needs to account for that social hierarchy too.

In practice Teachers apply this to household obligations and work suited to a person’s abilities. Traditional readings also assign obligations by social class. The difficult question is who decides which duties belong to you, particularly when an inherited role is unjust.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Better one's own duty, though destitute of merit, than the duty of another, well discharged. Better death in the discharge of one's own duty; the duty of another is full of danger.3.35

Better is one's own duty though destitute of merits than the well-executed duty of another. He who doeth the duty laid down by his own nature incurreth not sin.18.47

Congenital duty, O son of Kunti, though defective, ought not to be abandoned. All undertakings indeed are clouded by defects as fire by smoke.18.48

05Work should help hold the world together.Sacrifice, service, and public example

Krishna connects sacrifice with the cycle of food, rain, and the support of living beings (3.10-16). He also uses sacrifice for offerings of knowledge and disciplined practice. Lokasangraha, maintaining the world, is a reason for the wise to keep acting: other people follow their example. Knowledge does not remove this responsibility.

In practice The Ramakrishna Math and Mission treats teaching, feeding people, and nursing the sick as karma yoga. Its stated aim joins personal liberation with the welfare of the world. This is a modern institutional application of the Gita’s teaching about work and sacrifice.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

The world is bound by action, unless performed for the sake of sacrifice; for that sake, free from attachment, O son of Kuntî, perform thou action.3.9

Whatsoever a great man doeth, that other men also do; the standard he setteth up, by that the people go.3.21

As the ignorant act from attachment to action, O Bhârata, so should the wise act without attachment, desiring the welfare of the world.3.25

06Train the mind to remain steady in pleasure and pain.Equanimity, desire, and anger

Krishna describes a person free from craving, fear, and anger, whose judgment remains steady through pain and pleasure. He also explains how judgment fails: dwelling on an object produces attachment, attachment produces desire, and desire leads to anger. Anger brings confusion and the loss of discernment (2.62-63). Training attention and the senses helps interrupt that sequence.

In practice Meditation and restraint train a person to notice a craving before acting on it. The Gita connects this with a demanding ideal of freedom from desire. Verse 12.13 also requires compassion; steadiness alone is not the whole description of a good devotee.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

He whose mind is free from anxiety amid pains, indifferent amid pleasures, loosed from passion, fear and anger, is called a sage of stable mind.2.56

He attaineth Peace, into whom all desires flow as rivers flow into the ocean, which is filled with water, but remaineth unmoved…2.70

Man, musing on the objects of sense, conceiveth an attachment to these; from attachment ariseth desire; from desire anger cometh forth;2.62

07The wandering mind is trained by returning it.Meditation, practice, and dispassion

Chapter 6 expects the mind to wander and instructs the practitioner to bring it back. It recommends moderation in eating, activity, and sleep. When Arjuna doubts that he can control his mind, Krishna agrees that it is difficult, then gives two means: repeated practice and dispassion.

In practice Practitioners sit regularly and return attention when it strays. Different schools use different objects of meditation. In the Gita’s own instructions, Krishna tells the yogi to direct the mind toward him and make him the highest goal (6.14).

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Let him raise the self by the Self and not let the self become depressed; for verily is the Self the friend of the self, and also the Self the self's enemy;6.5

As often as the wavering and unsteady mind goeth forth, so often reining it in, let him bring it under the control of the Self.6.26

Without doubt, O mighty-armed, the mind is hard to curb and restless; but it may be curbed by constant practice and by dispassion.6.35

08The wise look equally on people and animals of different rank.Equal vision and compassion

Verse 5.18 puts a learned Brahmin, a cow, an elephant, a dog, and an outcaste in one list: the wise look equally on them. Verse 6.29 describes seeing the Self in all beings. Schools disagree about whether this establishes one universal Self or the divine presence within distinct souls. Equal vision coexists with the book’s unequal social duties.

In practice These passages inform teaching about care for animals and service across social divisions. Verse 6.32 asks the yogi to understand another being’s pleasure and pain by comparison with their own. The social implications remain contested within Hindu traditions.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Sages look equally on a Brâhmana adorned with learning and humility, a cow, an elephant, and even a dog and an outcaste.5.18

The self, harmonised by yoga, seeth the Self abiding in all beings, all beings in the Self; everywhere he seeth the same.6.29

He who, through the likeness of the Self, O Arjuna, seeth equality in everything, whether pleasant or painful, he is considered a perfect Yogî.6.32

09Krishna takes birth to restore dharma.Avatara, from age to age

Krishna says he takes birth whenever dharma declines, to protect the good, destroy evildoers, and restore right order. This is a foundational statement of the doctrine of avatara, divine descent. His birth differs from an ordinary person’s rebirth: at 4.6 he describes manifesting through his own power, while remaining unborn.

In practice Worship of Krishna treats his birth and acts as divine intervention in the world. Some movements extend this understanding to later teachers; the Ramakrishna movement, for example, reveres Ramakrishna as an avatar. Verse 4.11 also supports broad readings of how people approach God, though later verses distinguish the rewards of different forms of worship.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Whenever there is decay of righteousness, O Bhârata, and there is exaltation of unrighteousness, then I Myself come forth;4.7

For the protection of the good, for the destruction of evil-doers, for the sake of firmly establishing righteousness, I am born from age to age.4.8

However men approach Me, even so do I welcome them, for the path men take from every side is Mine, O Pârtha.4.11

10Devotion can begin with a leaf, a flower, fruit, or water.Bhakti, offering, and character

Krishna accepts a simple gift offered with devotion. He then asks for eating, giving, work, and austerity to be offered too (9.27). Bhakti makes these acts part of a relationship with him. He promises to care for devoted worshippers (9.22), while chapter 12 describes the character of a devotee: compassionate, forgiving, and free from hatred.

In practice Devotees offer flowers, fruit, and water in home and temple worship. Offered food may be received as prasad, a blessed gift. Singing and repetition of Krishna’s names sustain remembrance. Trust in his help belongs beside these daily practices.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

To those men who worship Me alone thinking of no other, to those ever harmonious, I bring full security.9.22

He who offereth to Me with devotion a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water, that I accept from the striving self, offered as it is with devotion.9.26

He who beareth no ill-will to any being, friendly and compassionate, without attachment and egoism, balanced in pleasure and pain, and forgiving,12.13

11The divine is present in the world and exceeds it.Krishna’s manifestations

In chapter 10 Krishna lists his vibhutis, manifestations of divine power: among lights he is the sun, among senses the mind, among bodies of water the ocean. Every splendor expresses only a fragment of him. Chapter 11 gives Arjuna a vision of the whole. Chapter 15 goes further by naming Krishna the Supreme Person, beyond the perishable and the imperishable.

In practice Reading or reciting chapter 10 provides objects for contemplation. Devotees can remember Krishna through familiar things such as sunlight or the ocean, as well as through images and worship. The account of the cosmic form also makes divine power frightening: it includes the destruction of the armies.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

There is naught whatsoever higher than I, O Dhananjaya. All this is threaded on Me, as rows of pearls on a string.7.7

I, O Gudakesha, am the Self, seated in the heart of all beings; I am the beginning, the middle, and also the end of all beings.10.20

Whatsoever is glorious, good, beautiful, and mighty, understand thou that to go forth from a fragment of My splendour.10.41

If the splendour of a thousand suns were to blaze out together in the sky, that might resemble the glory of that Mahâtman.11.12

12Three qualities keep shaping thought, appetite, and action.Sattva, rajas, and tamas

The gunas belong to prakriti, nature, which includes body and mind. Sattva brings clarity, rajas restless desire, and tamas ignorance or inertia. Their relative strength changes. All three bind: even sattva binds through attachment to happiness and knowledge (14.6). Liberation requires going beyond all three.

In practice The Gita applies this vocabulary to food, worship, gifts, and work. Chapter 17 distinguishes a gift given as duty from one given for repayment or with contempt. These examples let a practitioner examine the motive behind an ordinary act.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Harmony, Motion, Inertia, such are the qualities, Matter-born; they bind fast in the body, O great-armed one, the indestructible dweller in the body.14.5

From Harmony wisdom is born, and also greed from Motion; heedlessness and delusion are of Inertia and also unwisdom.14.17

The foods that augment vitality, energy, vigour, health, joy and cheerfulness, delicious, bland, substantial and agreeable, are dear to the pure.17.8

13Krishna asks for surrender and promises release.Reflection, refuge, and grace

Krishna tells Arjuna to reflect on the teaching and choose his action. He then asks him to take refuge in him alone and promises release from all sins. Arjuna credits Krishna’s grace for the end of his confusion. What “abandoning all duties” requires is disputed: commentators differ over renunciation, continued duty, and reliance on God.

In practice Devotional traditions understand sharanagati or prapatti as taking refuge in God and relying on his grace. In Sri Vaishnava traditions, surrender is a major teaching in its own right. Ramanuja reads 18.66 as compatible with continuing prescribed duties, giving up the sense that one independently acts or owns their fruit.

Read the versesBesant, 1922

Thus hath wisdom, more secret than secrecy itself, been declared unto thee by Me; having reflected on it fully, then act thou as thou listest.18.63

Abandoning all duties come unto Me alone for shelter; sorrow not, I will liberate thee from all sins.18.66

Destroyed is my delusion. I have gained knowledge through Thy grace, O Immutable one. I am firm, my doubts have fled away. I will do according to Thy word.18.73

Where rival readings meet

Shankara, Ramanuja, and Madhva all discuss action, knowledge, devotion, and liberation. They disagree about how these fit together and about whether the liberated Self is identical with Brahman or remains distinct. The entries above follow their shared text. They should not be read as a consensus on its final meaning.

04 Do not skip

War, caste, women, detachment, renunciation, other gods

These passages are part of the teaching. A reading that leaves them out makes the Gita easier than it is.

Krishna tells Arjuna to fight

In 11.32-34, Krishna says the opposing warriors are already doomed and tells Arjuna to kill them as his instrument. Within the epic, this is a war that kills relatives and teachers. Gandhi read the battlefield as a struggle within the human heart and used the Gita in a life committed to nonviolence. That influential interpretation does not explain away the literal command to fight.

Duty is tied to a ranked social order

Verse 4.13 says Krishna created the fourfold order according to qualities and action. 18.41-44 assigns duties to Brahmins (priests and teachers), Kshatriyas (rulers and warriors), Vaishyas (farmers and traders), and Shudras (servants). This ideal of varna differs from jati, the hereditary caste groups through which much social life is organized. Some interpreters emphasize qualities and work over birth. The verses still assign unequal roles, whose justice remains disputed.

Women are promised liberation in the language of low status

9.32 promises the highest goal to women, Vaishyas, and Shudras who take refuge in Krishna. Besant’s translation places them after “though of the womb of sin.” Commentators disagree about how the Sanskrit links the groups and that phrase. The next verse names Brahmins and royal sages as the more obvious candidates. The passage widens access to liberation while speaking within a hierarchy of birth and gender.

Detachment can be used to excuse harm

Krishna says nature’s qualities perform actions while the deluded ego claims to be the doer (3.27). In the battle scene he also calls Arjuna an instrument. Read alone, those claims can excuse a person from responsibility. But 18.25 condemns action that disregards consequences, loss, or injury to others. Giving up attachment to a reward does not remove the obligation to examine what an action will do.

Readers disagree about what must be renounced

At 5.2 Krishna praises both renunciation and karma yoga, preferring karma yoga. At 18.66 he tells Arjuna to abandon all duties and take refuge in him. Shankara gives knowledge and renunciation a decisive role in liberation. Ramanuja interprets surrender as compatible with continuing prescribed work while giving up possessiveness and independent agency. Whether action prepares for liberation or remains central to the liberating path depends on the school.

Other gods are recognized and subordinated

Krishna says he supports faith in other deities, but calls their rewards temporary (7.20-23). At 9.23-25 he says their worship reaches him, though performed without proper understanding, and distinguishes its destination from reaching him directly. Some Hindu readers use these passages to explain many forms of worship within one divine reality. Krishna-centered traditions also read them as a claim that devotion to Krishna is supreme.
